# Route Planning

## Objective
Route planning is the process of planning safe travel, turning movement from gambling into calculated risk. The core objective is to avoid getting lost, as getting lost is rarely the problem; walking into an ambush, a flooded river, or a dead-end canyon because of poor planning is the real killer.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
Before departing, the following must be verified and completed:

*   Destination bearing and distance confirmed.
*   Route broken into legs with identifiable waypoints.
*   Catching features identified for each leg.
*   Water sources marked (at least one per 8 hours of travel).
*   Estimated travel time calculated (including terrain penalties).
*   Escape routes identified.
*   Handrail features noted.
*   Calorie requirements estimated against food supply.
*   Departure time set to arrive at difficult sections in daylight.
*   Group members briefed on route, waypoints, and rally points.

## Material
The environment in which planning occurs is a post-collapse world where every journey burns calories, exposes travelers to hazards, and consumes time. The route must account for terrain (high ground, water courses, vegetation density), resources (water, shelter, food), and potential threats (hostile groups, unstable terrain).

## System
The system relies on analytical estimation, risk assessment, and breaking the journey into manageable segments to ensure safety and efficiency.

## Process
### Gathering Intelligence Before You Move
1.  **Terrain Assessment:**
    *   High ground observation: Climb to scan the route for rivers, cliffs, forests, open fields, roads, and settlements.
    *   Water course mapping: Note river crossings, direction of flow, and fordable sections.
    *   Vegetation density: Adjust time estimates based on whether the terrain is thick forest (1-2 km/h) or open ground (4-5 km/h).
2.  **Time and Distance Estimation:**
    *   Use realistic segments based on terrain type (see table below).
    *   Apply Naismith’s Rule for mountainous terrain to estimate travel time.
3.  **Route Selection:**
    *   Rank routes based on five criteria:
        1.  Safety (Avoid known threats).
        2.  Water access (Pass near water sources every 8-12 hours).
        3.  Cover and concealment.
        4.  Energy efficiency (Minimize climbing/rough terrain).
        5.  Navigation reliability (Follow trackable features).
4.  **Leg Planning:**
    *   Identify waypoints (recognizable features like a river crossing or hilltop).
    *   Assign bearing and distance to each leg (e.g., "From the river crossing, walk bearing 045 for approximately 2 km to the hilltop.").
    *   Identify a catching feature for each leg to determine if the route has been overshot.
    *   Identify an attack point for the final approach.
5.  **Aiming Off:**
    *   When heading toward a point on a linear feature, deliberately aim 5-10 degrees to the left of the target point to convert a 50/50 guess into certainty.

### Time and Distance Estimation Table
| Terrain Type | Speed (km/h) | Daily Range (8 hrs) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Road or trail, flat | 4-5 | 32-40 km |
| Open ground, gentle hills | 3-4 | 24-32 km |
| Forest with undergrowth | 1.5-2.5 | 12-20 km |
| Steep hills or mountains | 1-2 | 8-16 km |
| Swamp or dense brush | 0.5-1.5 | 4-12 km |
| Deep snow (no snowshoes) | 0.5-1 | 4-8 km |

### Naismith’s Rule for Mountainous Terrain
Estimate travel time using this formula:
1.  Allow 1 hour for every 5 km of horizontal distance.
2.  Add 1 hour for every 600 meters of ascent.
3.  For steep descent, add 10 minutes per 300 meters of descent.

### Group Travel Considerations
*   The slowest member sets the pace.
*   Spacing is critical in hostile territory to prevent a single ambush from catching everyone.
*   Designate a point person, rear guard, and rally points.
*   Agree on communication signals (e.g., one whistle = stop; three whistles = emergency).
*   If splitting, every subgroup needs the full route, waypoints, and destination.

### When Plans Fail
*   **Decision points:** Reassess at every waypoint to check schedule, terrain, and weather.
*   **Go/no-go criteria:** Set conditions before departure to trigger a turn-back (e.g., "If we haven’t reached the river by noon, we turn back.").
*   **Lost procedure:** Stop immediately. Use triangulation to fix position, backtrack to the last known waypoint, or climb to high ground for observation.

## Notes
*   **The 2/3 Rule:** Plan to cover only two-thirds of the distance you think you can manage; the remaining third serves as a buffer for navigation errors, unexpected obstacles, injury, or weather.
*   **Calorie Cost:** Rough terrain burns dramatically more calories (e.g., mountain travel can require 4,000-6,000 calories per day versus 2,500-3,000 on flat ground).
*   **Aiming Off Efficacy:** Aiming off converts a 50/50 guess into a certainty regarding the location of a point feature on a linear feature.
*   **Flexibility:** Plans are starting points, not commitments; flexibility is key.

## Key Takeaways
*   Ten minutes of planning saves hours of suffering.
*   Break routes into legs with bearings, distance, and waypoints.
*   Aim off deliberately to eliminate guesswork.
*   Water dictates routes more than distance.
*   Use the 2/3 rule as a safety buffer.
*   Naismith’s Rule prevents dangerous underestimation of mountain travel times.
*   Plans must be reassessed at every waypoint.
