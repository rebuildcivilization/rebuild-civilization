# Terrain Reading | Survival Wiki

## Objective
Understanding drainage patterns, wind behavior, and natural hazards lets you read the landscape like a map — and pick shelter sites that won’t try to kill you overnight.

## System
**Water Flow:** Water flows downhill. Ridgelines shed water in all directions. Slopes channel water downhill, with steeper slopes moving water faster. Valley floors and depressions collect water, which floods first and drains last. Saddles can channel wind and water, sometimes acting as sheltered passes. **Thermal Dynamics:** Cold, dense air sinks downslope into valleys (katabatic wind) at night, making valley floors and low areas 5-10 degrees C colder than mid-slope positions.

## Process
When arriving at a potential shelter area, spend 15 minutes doing a terrain read:
1. Identify the high ground (ridges, rises, and elevated platforms) to determine starting candidates.
2. Map the drainage (follow low lines, gullies, and dark soil) and eliminate any site in or near a drainage path.
3. Feel the wind (determine direction and strength) and find the leeward side of a natural windbreak.
4. Scan for hazards (look up for dead wood, look around for animal signs, look down for insect nests, check for unstable ground).
5. Choose the best compromise, prioritizing safety over comfort.

## Reading Drainage
Water flows downhill. Learning to read drainage patterns before the rain starts is a survival skill.

### How Water Moves Through Terrain
*   **Ridgelines and hilltops:** Shed water in all directions. Good for drainage, bad for wind exposure.
*   **Slopes:** Channel water downhill. Steeper slopes move water faster. Look for rills, small gullies, or lines of eroded soil — these are the paths water already takes.
*   **Valley floors and depressions:** Collect water from all surrounding slopes. These flood first and drain last.
*   **Saddles:** Can channel wind and water. Sometimes useful as sheltered passes, but avoid camping in the lowest point.

### Reading Drainage Indicators
| Indicator | What It Means |
| :--- | :--- |
| Dark, rich soil in a line or depression | Water channels here regularly — organic material collects in drainage paths |
| Gravel or sand deposits in a pattern | Fast-moving water sorted and deposited material here during floods |
| Debris lines on tree trunks or rocks | High-water mark — water reached this level before and will again |
| Green vegetation in an otherwise dry area | Subsurface water or drainage concentration — wet ground below |
| Erosion cuts in soil or exposed roots | Active water movement during rain events |
| Smooth, water-worn rocks | Persistent water flow, even if the channel is currently dry |
| Moss or algae on one side of rocks | Indicates persistent moisture; that side stays wet longer |

### The Stick Test
If unsure about slope direction, place a round stick or water bottle on the ground and watch which way it rolls. Water will follow the same path.

### The Dig Test
Dig a small hole 15-20 cm deep at your potential site. Wait 10 minutes. If water seeps in, the water table is too high for a ground-level shelter. Move upslope or pick a different area entirely.

## Reading Wind
Wind determines how fast your body loses heat. Understanding local wind patterns helps you position your shelter to block it or use it.

### Prevailing Wind Direction
*   **Tree lean:** Trees grow leaning away from prevailing wind. One-sided canopies (flag trees) point away from the dominant wind direction.
*   **Snow or sand drifts:** Accumulate on the leeward (sheltered) side. The wind blows from the opposite direction.
*   **Erosion patterns:** Windward sides of hills and ridges show more weathering and less vegetation.
*   **Branch growth:** Branches and foliage are denser on the leeward side of trees.

### Local Wind Effects
**Valley Winds (Anabatic and Katabatic)**
*   During the day, sun heats valley slopes, creating an upslope breeze (anabatic wind). Valley floors are relatively calm during the day.
*   At night, slopes cool rapidly, and cold, dense air sinks downslope into valleys (katabatic wind). Valley floors and low areas become the coldest spots (5-10 degrees C colder than slopes above).
*   The ideal overnight position is one-third to halfway up a slope — above the cold air pool, below the windswept ridge.

**Funnel Effects**
*   Narrow gaps between hills, cliff walls, or rock formations squeeze wind through at higher speeds. A gentle breeze on open ground can become a strong gust through a gap. Avoid building in natural wind tunnels (notches, narrow valleys, gaps between large boulders).

**Eddies and Turbulence**
*   On the leeward side of large obstacles, wind creates turbulent eddies. The area directly behind the obstacle may feel calm, but just beyond it the wind swirls unpredictably. For reliable wind protection, build close to the windbreak — within a distance roughly equal to the windbreak’s height.

### Wind Speed Estimation
| Observation | Estimated Wind Speed |
| :--- | :--- |
| Smoke rises vertically | Calm (< 2 km/h) |
| Smoke drifts, leaves barely move | Light (2-10 km/h) |
| Leaves and small twigs move constantly | Moderate (10-25 km/h) |
| Small branches sway, dust and loose paper lift | Fresh (25-40 km/h) |
| Large branches sway, whistling in wires/gaps | Strong (40-60 km/h) |
| Whole trees sway, resistance walking into wind | Gale (60+ km/h) — seek immediate solid shelter |

## Reading Hazards

### Overhead Hazards
*   Dead branches fall regularly due to wind, rain, ice, and decay. This is constant.
*   **How to check:** Stand at the proposed site, look up, scan every tree within falling distance (roughly 1.5x the tree height), look for dead branches (no leaves, peeling bark, lighter color), look for leaning trees (with shallow root plates or heaving soil at the base), and check for hung-up trees.
*   **Warning:** “Widow maker” is not a colorful nickname. Falling branches kill people in forests regularly. Never sleep under dead wood.

### Ground-Level Hazards
*   **Ant mounds and wasp nests:** Found in the ground, logs, and trees. Disturbing them at night in an enclosed shelter is dangerous.
*   **Snake habitat:** Rock piles, fallen logs with gaps underneath, and thick brush are common. Build a raised sleeping platform if possible in snake territory.
*   **Poison plants:** Poison ivy, poison oak, and stinging nettles can make shelter-building material miserable. Learn to identify them: “leaves of three, let it be” covers many offenders.
*   **Standing dead trees (snags):** Even without branches overhead, they can fall in their entirety during wind. Give dead standing trees a wide berth — at least 1.5x their height.

### Terrain Instability
*   **Undercut banks:** River and stream banks that are undercut can collapse, especially when wet. Never build on or under an undercut bank.
*   **Loose scree slopes:** Talus and scree (loose rock on steep slopes) can slide. Do not build at the base of a scree field.
*   **Saturated hillsides:** Steep hillsides with clay soil can fail as mudslides. Look for cracked soil, tilted trees, or fresh exposed earth — signs of recent or imminent movement.
*   **Coastal zones:** Tide lines, storm surge zones, and unstable dunes change rapidly. Build well above the highest visible tide line and watermark.

## Key Takeaways
*   Water always follows the same paths — read the indicators (dark soil, gravel deposits, debris lines, green strips) to know where those paths are before rain arrives.
*   Cold air sinks at night into valleys and low areas, making them 5-10 degrees C colder than mid-slope positions. Build one-third of the way up, not at the bottom.
*   Wind is predictable: prevailing direction shows in tree lean and erosion patterns; local effects (katabatic flow, funneling) follow terrain features.
*   Always look up. Dead branches, leaning trees, and hung-up trees are the most common and most overlooked shelter-site hazard.
*   Spend 15 minutes reading terrain before committing to a site. That investment prevents overnight disasters.
