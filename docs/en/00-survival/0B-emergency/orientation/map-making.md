# Map Making

## Objective
To create a hand-drawn map built on a measured, oriented baseline between two identifiable landmarks to capture a territory’s water sources, trails, and resources in a form anyone in the group can use and update.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- A measured, oriented baseline between two identifiable landmarks is required to start every map.
- A flat surface is needed to draw on.
- A systematic method is required for execution.

## Material
| Material | Purpose | Sources |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Paper or card | Drawing surface | Notebooks, books (blank pages), cardboard, birch bark |
| Pencil | Drawing (erasable) | Offices, schools, homes |
| Charcoal stick | Drawing (available anywhere) | From any fire — burn a stick, sharpen the end |
| Straightedge | Drawing lines | Any flat stick, ruler, strip of stiff material |
| Compass | Orienting the map | See [[00-immediate-survival/compass-building]] |
| Pacing cord or string | Measuring distance | Any cordage marked at intervals |
| Protractor (improvised) | Measuring angles | Draw a semicircle with degree markings using geometry |

**Weatherproofing:** If the map will be carried outdoors, rub candle wax or rendered fat lightly over both sides to make the paper water-resistant. Alternatively, store it in a sealed container (a zip-lock bag if available, or a lidded tin).

## System
### Map Foundation
Every map starts with a known line: the baseline, which is a real, measured line between two identifiable points that forms the foundation of the entire map.

### Scale Setting
Scale tells the relationship between distances on the map and distances on the ground. Suggested scales based on purpose:

| Map Purpose | Suggested Scale | 1 cm on Map = | 
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Camp/settlement detail | 1:500 | 5 meters |
| Local area (1-2 km radius) | 1:5,000 | 50 meters |
| Regional (5-10 km radius) | 1:25,000 | 250 meters |
| Route map (long distance) | 1:50,000 | 500 meters |

### Orientation
North must be established by drawing a north arrow. If magnetic north is shown, label it “MN”; if true north is used, label it “TN”.

### Grid System (For Settlements)
To make verbal communication precise, draw a grid of evenly spaced horizontal and vertical lines. The spacing depends on the scale (e.g., 1 cm spacing at 1:5,000 gives 50-meter grid squares). Vertical lines are numbered (eastings); horizontal lines are numbered (northings). Any point is referenced by its grid coordinates (e.g., “water source at grid 05-12”).

### Feature Mapping
- **Terrain:**
    - *Hachure Marks:* Short lines drawn perpendicular to slopes, pointing downhill. Closer lines indicate steeper slopes.
    - *Spot Heights:* Estimate the height difference from the baseline at hilltops or valley bottoms.
    - *Form Lines:* Freehand lines following the approximate contour of the terrain, often dashed or dotted.
- **Water Features:**
    - *Streams/Rivers:* Double lines for wide water, single lines for narrow. Add arrows showing flow direction. Mark fording points with a symbol.
    - *Springs/Sources:* Mark with a distinctive symbol (a small circle with a dot). Label as “reliable” or “seasonal.”
    - *Swamps/Wet Ground:* Horizontal dashes or a conventional marsh symbol.
    - *Lakes/Ponds:* Outline the shape and shade lightly.
- **Trails/Routes (Traverse):**
    - *Compass-and-Pace Traverse:* Start from a known point, take a compass bearing to the next turning point, walk the distance (counting paces), and draw a line from the start point in the compass direction at the scaled distance.

## Technology
- **Triangulation:** The core technique for locating distant features by taking compass bearings from two or more known points and finding the intersection of the resulting lines.
- **Compass/Bearing:** Essential for orienting the map and determining the direction of traverses.

## Tools
- **Baseline Measurement:** To establish the starting line.
- **Pacing:** To measure distances between points.
- **Scaling:** To convert ground distances to map distances.
- **Labeling Tools:** To add the north arrow, scale bar, legend, and date.

## Process
1. **Choose Your Baseline:** Select two landmarks that are clearly visible, permanent, and at least 100 meters apart (200-500 meters ideal).
2. **Measure the Baseline:** Pace the distance between the two points and record the measured distance in paces, converting it to an approximate meter value.
3. **Set the Scale:** Choose a scale based on the map purpose and draw a scale bar on the margin labeled with real-world distances.
4. **Establish North and Draw the Baseline:** Stand at point A, take a bearing to point B, draw a line from A, and mark point B at the correct scaled distance. Draw a north arrow.
5. **Triangulation:** To locate features:
    - Stand at baseline point A, take a bearing to a distant feature, and draw a line on the map.
    - Walk to baseline point B, take the same bearing, and draw a line on the map.
    - The intersection is the feature’s position.
    - *Accuracy Improvement:* Use three bearings to form a triangle; the feature lies within this triangle. Check for metal objects or re-measure if the triangle is large.
6. **Adding Features by Traverse:** To map routes:
    - Start from a known point.
    - Take a compass bearing to the next turning point.
    - Walk to that point, counting paces.
    - Draw a line from the start point in the compass direction, at the scaled distance.
7. **Terrain and Elevation:** Show terrain using Hachure Marks, estimate spot heights, and use Form Lines for general contours.
8. **Water Features and Vegetation:** Draw streams with double lines, mark fording points, use symbols for water sources, and approximate vegetation boundaries.
9. **Map Margins and Metadata:** Include a North arrow, scale bar, date created, creator name, and a legend/key.
10. **Updating and Copying:** Carry a working copy into the field. Add new information in pencil, transfer confirmed additions to the master with ink/charcoal, and date every addition. Copy critical maps by tracing.

## Notes
- **Accuracy Consideration:** Accuracy in triangulation depends on the angle of intersection; near 90 degrees provides the most accuracy.
- **Slope Correction:** Distances measured by pacing on slopes are shorter than horizontal map distances. On a 30-degree slope, measured distance is about 15% longer than the horizontal distance.
- **Common Mistakes:** Failures include not establishing a baseline, forgetting slope correction, mixing magnetic and true north without labeling, drawing too much detail, and failing to weatherproof the map.
- **Common Errors in Traverse:** Route closure errors (missed start point) must be corrected by distributing the closure error across all legs. Errors caused by compass readings near metal objects must be corrected by re-walking the leg away from metal.
