# Pace Counting

## Objective
To measure distance on foot without the use of instruments, serving as a fundamental part of navigation and enabling dead reckoning.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   **Calibration:** The most important step, requiring measurement of a known distance (e.g., 100 meters) to establish a personal pace count.
*   **Personal Pace:** The count must be calibrated based on individual leg length, walking speed, footwear, fatigue, and load.

## Material
*   Feet (for counting steps).
*   Cord or string (for ranger beads or the knot method).
*   Small pebbles or small bones or wooden discs (for the pebble transfer method).
*   A measuring tape or rope (for baseline measurement).

## System
*   **Pace Counting:** The field method for measuring distance by counting footfalls.
*   **Dead Reckoning:** The combination of pace counting with a direction (from the sun, stars, or compass) to determine a rough location.

## Technology
*   The core method relies on manual counting, indicating a system without technology.
*   Counting systems (beads, pebbles, knots) serve as physical tracking mechanisms when mental counting becomes unreliable.

## Tools
*   Measuring tape or rope (for baseline calibration).
*   Ranger Beads (a cord with two sets of beads for tracking distance).
*   Pebbles (for the pebble transfer method).
*   Fist/Finger (for the finger method).

## Process
### Calibration (Finding Your Personal Pace Count)
1. **Measure a known distance** (e.g., 100 meters). If no tape is available, use standard field metrics (e.g., running track straightaway = 100m).
2. **Walk the baseline** at your normal travel pace, carrying your expected load and wearing normal footwear.
3. **Count every time your left foot hits the ground** from start to finish.
4. **Repeat three times** and average the results to establish the personal pace count per 100 meters.

### Terrain Adjustment
*   The flat-ground number must be adjusted based on terrain type.
*   **Examples of Adjustments (if flat baseline is 70):**
    *   Gentle uphill: +10% (77 per 100 m)
    *   Steep uphill: +20-30% (84-91 per 100 m)
    *   Gentle downhill: -5% (67 per 100 m)
    *   Steep downhill: +10% (77 per 100 m)
    *   Sand/Gravel: +15-20% (81-84 per 100 m)
    *   Deep snow: +25-40% (88-98 per 100 m)

### Counting Systems
*   **Ranger Beads:** A cord with two sets of beads. 9 beads on the bottom section count hundreds of meters; 4-5 beads on the top section count kilometers (9 bottom beads = 900m; pulling the top bead down = 1 km).
*   **Pebble Transfer Method:** Transfer one pebble every 100 meters; 10 transferred pebbles equal 1 kilometer.
*   **Finger Method:** Extend one finger every 100 meters (5 fingers = 500m); transferring the count between hands (one hand = 500m; both hands open = 5 km).
*   **Knot Method:** Tie a simple overhand knot every 100 meters; tie larger knots/loops for kilometers in multi-day travel.

### Maintaining Accuracy
*   **Count Rhythm:** Develop a rhythm (e.g., counting in tens) to reduce mental load, transferring a bead at a calibrated count (e.g., counting to 'seven' for 70 paces).
*   **Dealing with Interruptions:** Note the current count before stopping, and restart counting from that number when resuming.
*   **Group Pace Counting:** Assign two counters; compare results, and average the counts to find the best estimate.

## Notes
*   **Fatigue Effect:** Pace shortens as fatigue sets in. After several hours with a heavy load, expect the pace count per 100 meters to increase by 5-10%.
*   **Accuracy Target:** The goal is 5% accuracy on flat ground and 10-15% accuracy on rough terrain.
*   **Historical Context:** Pace counting was the primary distance measurement for professional armies and surveyors for millennia (e.g., the Roman mile being 'a thousand paces').
*   **Common Mistakes:** Key errors include counting every footfall instead of one, failing to calibrate, losing count after interruptions, and striding deliberately rather than walking naturally.
*   **Key Takeaway:** Practice the full system: calibrate, walk, count, compare across varied terrain before needing it in survival situations.
