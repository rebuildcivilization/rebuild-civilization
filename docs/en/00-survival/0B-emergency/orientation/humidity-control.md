# Humidity Control

## Objective
To master moisture management in storage spaces to prevent food spoilage. Humidity is the invisible enemy of stored food; too much moisture breeds mold and rot, while too little dries out root vegetables until they shrivel.

## Dependencies and prerequisites

**Fundamental Conflict:** Root vegetables require high humidity, whereas dried and cured goods require low humidity. The prerequisite for successful storage is partitioning and managing airflow separately when storing both types of material.

**Temperature Consideration:** Temperature alone is insufficient; humidity levels must also be managed.

## Material

*   **Root Vegetables:** Require high humidity (85-95%)
*   **Dried/Cured Goods:** Require low humidity (below 65%)

## System

**Zone-Based Storage Design:** The most effective system divides storage into three distinct zones:

1.  **Zone 1 — High Humidity (85-95%):** Lowest level, earthen floor, minimal ventilation. Stores potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips, cabbages, and apples.
2.  **Zone 2 — Medium Humidity (65-80%):** Mid-level shelving, moderate airflow. Stores onions, garlic, squash, pumpkins, and fermented foods in sealed vessels.
3.  **Zone 3 — Low Humidity (below 65%):** Highest shelves near the exhaust vent, or a separate dry room. Stores dried meat, jerky, pemmican, dried herbs, dried fruit, salt-cured goods, and grain.

**Ventilation System:** A natural convection cycle utilizing two vents (one low, one high) to continuously remove moisture by allowing cool, dry air in and warm, moist air out.

## Technology

### Humidity Measurement Methods (Without Instruments)

*   **The Cloth Test:** Check for dryness (below 60%), slight coolness (60-75%), distinct dampness (80-90%), or dripping (above 95%).
*   **The Salt Test:** Check for looseness (below 75%), clumping (above 75%), or dissolution into brine (above 90%).
*   **The Bread Test:** Mold within 48 hours indicates humidity above 80%; drying hard without molding indicates humidity below 65%.
*   **The Metal Test:** Condensation on a metal surface indicates air is at or near dew point (very high relative humidity).

### Moisture Control Techniques

**Raising Humidity (For Root Vegetable Storage):**
1.  **Wet Floor Method:** Spread 2-4 inches of gravel or coarse sand, saturate with water, and re-wet every 2-4 days.
2.  **Wet Burlap Method:** Hang damp burlap sacks, re-wet daily, effective with mild airflow.
3.  **Water Pan Method:** Place shallow pans of water near the air intake vent, changing water weekly.
4.  **Packed Earth Walls:** Leaving earth walls unlined allows natural regulation.

**Lowering Humidity (For Dried and Cured Goods):**
1.  **Ventilation:** The single most effective tool. Install low and high vents to establish a convection cycle.
2.  **Desiccant Materials:**
    *   Charcoal: Excellent natural desiccant, replaced every 2-3 weeks.
    *   Dry wood ash: Absorbs moisture well, replace weekly.
    *   Quickite (calcium oxide): Aggressive desiccant, requires ventilated containers.
    *   Dry sand or sawdust: Moderate desiccant effect and food-packing medium.
3.  **Drainage:** Dig a sump (12-18 inches deep) lined with gravel to collect seepage and bail out regularly.

## Tools

*   Cloth (dry, cotton, linen)
*   Dry salt
*   Piece of metal (knife blade, tin can)
*   Small piece of bread
*   Gravel or coarse sand
*   Damp burlap sacks
*   Shallow pans or troughs
*   Charcoal, dry wood ash, Quickite

## Process

### Humidity Level Effects

| Humidity Level | Effect on Root Vegetables | Effect on Dried/Cured Goods |
| :-------------- | :------------------------ | :-------------------------- |
| Below 60%       | Shriveling, weight loss, rubbery texture | Ideal — stays dry and stable |
| 60-75%          | Mild dehydration over weeks | Acceptable if air moves |
| 75-85%          | Slight surface condensation | Beginning of moisture uptake — risky |
| 85-95%          | Ideal for roots, tubers, cabbages | Mold growth, spoilage within days |
| Above 95%       | Condensation pools, rot accelerates | Rapid spoilage, dangerous |

### Ventilation Schedule by Season

| Season | Vent Management |
| :--- | :--- |
| Dry summer days | Open both vents fully — maximum drying |
| Humid summer nights | Close intake, leave exhaust cracked |
| Fall harvest | Open during day, close at night |
| Winter (below freezing) | Close both — cold air holds little moisture |
| Rainy periods | Close both vents completely |

### Key Management Process Steps
1.  **Establish Baseline:** Monitor conditions during the Fall harvest to set initial baseline.
2.  **Zone Placement:** Place root vegetables in Zone 1, dried goods in Zone 3.
3.  **Moisture Management:** Apply raising methods (gravel, burlap) to Zone 1, and applying desiccants (charcoal) to Zone 3.
4.  **Monitor & Act:** Check stores weekly and remove spoiled items immediately.

## Notes

*   **Ethylene Gas:** Apples, pears, and some fruits release ethylene gas, accelerating ripening/rotting of nearby produce. Fruits should be stored separately from vegetables with independent ventilation.
*   **Standing Water Risk:** Open water attracts insects. To manage this risk, add a thin film of rendered fat to water surfaces or use covered containers with cloth wicks to create evaporative surface without open water.
*   **Critical Rule:** Open vents during dry weather, close them during rain or fog, as opening them in the rain pumps humid air into the stores.
*   **Drainage Imperative:** Always implement a sump and drainage plan for underground or semi-underground cellars.
