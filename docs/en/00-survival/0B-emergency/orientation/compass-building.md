# Making a Magnetic Compass

## Objective
To build a functioning magnetic compass in under ten minutes from a steel needle and a cup of still water.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   A steel needle or pin (aluminum, brass, and high-chromium stainless steel will not hold a charge).
*   A cup of still water (or an alternative float like cork or foam).
*   A method to magnetize the needle (a magnet, silk, fur, hair, or a battery and insulated wire).
*   A method for determining which end is north (cross-reference with the sun or stars).

## Material
| Material | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- |
| Steel needle or pin | Compass needle |
| Magnet (optional) | Magnetizing source |
| Silk, fur, or hair | Friction magnetizing |
| Leaf or bark chip | Flotation platform |
| Still water | Bearing surface |
| Cork or foam (optional) | Alternative float |
| Razor blade (alternative) | Alternative needle |

## System
*   **Magnetism:** The needle aligns with Earth’s magnetic field when magnetized.
*   **Surface Tension:** Used in Method 2 (Razor Blade Compass) where a thin, flat steel blade floats on the water surface.
*   **Friction:** Used in Method 1 to create a weak magnetic charge by stroking the needle against a ferromagnetic material.

## Technology
*   The Earth’s magnetic field provides a reliable baseline direction (north-south).
*   Magnetic declination (the angle between magnetic north and true north) must be considered.

## Tools
*   A container for still water (cup, bowl, helmet, hollow rock).
*   A source of magnetization (magnet, silk, fur, hair, or battery/wire).
*   A method for cross-referencing direction (sun position, shadow stick).

## Process
### Method 1: Floating Needle Compass
1.  **Select Your Needle:** Use a standard sewing needle or alternatives like safety pins, staples, small nails, or straightedge razor blades (3 cm/1.2 inch minimum). 
2.  **Magnetize the Needle:**
    *   **Option A (Magnet Stroke Method):** Hold the needle steady, place a magnet pole at the eye end, stroke the magnet along the full length toward the point, lift at the tip, and repeat in the **same direction** 30-50 times.
    *   **Option B (Friction Method):** Stroke the needle rapidly in one direction against silk, wool, fur, or dry hair for a minimum of 80-100 strokes in the same direction.
3.  **Prepare the Float:** Use a leaf (2-4 cm across) or bark chip, or push the needle through cork/foam.
4.  **Assemble and Float:** Place the float on the water, gently lay the magnetized needle on top, center it, and wait 10-20 seconds for it to align with the Earth’s magnetic field.
5.  **Determine Which End Is North:** Cross-reference the needle's direction with the sun (morning sun is roughly east). Mark the north-pointing end.

### Method 2: Razor Blade Compass
1.  Magnetize the blade using the stroke method.
2.  Fill a container with still water until the surface is slightly above the rim (surface tension dome).
3.  Gently lower the blade flat onto the water surface; it will float.
4.  The blade will rotate to align north-south.

### Method 3: Suspended Needle Compass
1.  Magnetize the needle.
2.  Tie a thread around the center balance point of the needle.
3.  Suspend it from a branch, doorframe, or finger, shielding it from wind.
4.  Wait for it to stop swinging; it will settle pointing north-south.

## Notes
*   **Magnetization Strength:** Friction-charged needles lose their charge within hours. Magnet-stroked needles last days to weeks depending on the steel’s carbon content.
*   **Maintenance:**
    *   Re-magnetize daily for friction-charged needles.
    *   Keep the needle dry to prevent rust, which weakens magnetic domains.
    *   Store the needle stuck to another piece of steel to slow demagnetization.
    *   Keep the needle away from heat; temperatures above 770C (1418F) permanently destroy magnetization.
*   **Accuracy Improvements:**
    *   Use a wider, flatter vessel to prevent the needle from drifting to the edge.
    *   Re-magnetize if the needle fails to rotate.
    *   Move away from large metal objects (vehicles, rebar) to avoid magnetic interference.
*   **Common Mistakes:**
    *   Stroking the needle back and forth, which randomizes the magnetic domains.
    *   Using stainless steel, as its high chromium content prevents it from holding a magnetic charge.
    *   Placing the compass on a metal surface, which distorts the reading.
    *   Assuming magnetic north is true north without accounting for magnetic declination.
*   **Permanent Housing Upgrade:** A proper instrument involves carving a shallow wooden bowl, marking cardinal directions (N, S, E, W) on the rim, sealing the interior, and mounting a pivot pin to balance the magnetized needle.
*   **Magnetic Interference:** False readings occur near large metal objects; readings should be taken at least 3 meters (10 feet) away from metal structures.

## Key Takeaways
1.  Any thin steel object can be a compass needle if magnetized by stroking in one direction with a magnet, silk, or hair.
2.  A floating needle on still water is the simplest reliable design; a suspended needle works without water.
3.  Always cross-reference the compass with at least one other method (sun position, stars, shadow stick) to confirm north.
4.  Re-magnetize daily for friction-charged needles.
5.  Stay away from large metal objects when taking readings because magnetic interference is the most common source of error.
