# Wind Direction Reading

## Objective
Practical methods for determining wind direction and building simple instruments to track it consistently.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- An open area is required for methods like the Grass and Leaf Toss.
- Light materials are needed for Flag and Cloth Strips.
- Specific conditions for the Wet Finger Test (moderate winds).

## Material
- Saliva
- Dry grass or fine dust or light leaf fragments
- Lightweight woven cloth, thin leather strips, unraveled rope fibers, or long grass bundles
- A straight stick or dowel (30-40 cm)
- A flat piece of wood, bark, or hide for the tail fin
- A vertical pole or post (2+ meters tall)
- A pivot point (a nail, sharpened stick, or smooth stone)

## System
- Weather Forecasting
- Wind Patterns

## Technology
- Evaporative cooling (Wet Finger Test)
- Understanding the difference between surface wind and upper wind.

## Tools
- Wet Finger Test
- Smoke Observation
- Grass and Leaf Toss
- Flag and Cloth Strips
- Simple Wind Vane

## Process
### The Wet Finger Test
1. Wet one finger with saliva and hold it up.
2. The side that feels cold is facing into the wind, as evaporative cooling is fastest on the windward side.

**Improving accuracy steps:**
- Use the back of the hand instead for more sensitive nerve endings.
- Hold the hand high above the head to clear ground-level turbulence.
- Rotate slowly until maximum cooling is felt on one side.
- If winds are very light, switch to smoke observation.

### Smoke Observation
1. Light a small fire or use a smoldering stick.
2. Watch the smoke trail at two heights:
    - Near the ground (1-2 m): Shows surface wind direction.
    - Higher up (treetop level): Shows upper wind direction, which is more representative of the large-scale weather pattern.
3. If surface and upper wind differ significantly, weather is likely to change soon.

### Grass and Leaf Toss
1. Pick up a handful of dry grass, fine dust, or light leaf fragments.
2. Toss them into the air from chest height.
3. Repeat three times and average the direction for accuracy.

### Flag and Cloth Strips
1. Hang a lightweight cloth strip, ribbon, or strip of bark fiber from a tall pole or branch.
2. Check it throughout the day and note when direction shifts occur.

### Building a Simple Wind Vane
**Construction:**
1. Attach the flat tail piece to one end of the pointer stick. The tail must have more surface area than the front.
2. Find the balance point of the pointer (it should be forward of center due to the tail weight—adjust by trimming).
3. Mount the pointer on the vertical pole at its balance point with a pivot that allows free rotation (e.g., a nail through the pointer into the pole top).
4. Mark cardinal directions (N, S, E, W) on the ground around the pole base using a compass, North Star, or shadow stick method.

**Testing:** Blow on the vane from different directions to confirm it reliably points into the wind.

### Recording Wind Direction
1. Use the 8-point compass system: N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW.
2. Record the following data:
    - Direction (8-point compass)
    - Relative strength (calm, light, moderate, strong)
    - Time of day
    - Weather that followed
3. **Recording schedule:** Check wind direction at dawn, midday, and dusk at minimum.

### Common Mistakes
- Reading in sheltered locations (wind near buildings, cliffs, or dense forest is deflected).
- Ignoring upper winds (trust the cloud direction when surface wind differs).
- Relying on single readings (observe for at least 30 seconds to identify the prevailing direction).

## Notes
- Wind direction is always stated as the direction the wind blows FROM, not where it blows toward.
- Finer divisions for the 8-point compass system are unnecessary for weather prediction.
- Consistent records over time reveal patterns specific to a location, helping to determine which wind directions bring rain, clear weather, or precede storms.
