# Climate-Specific Shelters

## Objective
To cover how to adapt shelter strategy to the climate one is actually in, recognizing that a generic debris hut works only in temperate forests.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
The core principles from Emergency Shelter still apply, which include: dead air space, wind protection, and ground insulation.

## Material
*   Debris (for the generic debris hut)
*   Snow (for added insulation and windproofing)
*   Bamboo or straight poles (for sleeping platforms)
*   Tarp
*   Rocks
*   Driftwood
*   Green logs (for fire reflectors)
*   Fabric (for netting)

## System
The system involves shifting shelter priorities based on environmental threats, recognizing that one design does not fit all climates.

## Technology
*   **Insulation:** Double-roof design with an air gap to maximize cooling (Hot/Arid).
*   **Windproofing:** Low profile shelters and windbreaks (Coastal/Windy).
*   **Waterproofing:** Steep roof pitches (minimum 30 degrees, ideally 45 degrees) and drip lines (Wet Temperate).
*   **Thermal Management:** Entry tunnels to allow cold air to sink while warm air stays in the sleeping chamber (Cold Climate).

## Tools
- None explicitly identified in the source text.

## Process
### General Adaptation Principle
Adapt shelter strategy based on specific climate threats. The priority ranking of core principles shifts dramatically depending on the environment.

### Cold Climate Adaptations
1.  **Shrink the interior:** Make the shelter barely large enough to lie in (coffin-sized) to minimize air volume that needs heating.
2.  **Double the insulation:** Increase debris covering (e.g., 60-90 cm) and pile snow on top for windproofing and insulation.
3.  **Seal the entrance:** Build a plug from stuffed bags, woven branches, or snow blocks. Maintain a ventilation gap the size of a fist to prevent CO2 buildup.
4.  **Dig down if possible:** Build a semi-subterranean shelter, digging down to the frost line in permafrost areas.
5.  **Entry tunnel:** Build a short entrance tunnel (60-90 cm long) angled downward to allow cold air to sink.

### Hot and Arid Adaptations
1.  **Maximize shade, maximize airflow:** Utilize a roof with open sides (e.g., raised tarp, lean-to with open sides).
2.  **Get off the ground:** Elevate the sleeping surface 30-45 cm on rocks or branches, or dig down 30-45 cm for cooler sand.
3.  **Double-roof design:** Use two layers of material with an air gap between them to prevent heat conduction.
4.  **Timing:** Shelter during the day and work/travel at night.

### Tropical and Humid Adaptations
1.  **Elevate everything:** Build sleeping platforms 60-90 cm off the ground to avoid water, insects, snakes, and scorpions.
2.  **Steep roof pitch:** Use 45 degrees or steeper to ensure water sheds and pools less.
3.  **Ventilation walls:** Use loosely woven palm fronds or spaced bamboo instead of solid walls to allow air circulation.
4.  **Mosquito defense:** Use smudge fires near the entrance or drape fabric netting over the sleeping area.

### Wet Temperate Adaptations
1.  **Waterproofing is priority one:** Use a well-thatched roof with overlapping layers and steep pitch (minimum 30 degrees).
2.  **Trench drainage:** Dig a shallow trench (10-15 cm deep, 15 cm wide) around the uphill side to divert runoff.
3.  **Raised bed:** Create a raised bed (15-20 cm off the ground) to prevent water wicking into bedding.
4.  **Fire reflector:** Use a lean-to with a fire reflector wall (stacked green logs behind the fire) to bounce heat into the shelter.

### Coastal and Windy Adaptations
1.  **Go low:** Maintain a low profile (no more than 60-90 cm tall at the ridgeline) to catch less wind.
2.  **Windbreak first:** Construct a wall of stacked driftwood, rocks, or sand-filled containers on the windward side.
3.  **Anchor everything:** Bury stakes deep (at least 30 cm), weight tarps with rocks, and lash joints twice. Use "deadman anchors" in sandy soil.
4.  **Sand management:** Orient the shelter opening perpendicular to prevailing wind and use a low barrier (30 cm wall) to block ground-level sand flow.

## Key Takeaways
- The same shelter design does not work everywhere — adapt to your specific climate threats.
- Cold climates: maximize insulation, minimize interior volume, seal against wind.
- Hot climates: maximize shade and airflow, get off the ground, use double-roof designs.
- Tropical climates: elevate the platform, steep roof pitch, prioritize insect defense.
- Wet climates: waterproofing and drainage come before everything else.

Notes
- The generic debris hut works specifically in temperate forests.
- The core principles of Emergency Shelter remain applicable across all adaptations.
- A warning explicitly stated is to never seal a shelter completely due to CO2 accumulation.
