# Storm Clouds | Survival Wiki

## Objective
Part of Weather Forecasting: Identifying dangerous cloud formations and estimating severity and time-to-impact.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- None explicitly identified in the source text.

## Material
### Cumulonimbus — The Thunderstorm Engine
- **Characteristics:** Stretches from near the surface to the top of the troposphere, contains updrafts exceeding 100 mph, produces lightning, heavy rain, hail, damaging winds, and tornadoes.
- **Identifying Features:** Massive vertical extent (towers above all other clouds), dark, flat base (sometimes with visible rotation), anvil-shaped top spreading out at high altitude, fibrous or icy appearance at the top edges, rain shafts visible beneath as dark curtains.
- **Life Stages:**
    1. **Developing (15-30 min):** Strong updraft only. Towering cumulus growing rapidly. Lightning may begin.
    2. **Mature (30-60 min):** Both updraft and downdraft present. Heaviest rain, strongest winds, largest hail, most lightning. This is when tornadoes form. The anvil is fully developed.
    3. **Dissipating (30-60 min):** Downdraft dominates, cutting off the updraft. Rain weakens, anvil spreads, cloud thins. Downdraft winds can exceed 60 mph at the surface.

### Nimbostratus — The Steady Soaker
- **Characteristics:** A thick, dark, featureless layer cloud that produces continuous moderate rain or snow for hours or days.
- **Identifying Features:** Uniformly dark grey, no visible features or texture, base is often ragged and indistinct, covers the entire sky (no blue visible), steady precipitation.
- **Meaning:** Indicates a large-scale weather system (warm front or occluded front) is passing. Rain will continue for 6-24 hours.
- **Time-to-impact:** Nimbostratus and rain arrive within 3-6 hours if altostratus thickens and lowers.

### Wall Clouds — Tornado Warning
- **Characteristics:** A localized, abrupt lowering of the rain-free cloud base beneath a cumulonimbus, indicating an intense updraft where a tornado is most likely to form.
- **Identifying Features:** A distinct block or wedge of cloud hanging below the main base, may rotate visibly (watch for slow, steady rotation over 10-20 minutes), often rain-free, typically 1-4 miles wide.
- **Meaning:** If a wall cloud persists for more than 10 minutes, the storm has a sustained rotating updraft (mesocyclone). If persistent rotation is visible, tornado probability is high. A tornado may descend within 10-30 minutes of rotation becoming visible.
- **Action:** If a rotating wall cloud is seen, get into the most solid shelter immediately. Potential time window: 10-30 minutes.

### Shelf Clouds — Outflow Boundary
- **Characteristics:** A low, wedge-shaped cloud formation at the leading edge of a thunderstorm’s outflow, looking like a rolling wave of cloud advancing toward the observer.
- **Identifying Features:** Long, horizontal wedge or arch shape, clearly attached to the storm’s main cloud base, leading edge is smooth and well-defined, often appears to be rolling or tumbling.
- **Meaning:** The storm’s cold air outflow is spreading along the ground, lifting warm air ahead of it. Indicates strong, gusty winds (40-70 mph) followed immediately by heavy rain.
- **Time-to-impact:** Wind hits first, then rain. Arrival time: 15-30 minutes depending on storm speed.

### Mammatus — Turbulence Indicator
- **Characteristics:** Rounded, pouch-like bulges hanging from the underside of an anvil or other cloud layer, looking like bubble wrap on the sky.
- **Identifying Features:** Rounded, hanging lobes, can be smooth or ragged, often illuminated dramatically by low sun angles.
- **Meaning:** Indicate strong turbulence and instability in the cloud layer, forming when cold, dense air in the anvil sinks into warmer air below. Indicates a powerful storm.
- **Action:** If developing ahead of a storm, the storm is severe. If seen after a storm passes, conditions are improving but remain unsettled.

### The Green Sky
- **Characteristics:** A greenish or yellow-green tint to the sky beneath a thunderstorm.
- **Cause:** Sunlight filtering through a deep cloud containing large water drops and ice stones, resulting from light scattering.
- **Meaning:** A reliable indicator of large hail, meaning the storm has lifted enormous quantities of water and frozen it into hailstones.
- **Expectation:** Hail ranging from pea-sized to baseball-sized. Hail larger than golf balls can be lethal.
- **Action:** Get under the most solid cover immediately. A green sky with a dark, rotating base signifies the most dangerous storms (potential for large hail and tornadoes).

### Estimating Storm Distance and Movement
- **Lightning Distance:** Count seconds between lightning flash and thunder. Divide by 5 for distance in miles, or by 3 for kilometers (Sound travels roughly 1 mile per 5 seconds).
- **Storm Speed:** Most thunderstorms move at 20-40 mph. A storm 10 miles away will reach you in 15-30 minutes. Storms in strong jet stream can move at 50-60 mph.
- **Storm Direction:** Storms generally move from west/southwest to east/northeast in the mid-latitudes. The anvil points in the direction the storm is moving. Rain shaft angle shows movement (if leaning toward you, the storm is approaching).
- **Multiple Storms:** Multi-cell storm complexes can affect an area for hours and produce severe weather from multiple directions.

### The 30-30 Rule
- **Rule:** When lightning is seen, count to the thunder. If the count is 30 seconds or less (storm within 6 miles), get to shelter. Stay in shelter until 30 minutes after the last thunder.
- **Safety Margin:** 30 minutes of silence reliably indicates the storm has moved to a safe distance.

## System
- The system relies on observation, identification of specific cloud types, and mathematical estimation techniques.

## Technology
- The primary technology is visual observation of cloud formations and lightning/thunder sequencing.

## Tools
- Visual perception.
- Clock/Timer for counting lightning intervals.
- A map or reference for storm movement direction.

## Process
1. Observe and identify the cloud type present (e.g., Cumulonimbus, Nimbostratus).
2. Analyze the specific identifying features of that cloud type.
3. Apply estimation techniques (e.g., lightning count, storm speed) to determine distance and time-to-impact.
4. Take appropriate action based on the identified threat (e.g., shelter based on Wall Cloud rotation, seek shelter based on the 30-30 Rule).
