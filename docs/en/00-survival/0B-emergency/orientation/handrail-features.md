# Handrail Features | Survival Wiki

## Objective
Part of Navigation Without Technology. A handrail is any long, continuous landscape feature you can follow without needing a compass or bearings.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- None explicitly identified in the source text.

## Material
Handrails are any long, continuous landscape features that can be followed, including rivers, ridgelines, roads, power lines, fences, and forest edges.

## System
Navigation by handrail relies on following a continuous landscape feature to maintain orientation, allowing the navigator to focus on speed, security, and observation instead of constant bearing checks.

## Technology
Technology required for traditional navigation (compass or bearings) is bypassed when using a handrail.

## Tools
- Sight
- Sound (to follow a river at night or in dense fog)
- Basic sense/terrain knowledge

## Process
### Basic Technique
1. Identify a handrail that runs roughly parallel to your direction of travel.
2. Move to the handrail using compass or terrain.
3. Follow it in your direction of travel.
4. Identify your departure point (an attack point) before starting, determining when to leave the handrail and navigate the final leg.
5. Leave the handrail and navigate to your destination using compass bearing or terrain features for the final short leg.

### Offset Following
Maintain a distance (50-200 meters) from the handrail for security and terrain benefits, rather than walking directly on it.

### Combining Handrails
Complex routes chain multiple handrails together, using transition points as waypoints. For example: Follow river $\rightarrow$ road bridge $\rightarrow$ power line crossing $\rightarrow$ hilltop. Each transition point is a waypoint.

## Technology
### Handrail Selection Criteria
When multiple handrails exist, choose based on:
- **Direction Match:** The handrail should run within 30-45 degrees of the desired direction of travel.
- **Continuity:** Prefer features running for long distances (e.g., 10 km) over fragmented ones.
- **Identifiability:** Must be recognizable and distinguishable from similar features.
- **Accessibility:** Must be reachable, followable, and safe to leave.

### Handrails in Different Terrain
- **Forest:** Rivers and streams are primary; follow watercourses downstream if lost; trails are secondary.
- **Mountains:** Ridgelines and valleys are dominant; ridgelines offer visibility and observation, valleys offer concealment.
- **Open Plains and Grassland:** Handrails are scarce; roads, fences, and power lines are critical; wind direction can serve as a rough handrail if consistent.
- **Desert:** Dry washes (wadis/arroyos) are handrails; roads are visible; dune ridges shift over time.
- **Urban/Suburban Ruins:** Streets are excellent; main roads follow routes; railways run between towns.

## Notes
- Rivers are excellent natural handrails but must be watched for gorges, waterfalls, and flood zones, as following them blindly into narrowing terrain can be fatal.
- When following a river, sound alone can be used in low visibility.
- Common Mistakes include following a river into a gorge, assuming a road goes where desired, losing the handrail in dense terrain, and walking right on the feature in hostile territory (leading to ambush vulnerability).
- A practice exercise involves walking parallel to a linear feature for 2 km with an offset, identifying departure points, and transitioning to a second handrail if available.
