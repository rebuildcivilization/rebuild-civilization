# Stratus Layers

## Objective
Reading flat, layered clouds to predict overcast conditions, drizzle, steady rain, and fog.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
Knowledge of cloud types to correctly identify and interpret atmospheric conditions.

## Material
### Stratus Clouds
Stratus clouds form when air cools uniformly across a wide area rather than rising in individual columns, resulting in flat sheets and layers. They are the most common clouds on Earth, covering vast areas and persisting for days. They dominate more of the weather experience than thunderstorms.

### Stratus — The Basic Layer
*   **Appearance:** A uniform, grey, featureless sheet covering the sky, resembling a ceiling. It lacks texture, breaks, or individual elements.
*   **Precipitation:** Light drizzle at most. True stratus is too thin to produce significant rain, resulting in tiny, slow-falling drops.
*   **Duration:** Hours to days. They often form overnight and persist until solar heating breaks them up. In maritime climates or winter, they can last for weeks.
*   **What it tells you:** The air is stable and moist at low levels but lacks energy for convective development. No storms will form. Conditions are safe but dreary. Stratus often lifts into stratocumulus as the day progresses.

### Stratocumulus — The Lumpy Layer
*   **Appearance:** A low layer of grey-white cloud with visible texture, including rounded lumps, rolls, or patches with darker crevices. Gaps of blue sky may be visible.
*   **Precipitation:** Occasionally light rain or drizzle, but usually dry.
*   **Differentiation:** Stratocumulus has visible structure; stratus is featureless.
*   **Differentiation from Altocumulus:** Stratocumulus lumps are larger than a fist (low level); altocumulus lumps are about thumb-sized (mid-level).
*   **What it tells you:** Low-level moisture with mild instability, enough for vertical motion (lumps) but not enough for storms. Stratocumulus typically breaks up during the afternoon.

### Nimbostratus — The Rain Machine
*   **Appearance:** A thick, dark, featureless layer covering the entire sky. The base is ragged and indistinct, often obscured by falling rain. Low, torn cloud fragments (scud) race beneath the main layer.
*   **Precipitation:** Continuous moderate rain or snow, lasting hours. This is steady, relentless precipitation from a deep cloud layer (potentially 10,000-15,000 feet thick).
*   **Differentiation from Stratus:** Nimbostratus is dark grey to nearly black, and produces real rain, unlike stratus which produces drizzle at most.
*   **What it tells you:** A major weather system is overhead (typically a warm front or occluded front). The rain will continue for 6-24 hours. There is no quick fix.
*   **Transition Sequence:** Cirrus (24-36 hours out) -> Cirrostratus (12-24 hours out) -> Altostratus thickens (6-12 hours out) -> Altostratus lowers and darkens into nimbostratus (rain begins). This sequence provides up to 36 hours of warning.

### Fog — Stratus on the Ground
Fog is stratus cloud at ground level, where visibility drops below 1,000 meters. The physics involve air cooling to its dew point and condensation, but implications differ because the observer is inside the cloud.

*   **Radiation Fog:**
    *   **How it forms:** Clear nights with light winds allowing the ground to radiate heat away, cooling the air to the dew point. Most common in valleys and low-lying areas.
    *   **When:** Late night and early morning, peaking near dawn. Requires clear skies and light winds.
    *   **Burn-off Prediction:** Thin fog burns off within 1-2 hours of sunrise. Thick fog may persist until mid-morning.
    *   **Rule of Thumb:** If the sun is visible as a bright disc, it will burn off within 2 hours. If the sun is not visible, expect fog through mid-morning.

*   **Advection Fog:**
    *   **How it forms:** Warm, moist air moves over a cold surface (cold ocean current, snow-covered ground, cold lake), cooling from below to the dew point. Common along coastlines.
    *   **When:** Any time of day or night; not dependent on solar heating.
    *   **Duration:** Can persist for days because the cold surface remains.

*   **Upslope Fog:**
    *   **How it forms:** Air forced up a slope (by wind or terrain) cools as it rises, forming fog on the slope if it reaches the dew point. Common on mountain flanks facing the prevailing wind.
    *   **Duration:** As long as the wind continues pushing air upslope; can last days.

### Layer Thickness and Precipitation Type
| Layer Thickness | Precipitation | Duration |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Under 3,000 ft | None or light mist | Hours to days |
| 3,000-6,000 ft | Drizzle | Hours |
| 6,000-10,000 ft | Light rain | Hours to half a day |
| Over 10,000 ft | Moderate steady rain | 6-24 hours |

### When Stratus Becomes Dangerous
*   **Hypothermia:** The prime window occurs during days of cold drizzle under nimbostratus, with temperatures in the 35-50 degrees F range. The condition of being wet, cold, and unable to dry out is critical.
*   **Flash flooding:** Nimbostratus producing steady rain for 12+ hours saturates the ground. Even moderate rain rates become dangerous when they persist; streams and rivers must be watched.
*   **Navigation:** Thick stratus and fog eliminate landmarks. Visual navigation should be replaced by compass bearings and pace counting.

## System
Weather Forecasting

## Technology
Cloud Reading

## Tools
Compass bearings and pace counting (for navigation when landmarks are eliminated).

## Process
1. Observe flat, layered clouds.
2. Identify the type (Stratus, Stratocumulus, Nimbostratus).
3. Determine the precipitation potential based on layer thickness.
4. Assess the overall atmospheric stability and moisture level.
5. Predict duration and potential dangers (hypothermia, flooding).


## Notes
- Stratus clouds are defined by uniform cooling across a wide area, contrasting with convective cumulus towers.
- Nimbostratus indicates a major weather system is overhead, leading to continuous moderate rain.
- Fog is explicitly defined as stratus cloud at ground level.
- The dew point spread rule is a tool for predicting overnight fog likelihood.
- Layer thickness is estimated by observing lightening of the cloud where the sun is (thin) or uniform grey (thick).
