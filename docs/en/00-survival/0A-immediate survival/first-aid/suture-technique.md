# Suture Technique

## Objective
Suturing is defined as sewing a wound shut with needle and thread, which is the most reliable method of wound closure available without modern medical infrastructure.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
**Pre-suture checklist:**
1. Wound is clean (irrigated with 500+ ml of clean water under pressure).
2. Wound is less than 6-8 hours old, OR performing delayed primary closure after 3-5 days of clean observation.
3. No signs of infection.
4. Bleeding is controlled (minor oozing is acceptable).
5. Needle and thread are sterilized.
6. Clean hands.
7. Adequate light to see what is being done.
8. The patient understands what is about to happen.

**Anesthesia/Pain Management:
Without lidocaine, options include:
- Ice or cold water applied for 10-15 minutes.
- Alcohol on the wound (causes burning but brief numbing).
- Oral pain control (strong spirits, willow bark tea, or available analgesics).
- Psychological preparation (explaining steps).
- Speed (a fast, confident suture job causes less total pain).

**Warning:** Do not give alcohol to someone in shock or who has lost significant blood, as it can worsen hemorrhagic shock.

## Material
### Needles
| Needle Type | Suitability | Preparation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sewing needle (curved) | Best improvised option | Sterilize by holding in flame until red-hot, then cool in alcohol or clean water |
| Sewing needle (straight) | Usable but harder to work with | Same sterilization; bend slightly with pliers to create a curve if possible |
| Safety pin | Usable for very small wounds | Open, sterilize, use the pointed end |
| Fish hook (barb filed off) | Good curve, strong | File off the barb completely; sterilize |
| Thorn (hawthorn, acacia) | Desperation only | Naturally curved, but fragile and nearly impossible to sterilize |
| Bone needle (hand-carved) | Historical precedent | Carve from dense bone, sand smooth, create an eye hole with a heated wire |

**Ideal Improvised Needle:** A medium sewing needle (3-5 cm long) bent into a gentle C-curve with pliers to allow for scooping motion.

### Thread / Suture Material
| Material | Strength | Absorption | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Fishing line (monofilament, 2-6 lb test) | Excellent | None (must be removed) | Best improvised option. Smooth, strong, low infection risk |
| Dental floss (unwaxed) | Good | None | Readily available; slightly abrasive |
| Silk thread | Good | Very slow | Natural material; wicks bacteria along the strand |
| Cotton thread | Moderate | Slow | Weakens when wet; double or triple for strength |
| Thin gut / sinew | Good | Yes (weeks) | Traditional surgical material; soak to soften before use |
| Hair (human or horse) | Weak | None | Historical emergency suture; only for very low-tension wounds |
| Nylon thread | Good | None | Synthetic; low bacterial wicking |

**Best Choice for Thread:** Monofilament fishing line, 4-6 lb test weight, because it is strong, smooth (less tissue damage), non-absorbent, and widely available.

## System
The system relies on the basic interrupted stitch as the foundation for wound closure.

## Technology
The technology employed is the mechanical process of sewing a wound shut using a needle and thread.

## Tools
**Needle Holder Alternatives (Without hemostats or needle driver):**
- Small pliers with smooth jaws (wrap jaws with cloth to prevent crushing the needle).
- Folded leather strip to grip the needle without slippage.
- Bare fingers (requires wrapping gripping fingers with tape or leather).
- Two flat sticks used as improvised chopsticks to grip the needle.

## Process
### Basic Suturing Technique: Simple Interrupted
**Positioning:**
- Position the wound to run left-to-right in front of the person (right-handed) or reverse for left-handed individuals.
- Hold the needle in the dominant hand, grasping it about two-thirds back from the tip with fingers or an improvised needle holder.
- Hold the thread tail with the non-dominant hand.

**The Stitch Steps:**
1. **Enter:** Push the needle through the skin on one side, 3-5 mm back from the wound edge, at a 90-degree angle to the surface (perpendicular entry).
2. **Scoop:** Scoop the needle through the tissue in an arc following the needle’s curve, ensuring the deepest point is deeper than the wound itself.
3. **Exit:** Exit the skin on the opposite side, the same distance from the edge (3-5 mm). Entry and exit points must be mirror images.
4. **Pull:** Pull the thread through, leaving a 3-4 cm tail on the entry side.
5. **Tie the Knot:** Use a surgeon’s knot (double-throw first, single-throw second, single-throw third):
    - Double-throw: Wrap around the needle holder twice, grab the short tail, and pull through.
    - First securing throw: Wrap once in the opposite direction, pull through.
    - Final lock: Wrap once more in the original direction, pull through.
6. **Tighten:** Tighten the knot just enough so the wound edges touch but do not pucker, overlap, or blanch white (white tissue indicates cut off blood supply, requiring immediate loosening).
7. **Cut:** Cut the thread, leaving 1 cm tails on both sides of the knot.
8. **Spacing:** Space stitches 5-8 mm apart along the wound.

**Stitch Placement Strategy (Halving Technique):**
1. Place the first stitch at the exact center of the wound.
2. Place the next stitches at the center of each half.
3. Continue halving until spacing is 5-8 mm throughout.

**Suture Removal:**
1. Clean the area with water or antiseptic.
2. Grasp one knot tail and lift the knot gently away from the skin.
3. Cut the thread on one side of the knot, between the knot and the skin.
4. Pull the suture out by the knot, pulling toward the wound.
5. If stuck, soak with warm water for several minutes before removal.

## Notes
**Common Mistakes and Prevention:**
| Mistake | Result | Prevention |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Entering too close to wound edge | Thread tears through tissue under tension | Stay 3-5 mm from the edge minimum |
| Unequal depth on each side | Wound edges at different heights; one side rolls under | Match entry and exit depth precisely |
| Knot too tight | White, blanched tissue that dies and opens the wound | Edges touching, not squeezed; tissue should remain pink |
| Stitches too far apart | Wound gapes between stitches; poor healing | Maximum 8 mm apart; closer in high-tension areas |
| Not sterilizing materials | Deep tissue infection, abscess, sepsis | Boil or flame-sterilize everything without exception |
| Suturing a dirty wound | Trapped infection becomes abscess or spreads systemically | Irrigate thoroughly; when in doubt, leave open |

**Key Takeaways:**
- Monofilament fishing line and a flame-sterilized curved sewing needle are the best improvised suture materials.
- The simple interrupted stitch is the most versatile and forgiving pattern—learn it first.
- Enter perpendicular to the skin, 3-5 mm from the edge, and scoop deeper than the wound to close dead space.
- Use the halving technique (center first, then quarter-points) for even wound alignment.
- Sterilize everything, close only clean wounds, and check your tension—edges touching, never blanching white.

**Additional Observations:** The source text explicitly mentions the need for adequate light and the importance of speed when suturing to minimize patient pain.
