# Medicinal Plants Guide

## Objective
To cover the most reliable, widespread healing plants, detail their uses, explain the chemical basis of plant medicine, and outline safe preparation and dosing methods for immediate survival and symptom management.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Knowledge of plant identification.
*   Understanding of safe dosing to avoid adverse reactions.
*   Recognition that plant medicine is a supplement to proper medical care, not a replacement for surgery or antibiotics.

## Material
### Essential Medicinal Plants
| Plant | Key Uses |
| :--- | :--- |
| Yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*) | Wound care, bleeding control, fever reduction, digestive aid |
| Plantain (*Plantago major* and *P. lanceolata*) | Insect stings, minor wounds, skin irritation, cough suppressant |
| Willow (*Salix* species) | Pain relief, fever reduction, anti-inflammatory |
| Chamomile (*Matricaria chamomilla*) | Sleep aid, digestive upset, anxiety, mild antiseptic |
| Elderberry and Elderflower (*Sambucus nigra*) | Immune support, cold and flu symptoms, fever |
| Peppermint and Wild Mint (*Mentha* species) | Digestive aid, nausea, headache, congestion, flavoring |
| Calendula (*Calendula officinalis*) | Wound healing, skin conditions, antifungal, anti-inflammatory |

### Chemical Basis
*   Willow bark contains salicin, which the body converts to salicylic acid, the active compound in aspirin.

## System
### Plant Medicine Philosophy
Plant medicine is chemistry, not folk wisdom or placebo. It is based on knowing which plants contain necessary compounds and how to prepare them safely. It serves as a supplement to proper medical care.

### Modern Medicine Context
Modern medicine relies on global supply chains (factories, shipping, refrigeration). Failure in these chains leads to a loss of access to antibiotics, anti-inflammatories, fever reducers, and wound care supplies.

## Technology
The technology involves identifying specific plant compounds, understanding preparation techniques (infusions vs. decoctions), and calculating safe dosages.

## Tools
*   Water (for infusions and decoctions).
*   Oil (for infusions/salves).
*   Beeswax (to solidify infused oils into salves).
*   Crushed leaves/bark (for poultices).

## Process
### Preparation Methods
**1. Making a Tea (Infusion)**
*   Bring water to a boil, then remove from heat.
*   Add 1-2 teaspoons of dried plant material (or a small handful of fresh) per cup of water.
*   Cover and steep for 10-15 minutes to trap volatile oils.
*   Strain and drink. Can be taken 2-3 times daily.

**2. Making a Decoction**
*   Add 1-2 teaspoons of chopped bark or root per cup of cold water.
*   Bring to a gentle simmer (not a rolling boil) and maintain for 15-20 minutes.
*   Strain and drink. Decoctions are stronger than infusions.

**3. Making a Poultice**
*   Chew, crush, or pound fresh plant material into a paste. If using dried material, moisten with hot water.
*   Apply directly to the affected area.
*   Hold in place with a cloth bandage or large leaf; replace every 2-4 hours.

**4. Making an Oil Infusion (For Salves)**
*   Fill a jar halfway with dried plant material (must be dried).
*   Cover completely with oil (stable oil like rendered animal fat, olive oil, or sunflower oil).
*   Seal and place in a warm spot for 2-4 weeks; shake daily.
*   Strain through cloth. Add melted beeswax (roughly 1:4 ratio of wax to oil) to make a solid salve.

### Dosing Safety
| Rule | Detail |
| :--- | :--- | 
| Start small | Use half the suggested amount first; increase only if no adverse effects occur. |
| One remedy at a time | Do not combine multiple plant medicines to identify the source of any reaction. |
| Watch for allergies | Pay attention to Asteraceae family plants (yarrow, chamomile, calendula). |
| Pregnancy caution | Avoid yarrow, large doses of mint, and most strong herbal teas. |
| Children | Use half adult doses for ages 6-12; quarter doses for under 6; avoid willow bark entirely for children. |
| Duration | Do not use any single plant medicine continuously for more than 2-3 weeks without a break. |

## Notes
*   Plant medicine treats symptoms and supports healing but cannot replace surgery, set bones, or cure bacterial infections the way antibiotics can.
*   Raw elderberries cause nausea and vomiting; they must always be cooked first. Leaves, bark, and stems of the Elderberry are toxic, requiring only flowers and cooked berries to be used.
*   Identification markers include distinguishing the hollow center of Chamomile flowers and the square stem/minty smell of Peppermint.
