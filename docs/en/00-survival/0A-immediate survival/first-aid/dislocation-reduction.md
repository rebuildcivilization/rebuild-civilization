# Joint Relocation | Survival Wiki

## Objective
To prevent permanent nerve damage and restore function within minutes by relocating a joint when it pops out of its socket and there is no hospital to drive to.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
**Assessment of the injury:**
1. Compare the injured side to the uninjured side to look for obvious deformity, swelling, and abnormal positioning.
2. Check circulation below the joint (feel for a pulse, check skin color and temperature; cold, pale, or blue skin indicates a compressed blood vessel).
3. Check nerve function (determine if the person can feel light touch below the injury or wiggle fingers/toes).
4. Document findings. (If circulation or sensation is compromised, roughly 4-6 hours before permanent damage sets in).

**Pain Management:**
- Have the person bite down on a leather strap or folded cloth.
- If access to alcohol is available, use a moderate amount (not excessive) for pain and muscle relaxation.
- Willow bark tea (containing natural salicin) can reduce pain; steep a thumb-sized piece of inner bark in hot water for 15 minutes.
- Wait 20-30 minutes after pain management before attempting reduction.

## Material
- Leather strap or folded cloth (for pain management).
- Alcohol (optional, for pain and muscle relaxation).
- Willow bark tea (for pain and swelling).
- Splints, slings, or buddy tape (for post-reduction immobilization).
- Cold water-soaked cloths or ice wrapped in fabric (for cold therapy).

## System
**Understanding Dislocations:**
A dislocation occurs when the bones forming a joint are forced completely out of alignment.

**Common Dislocated Joints and Risks:**
| Joint | Frequency | Difficulty to Reduce | Risk Level |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | 
| Shoulder (anterior) | Very common | Moderate | Low-medium |
| Finger/toe | Common | Easy | Low |
| Kneecap (patella) | Moderate | Easy-moderate | Low |
| Elbow | Less common | Difficult | High |
| Hip | Rare (high force) | Very difficult | Very high |

**Critical Safety Rules:**
- Never attempt to reduce a joint if a fracture at the same site is suspected (deformity with grinding or crepitus means a broken bone, not just dislocation).
- Never force a joint; if it doesn't reduce with steady, gentle traction after two attempts, immobilize it and wait for experienced help.
- Elbow and hip reductions carry serious risks of nerve and blood vessel damage; attempt only in true survival situations.

## Technology
The primary technology is manual manipulation and mechanical traction/rotation to realign the dislocated joint.

## Tools
- Hands (for traction, massage, and physical guidance).
- Splints, slings, or buddy tape (for post-reduction immobilization).

## Process
**Shoulder Reduction (Anterior Dislocation):**
1. **Cunningham Technique (Preferred - No Traction Needed):**
    a. Sit the patient upright.
    b. Have the patient place their hand of the injured arm on your shoulder.
    c. Ask them to consciously relax through slow breathing.
    d. Massage the muscles in this order (1-2 minutes per muscle): Trapezius, Deltoid, Biceps.
    e. Gently encourage the patient to sit up straighter and draw shoulders back.
2. **External Rotation Method (Alternative):**
    a. Patient lies on their back.
    b. Hold the elbow at 90 degrees.
    c. Very slowly rotate the forearm outward (externally), no faster than 1 degree per second.
    d. Continue until about 70-90 degrees of external rotation is reached.

**Finger and Toe Reduction:**
1. Grasp the dislocated digit firmly beyond the joint.
2. Apply steady traction along the axis of the digit.
3. Gently guide the bone back into alignment.
4. Buddy-tape the reduced digit to the adjacent digit for 2-3 weeks.

**Kneecap (Patellar) Reduction:**
1. Have the patient lie down and relax thigh muscles.
2. Slowly straighten the knee by lifting the ankle.
3. Gently push the kneecap toward the midline (inward).
4. Immobilize the knee in a straight position with a splint for 3-4 weeks.

**Post-Reduction Care:**
1. Re-check circulation and nerve function immediately.
2. Immobilize the joint (Shoulder: arm sling holding elbow at 90 degrees with a swath; Fingers: buddy tape; Kneecap: straight-leg splint).
3. Cold therapy: Apply cold water-soaked cloths or ice for 20 minutes on, 20 minutes off, for the first 48 hours.
4. Anti-inflammatory: Use willow bark tea 3 times daily.
5. Gentle movement: Start small range-of-motion exercises and gradually increase over 2-4 weeks.

**Recognizing Complications:**
- Loss of pulse below the joint after reduction (bone shifted onto a blood vessel).
- Increasing numbness or weakness in the days following (nerve compression).
- Inability to reduce after two attempts (tissue trapped or associated fracture).
- Re-dislocation with minimal force (requires prolonged immobilization of 4-6 weeks).

**Key Takeaways:**
- Always check circulation and nerve function before and after any reduction.
- Muscle relaxation is more important than force.
- Shoulders and fingers are the most common and safest to reduce in the field.
- Never force a joint; two gentle attempts maximum, then immobilize and splint.
- Post-reduction immobilization is critical to prevent chronic instability.

Notes
- The source explicitly mentions that circulation or sensation compromise requires action within roughly 4-6 hours before permanent damage sets in.
- The difference between dislocation and fracture is highlighted: deformity with grinding or crepitus signifies a broken bone.
- The Cunningham Technique is presented as the preferred method because it requires no traction.
