# Stitch Patterns

## Objective
To determine the correct stitch pattern based on wound geometry to ensure proper healing, as different wound geometries demand different stitch patterns.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
**Practice Materials:**
- Banana peels (to mimic tissue thickness and toughness)
- Raw chicken skin with the underlying meat (for realistic tissue layers)
- Pig feet (trotters) (closest to human skin available without surgical simulation tools)
- Leather scraps (for developing needle control)

**Prerequisite Skill:**
Practice each pattern until 10 stitches can be placed in under 5 minutes with consistent spacing and tension, as emergency performance relies on practiced motor skills.

## Material
- Needle
- Thread
- Bolsters (small pieces of rubber tubing or rolled gauze to prevent cutting through thin skin during horizontal mattress use)

## System
Suturing is a motor skill requiring consistent tension control and pattern recognition to select the appropriate closure method for specific tissue types and tension levels.

## Technology
The technology centers on selecting patterns that match the wound geometry to ensure proper skin-to-skin contact and force distribution across the tissue.

## Tools
- Thread
- Needle

## Process
### Pattern Selection Guide
Before threading, assess the wound and match it to the right pattern based on the following guide:

| Wound Type | Best Pattern | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Short, clean, low-tension laceration | Simple interrupted | Simplest, most forgiving, easiest to learn |
| Long, clean laceration (10+ cm) | Running (continuous) | Faster closure, even tension distribution |
| High-tension area (over joints, on extremities) | Horizontal mattress | Superior holding strength, resists pull-through |
| Wound edges tending to roll inward (evert) | Vertical mattress | Forces edges outward for proper skin-to-skin contact |
| Irregular or stellate wound | Simple interrupted (corner stitches) | Each stitch is independent; adapt to irregular geometry |
| Wound under extreme tension | Figure-of-eight or horizontal mattress with bolsters | Distributes force across wider tissue area |

### Specific Stitch Patterns

**Simple Interrupted Stitch:**
- **Foundation:** Each stitch is an independent unit, tied and cut separately.
- **Advantages:** If one stitch fails, the rest hold; easy to remove individual stitches for drainage; adjustable tension per stitch; simplest to learn under stress.
- **Disadvantages:** Slower for long wounds; uses more thread material; can create uneven tension if spacing is inconsistent.
- **Placement Recap:** Enter 3-5 mm from the edge, scoop to full depth, exit 3-5 mm from the opposite edge. Tie surgeon’s knot. Space 5-8 mm apart. Use the halving technique for alignment.

**Running (Continuous) Stitch:**
- **Technique:**
    1. Place the first stitch as a simple interrupted stitch (tie, but do NOT cut the thread).
    2. Take the next bite 5-8 mm along the wound. Enter and exit as for a simple interrupted stitch.
    3. Pull through, continue to the next bite, forming a spiral pattern.
    4. Maintain consistent tension. Use three throws for security at the final bite.
- **Running Locked Variant:** Lock each stitch by passing the needle through the loop of the previous stitch before pulling tight. Used for wounds longer than 15 cm or moderate tension.
- **Advantages:** Fast (3 to 4 times faster than interrupted for long wounds); even tension across the entire wound; uses less thread material.
- **Disadvantages:** If the thread breaks or one point fails, the entire closure can unravel; cannot selectively remove stitches for drainage.
- **Warning:** Never use a running stitch on a wound at high risk of infection, as losing the closure is permanent.

**Horizontal Mattress Stitch:**
- **Technique:**
    1. **Far-Far Bite:** Enter 8-10 mm from the edge, pass across, exit 8-10 mm from the opposite edge.
    2. **Near-Near Bite:** Reverse direction; enter 3-4 mm from the edge, cross, exit 3-4 mm from the starting edge.
    3. Tie the surgeon’s knot.
- **When to Use:** Wounds over joints, scalp lacerations, fascial closures, or any wound where simple stitches are pulling through tissue edges.
- **Spacing:** 10-15 mm apart.

**Vertical Mattress Stitch:**
- **Technique:**
    1. **Far-Deep Bite:** Enter 8-10 mm from the edge, pass deep, exit 8-10 mm from the opposite edge.
    2. **Near-Shallow Bite:** Reverse direction; enter 2-3 mm from the edge, pass superficially, exit 2-3 mm from the starting edge.
    3. Tie the knot.
- **Effect:** The deep pass provides holding strength; the shallow pass lifts edges upward, correcting inversion.
- **When to Use:** Wounds on the back of the hand or dorsal forearm, or any wound where simple stitches cause edges to roll inward.

**Figure-of-Eight Stitch:**
- **Technique:**
    1. Enter on one side, pass through to the other side.
    2. Cross over the surface and enter on the same side, offset 5-8 mm, pass through to the opposite side.
    3. The thread forms an X pattern.
- **When to Use:** Deep tissue layers, fascial closure, extremely high-tension wounds.

### Choosing Thread Tension
| Observation | Problem | Fix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Wound edges blanching white | Too tight — blood supply cut off | Loosen or remove and redo |
| Gap visible between wound edges | Too loose — wound will not heal | Add additional stitches or tighten |
| Wound edges overlapping or bunching | Uneven bites (deeper on one side) | Remove and redo with matching depth |
| Edges rolling inward (inverting) | Insufficient eversion | Switch to vertical mattress pattern |
| Thread cutting through tissue edges | Too tight, or material too narrow | Use mattress stitches with wider bites; add bolsters |

### Combining Patterns
For complex wounds:
1. **Start with horizontal mattress** stitches at the points of highest tension.
2. **Fill gaps with simple interrupted** stitches for fine alignment.
3. **Use a running stitch** only for long, low-tension segments where speed matters and infection risk is low.

## Notes
- The fundamental principle is that wound geometry dictates the pattern choice.
- Tension is the most common error in suturing.
- The running stitch is fast but sacrifices the ability to selectively drain a wound.
- Practice is crucial because emergency conditions degrade skill significantly.
