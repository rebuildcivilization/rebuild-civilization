# Rigid Splints: Field Fracture Management

## Objective
Rigid splints are the backbone of field fracture management. They prevent bending and rotational forces from reaching the fracture site, which is essential for proper healing in long bone fractures (femur, tibia, forearm, humerus).

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Knowledge of the principles of splinting (span joint above to joint below, pad everything, check circulation).
*   Materials (sticks, boards, bark, or improvised materials).

## Material
### Sticks and Branches
*   The most universally available rigid splint material.
*   Found in every environment with trees or shrubs.

### Boards and Planks
*   Scavenged from buildings, furniture, pallets, fences, and vehicles.
*   Often superior to sticks because they provide broad, flat support.

### Bark
*   Large sheets of bark from certain trees can be peeled, shaped, and dried into custom-molded rigid splints.

### Improvised Rigid Splints from Scavenged Materials
*   Rolled magazine (tight roll, secured with tape).
*   Cardboard (folded into 4-6 layers).
*   Metal strips (car body panels, shelving brackets).
*   Plastic pipe (PVC, drain pipe).
*   Shovel/broom handle.
*   Golf clubs, baseball bats, hockey sticks.
*   Folding chair legs.
*   Hardcover books.

## System
The system relies on applying rigid immobilization to manage fractures by preventing bending and rotational forces. The principle involves spanning the joint above to the joint below and padding all contacts.

## Technology
The technology centers on material properties and shaping to achieve rigid immobilization. Key considerations include:
*   **Material Strength:** The ability to resist flexing under pressure.
*   **Surface Area:** Using broader surfaces to distribute pressure.
*   **Shape Fidelity:** Ensuring the splint conforms to the limb's anatomy.

## Tools
*   Knife (for cutting/shaving).
*   Saw (if available).
*   Tape or ties (for securing).
*   Measuring device (for length determination).
*   Potentially a flat stick or knife blade (for prying bark).

## Process
### Selection Criteria (For Sticks)
*   **Diameter:** 3-5 cm (roughly thumb to two-finger width).
*   **Length:** Must extend from the joint above the fracture to the joint below, plus 10-15 cm extra at each end for secure tying.
*   **Straightness:** Choose the straightest available.
*   **Strength:** Must NOT flex more than 1-2 cm under strong pressure. Dead, dry hardwood is strongest. Avoid rotten wood and freshly cut green softwood.
*   **Smoothness:** Shave off knots, branch stubs, and sharp edges.

### Preparation
1.  Cut to length with a knife, saw, or by scoring around the circumference and snapping.
2.  Remove all side branches flush with the main shaft.
3.  Shave off rough bark if it will be against skin (or use bark as padding).
4.  For forearm splints, split a 5 cm stick in half lengthwise to create two flat-sided splints.

### Shaping (For Bark)
1.  While pliable, wrap the bark around the injured limb (over padding) and let it conform to the shape.
2.  Secure with ties while wet.
3.  As it dries (12-24 hours), it stiffens in the molded shape.
4.  For immediate rigidity, layer 3-4 sheets of bark with the grain direction alternating (0 degrees, 90 degrees, 0 degrees, 90 degrees) to create a plywood-like composite.

### Securing Rigid Splints
*   **Tie Width:** At least 2-3 cm wide to avoid cutting swollen tissue.
*   **Firmness:** Firm but not constricting; checked by the ‘finger test’ (one finger slides under each tie).
*   **Placement:** Minimum of 4 points: above the fracture, below the fracture, and near each joint being immobilized.
*   **Knot Type:** Use a square knot or other non-slip knot. Position knots on the outer side of the splint, away from the skin.

### Padding Materials
*   **Natural Padding:** Moss (sphagnum or soft varieties), dry grass or leaves (stuffed into cloth), animal fur or hide (soft side against skin), cattail fluff.
*   **Scavenged Padding:** Clothing (t-shirts, socks, scarves), towels, foam (vehicle seats, packing material, yoga mats), bubble wrap, insulation material (fiberglass-free types).

### Critical Padding Locations
*   Over bony prominences: ankle bones (malleoli), wrist bones, elbow point (olecranon), kneecap.
*   Between splint edges and skin.
*   Between two limbs when buddy-splinting.
*   In the palm of the hand (rolled cloth for grip) when splinting a forearm.

## Notes
*   **Bark Warning:** Fresh bark shrinks as it dries. Ties must be checked frequently and loosened as needed to prevent circulation compromise.
*   **Best Wood Types:** Oak, maple, ash, hickory (Excellent); Birch (Good); Pine, spruce, fir (Moderate); Bamboo (Excellent - strong-to-weight ratio); Willow (Poor - too flexible).
*   **Board Advantage:** Boards are superior to sticks because they distribute pressure broadly and prevent rotation.
