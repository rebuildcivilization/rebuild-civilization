# Bowline

## Objective
The bowline is the quintessential non-slip rescue loop. It is a fixed loop that holds under extreme load, never tightens on whatever it encircles, and unties cleanly after use.

## Dependencies and prerequisites

*   Understanding the anatomy of the bowline (standing part, working end, small loop, large loop).
*   Practice to achieve proficiency (learning the knot until it can be tied asleep, one-handed, behind the back, and in the dark).

## Material

*   **Rope Types Tested:** Dry natural fiber rope, wet natural fiber rope, dry synthetic rope (nylon, poly), rawhide or sinew, improvised plant cordage, Paracord (550), Bungee/elastic cord.
*   **Key Material Requirement:** For synthetic rope, a stopper knot is recommended on the tail.

## System

### Anatomy of the Bowline
*   **Standing part:** The long end of the rope leading to the load or anchor.
*   **Working end (tail):** The free end manipulated while tying.
*   **The small loop (“rabbit hole”):** Formed in the standing part; the working end passes through it twice.
*   **The large loop:** The fixed eye that goes around the object or person.
*   **The collar:** Where the working end wraps around the standing part before re-entering the small loop.

### Bowline Variations
*   **Water Bowline (Double Loop Bowline):** Adds a second turn to the small loop for resistance to shaking loose when repeatedly loaded and unloaded.
*   **Bowline on a Bight (Double Loop):** Creates two fixed loops from a single knot, useful for a two-point attachment.
*   **Running Bowline (Slip Noose):** Threading the standing part through the large loop results in a sliding noose that grips under load but releases when slack.

### Reliability Under Different Conditions
| Condition | Performance | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Dry natural fiber rope | Excellent | Ideal combination |
| Wet natural fiber rope | Excellent | Swelling actually increases grip |
| Dry synthetic rope (nylon, poly) | Good | Add a stopper knot to the tail |
| Wet synthetic rope | Fair | Slippery; use a water bowline or stopper |
| Rawhide or sinew | Excellent | Holds very well; hard to untie once dry |
| Improvised plant cordage | Good | Keep tail long; cordage is rough enough to grip |
| Paracord (550) | Good | Always back up with a stopper knot |
| Bungee/elastic cord | Poor | Do not use; elasticity allows capsizing |

### Failure Modes and Prevention
*   **Capsizing:** Occurs if the tail is too short or the knot is not properly dressed under ring-loading (force applied perpendicular to the loop). Prevention requires at least 15 cm (6 inches) of tail and careful dressing.
*   **Shaking loose:** An unloaded bowline can separate through vibration or repeated slack-and-load cycles. Prevention involves using a water bowline or tying a stopper knot in the tail.
*   **Incorrect tying:** Threading the working end through the small loop in the wrong direction results in a "cowboy bowline" or left-handed bowline, which is less stable. Prevention requires verifying the tail exits inside the main loop.

## Technology

Sailors have historically called the bowline the “King of Knots” due to its ability to create a fixed loop that will not slip, tighten, or jam, capable of handling thousands of pounds of force.

## Tools

*   Rope (various types).
*   Hands (for tying and testing).
*   Eyes (for critical inspection).

## Process

### Standard Two-Handed Method
1. Determine the loop size needed. Hold the rope where the bottom of the loop should be, with the working end in the dominant hand.
2. Measure enough working end to complete the knot (roughly three times the desired loop diameter plus 20 cm for the tail).
3. Form a small overhand loop in the standing part; the working end crosses *over* the standing part. Pinch the crossing point.
4. Pass the working end up through the small loop from underneath, pulling until the large loop is the desired size.
5. Route the working end behind the standing part (going away, around, back toward you).
6. Pass the working end back down through the small loop, ensuring it exits underneath, parallel to the near side of the large loop.
7. Tighten by holding the working end and the adjacent side of the large loop together, then pulling the standing part firmly. The small loop must close snugly around the collar.

### The Speed Method (Flying Bowline)
1. Hold the standing part in the non-dominant hand, palm up, lay the rope across the palm.
2. Twist the wrist toward you (clockwise for right-handers, counterclockwise for lefties) to form the small loop (the working end is already through it).
3. Route the working end behind the standing part and back through the loop.
4. Tighten. (Practice goal time: under three seconds).

### Teaching Others (The Rabbit Story Method)
This mnemonic is used to teach the knot:
1. “Make a small loop — that’s the rabbit hole.” (Form the overhand loop.)
2. “The rabbit comes out of the hole.” (Working end up through the loop.)
3. “The rabbit runs around the tree.” (Working end behind the standing part.)
4. “The rabbit goes back into the hole.” (Working end back through the loop.)
5. “Pull the tree to tighten.” (Pull the standing part.)

### Practice Progression
| Level | Drill | Goal Time |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Tie a bowline, two hands, looking | Under 15 seconds |
| 2 | Tie a bowline, two hands, eyes closed | Under 20 seconds |
| 3 | Tie a bowline around a post without seeing behind it | Under 25 seconds |
| 4 | Tie a bowline one-handed | Under 30 seconds |
| 5 | Tie a bowline around your own waist | Under 15 seconds |
| 6 | Teach someone else to tie a bowline | Under 5 minutes |

## Notes

*   **Critical Inspection Checklist:** Before loading any bowline, verify: (1) the tail runs parallel inside the main loop, (2) the tail is at least 15 cm long, (3) the knot is snug with no loose sections, and (4) for synthetic rope, a stopper knot must back up the tail.
*   **Key Takeaways:** The bowline creates a fixed, non-constricting loop. Always verify the tail exits inside the main loop and is at least 15 cm long. Back up with a stopper knot on slippery rope (synthetic/wet cordage). Teach the knot widely for maximum survival capability.
*   **Real-World Application Summary:** Used for shelter building (anchoring ridgelines), water rescue (creating loops large enough for under-arm placement), load securing (combined with trucker’s hitch), and improvised harnesses (two bowlines for a basic system).
