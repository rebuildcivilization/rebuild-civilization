# Rope Making

## Objective
To create strong, reliable rope by exploiting the principle that twist creates friction between fibers, which locks fibers together under load, resulting in a rope many times stronger than the sum of its individual strands.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   An understanding of how twist creates strength.
*   Manual dexterity (hands) for cordage.
*   A few simple aids can dramatically increase output.
*   Slightly damp fibers are preferred for better twisting.

## Material
*   Plant fibers (nettle, dogbane, cattail, grass).
*   Animal fibers (sinew).

## System
**Fundamental Principle:** Twist creates friction, and friction creates strength. A properly twisted rope is many times stronger than the sum of its individual fibers because the fibers can no longer slide past each other under load.

## Technology
### Rope Construction Methods
*   **Method 1: Two-Ply Reverse Wrap (Recommended):** Two bundles of fiber are individually twisted in one direction, then wrapped around each other in the opposite direction. This produces a balanced, self-locking cord.
*   **Method 2: Thigh Rolling (Leg Rolling):** A faster variation where two bundles are laid parallel on the thigh, twisted simultaneously (clockwise), and then wrapped counterclockwise.
*   **Method 3: Three-Ply Braiding:** Produces flat, strong cordage based on the interlocking pattern of three strands, not primarily twist.
*   **Method 4: Four-Strand Round Braiding:** Produces a round, symmetrical cord, stronger than three-ply braiding.
*   **Method 5: Laying Up (Making Rope from Cord):** To make thick rope, several lengths of two-ply cord (strands) are twisted together using the reverse-wrap principle, where each strand is an entire cord.

### Splicing (Joining Fiber Lengths)
*   **Stagger Rule:** The most important splicing principle is to never splice both plies at the same point. Splices must be staggered by at least 3-4 inches.
*   **Splice Procedure (Two-Ply Twist):** When one ply is running short, lay a new fiber bundle alongside the short end, overlapping by the full remaining length. Continue twisting to incorporate both the old tail and the new fiber. After 2-3 inches of twist, the splice is secure. Do not splice the other ply until at least 3 inches past this splice point.

## Tools
*   **Spinning Hook:** A hooked stick or bent nail mounted in a handle, used to hook a fiber bundle and spin the handle to apply twist, freeing one hand for feeding fiber.
*   **Rope Winder (Twisting Frame):** A Y-shaped branch with two hooks, used to attach fiber bundles, twist by rotating the branch, and allow the reverse wrap to happen mechanically.
*   **Weighted Spindle:** A straight stick with a weight near the bottom, used to set the spindle spinning so that gravity and rotation apply consistent twist.
*   **Fixed Anchor:** Any solid attachment point (tree branch, stake, heavy rock with a notch) used to anchor one end of the work and free both hands for twisting.

## Process
### Rope Construction Steps (Key Methods)
*   **Two-Ply Reverse Wrap:** Twist two bundles in one direction, wrap them in the opposite direction to lock the twist.
*   **Thigh Rolling:** Lay two bundles parallel on the thigh, roll the palm forward in one stroke to twist both plies, and then wrap them counterclockwise.
*   **Three-Ply Braiding:** Gather three bundles, anchor one end, cross the outer strand over the center, then cross the outer strand over the new center, repeating the alternating pattern while maintaining consistent tension.
*   **Laying Up:** Make several two-ply cords (strands). Twist these three strands together using the reverse-wrap principle.
*   **Splicing:** Identify a short end, lay a new fiber bundle on, overlap by the remaining length, incorporate the new fiber into the existing twist, and ensure the second ply is staggered by 3+ inches.

## Diameter and Strength Guide
Approximate breaking strengths for well-made two-ply reverse-wrap cordage:

| Cord Diameter |
| :--- | 
| 1/16” (1.5mm) | 15-25 lbs (Plant Fiber), 5-10 lbs (Grass), 40-60 lbs (Sinew) |
| 1/8” (3mm) | 40-70 lbs (Plant Fiber), 15-30 lbs (Plant Fiber), 80-150 lbs (Sinew) |
| 1/4” (6mm) | 100-200 lbs (Plant Fiber), 40-80 lbs (Plant Fiber), 200-400 lbs (Sinew) |
| 1/2” (12mm) | 300-500 lbs (Plant Fiber), 100-200 lbs (Plant Fiber), 500+ lbs (Sinew) |

## Common Mistakes
1. **Inconsistent ply thickness:** One ply being thicker than the other leads to the thin ply breaking first.
2. **Insufficient twist:** Rope feels soft and loose; it will fail because fibers can still slide.
3. **Over-twist:** Rope kinks, tangles, and develops hockles when too much twist is applied.
4. **Splicing both plies at the same point:** Creates a guaranteed weak spot.
5. **Working with bone-dry fiber:** Fibers twist better when slightly damp; do not soak them.

## Notes
*   All rope strength fundamentally derives from twist creating friction between fibers.
*   The two-ply reverse wrap is the recommended foundational technique.
*   Thigh rolling is the fastest hand method, roughly twice the speed of finger twisting.
*   A properly made nettle/dogbane cord (1/4 inch diameter) holds 100–200 lbs; sinew of the same diameter holds 200–400 lbs.
*   The key takeaway for strength is that fibers lock together when twisted, preventing slippage under load.
*   Carry cordage because replacing it from natural materials requires significant time, energy and skill.
