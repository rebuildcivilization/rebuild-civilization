# Joining Knots

## Objective
To connect two separate ropes into one using joining knots (bends). In survival, this is critical because rope is often never long enough, and the correct join ensures a secure connection to prevent catastrophic separation.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Ropes of different diameters and materials.
*   Cordage.
*   Flat materials (webbing, fabric strips, bark strips, rawhide strips) for water knots.
*   Various stiffness levels for material compatibility.

## Material
*   Ropes (paracord, vine cordage, rawhide, electrical wire).
*   Flat materials (webbing, fabric strips, bark strips, rawhide strips).

## System
The system relies on selecting the correct joining knot based on the materials being joined and the required strength or ease of untying.

## Technology
### Joining Knots (Bends)
Knots are the mechanism that creates an interlocking friction grip between two ropes.

### Primary Knots
1.  **Sheet Bend:** The standard knot for joining two ropes, especially those with different diameters or materials. Functions by creating an interlocking friction grip where the thicker rope forms a bight, and the thinner rope threads through and wraps around it.
2.  **Fisherman’s Knot (Angler’s Knot):** Used to join two ropes of similar diameter by tying each rope around the other with an overhand knot, allowing the overhand knots to slide together and lock under load.
3.  **Double Fisherman’s Knot:** The strongest rope-to-rope join available, replacing each overhand knot with a double overhand knot. Ideal for permanent connections where the join must not fail and untying is not required.
4.  **Water Knot (Ring Bend):** Used to join flat materials where round-rope knots would slip. Involves threading the other strip's working end into the first strip's overhand knot, retracing the path.
5.  **Zeppelin Bend:** The ideal all-rounder. It holds securely, unties easily after loading, works well with similar diameter ropes, and never jams. It is symmetrical and easy to verify.

## Tools
*   Hands for tying and testing.
*   Weights or progressive loads (5 kg, 10 kg, 20 kg) for testing.

## Process
### Tying Processes
**Sheet Bend:**
1.  Form a bight (U-shape) in the end of the thicker rope.
2.  Pass the thinner rope up through the bight from underneath.
3.  Route the thinner rope around the back of the bight, wrapping around both legs of the U.
4.  Tuck the thinner rope under its own standing part, but over the bight. The working ends must exit on the same side.
5.  Pull all four strands to tighten.

**Fisherman’s Knot:**
1.  Lay two ropes parallel, overlapping by about 30 cm (12 inches), working ends pointing in opposite directions.
2.  Tie an overhand knot in Rope A’s working end around Rope B’s standing part (do not tighten fully).
3.  Tie an overhand knot in Rope B’s working end around Rope A’s standing part (do not tighten fully).
4.  Pull both standing parts simultaneously to lock the overhand knots.
5.  Tighten each overhand knot individually, then snug the standing parts.

**Water Knot (Ring Bend):**
1.  Tie a loose overhand knot in the end of one strip.
2.  Thread the other strip’s working end into the overhand knot, retracing the knot’s path exactly but in the opposite direction.
3.  Pull all four strands to tighten.

**Zeppelin Bend:**
1.  Form a loop in each rope end, laying them on top of each other so loops overlap, working ends pointing in opposite directions.
2.  Pass Rope A’s working end through both loops from the top.
3.  Pass Rope B’s working end through both loops from the bottom (opposite direction of Rope A).
4.  Pull both standing parts to tighten.

### Choosing the Right Join
| Scenario | Best Knot | Rationale |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Two ropes, different diameters | Double sheet bend | Designed for diameter mismatch |
| Two ropes, same diameter, temporary | Sheet bend or Zeppelin | Easy to untie |
| Two ropes, same diameter, permanent | Double fisherman’s | Strongest possible join |
| Fishing line to fishing line | Fisherman’s knot | Compact, slides through guides |
| Flat webbing to flat webbing | Water knot | Only option for flat material |
| Wet or slippery ropes | Double sheet bend + stopper knots | Maximum grip on low-friction materials |
| Plant cordage to plant cordage | Sheet bend | Rough fibers grip well |
| Cordage to scavenged wire | Double sheet bend | Handles stiffness mismatch |

### Testing Processes
1.  **Dress the Knot:** Tie the knot and dress it neatly (no crossing or overlapping strands within the knot body).
2.  **Initial Pull Test:** Pull firmly on both standing parts with hands or body weight. The knot must not slip, deform, or move.
3.  **Tail Check:** Verify that both tails are at least 10-15 cm (4-6 inches) long.
4.  **Load Testing:** For critical applications, hang a progressively heavier load (start with 5 kg, then 10 kg, then 20 kg). Watch for slippage.
5.  **Post-Load Inspection:** Inspect the knot again to see if it has tightened further or changed profile, which may indicate approaching failure.

## Notes
*   **Critical Detail (Sheet Bend):** Both working ends (tails) must exit on the *same side* of the knot; otherwise, it is a left-handed sheet bend, which is weaker.
*   **Common Mistakes:**
    *   Using a square knot (reef knot) to join two ropes under load; it will capsize and separate.
    *   Using a fisherman’s knot for large diameter mismatches; a sheet bend is required.
    *   Leaving short tails (under 10 cm) is a failure risk.
    *   Failing to dress the knot results in a weaker connection.
    *   Failing to test the knot renders it untrustworthy.
*   **Key Takeaways:** The sheet bend is the default rope join; the double fisherman’s knot is the strongest permanent join; the water knot is mandatory for flat materials; and always leave long tails and test before loading.
