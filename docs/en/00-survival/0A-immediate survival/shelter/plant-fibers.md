# Bark, Leaf, Grass Fibers

## Objective
To extract usable fibers from raw plant material and prepare them for twisting into cordage. Each fiber type — bark, leaf, and grass — requires different extraction and processing techniques.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
Raw plant material (Bark, Leaf, Grass) and water for retting/soaking.

## Material
*   Bark Fibers (Bast Fibers)
*   Leaf Fibers (Yucca, Stinging Nettle, Cattail)
*   Grass Fibers

## System
The system involves extracting fibers from the plant material and preparing them for twisting into cordage. The process requires careful management of moisture, temperature, and mechanical action (pounding, scraping, twisting).

## Technology
*   **Bark Processing:** Fresh use, Retting (soaking to separate fibers).
*   **Leaf Processing:** Pounding and scraping to crush tissue and separate fibers.
*   **Grass Processing:** Drying, softening, and twisting.
*   **Cordage Technique:** Reverse-wrap technique.
*   **Improvement Techniques:** Three-ply construction, braiding, mixing with stronger fiber, waxing/greasing.

## Tools
*   Knife
*   Smooth rock or mallet
*   Dull blade or bone scraper
*   Water
*   Weights (for tension/submerging)
*   Thick bundles (for grass)

## Process
### Bark Fibers (Bast Fibers)
**Method 1: Fresh Use (Quick and Dirty)**
1. Peel the inner bark into strips as narrow as the intended cordage diameter (5-10 mm for general-purpose rope).
2. Twist into two-ply cordage immediately using the reverse-wrap technique.
3. Let the finished cordage dry under tension.
**Method 2: Retting (Produces Superior Fiber)**
1. Bundle bark strips loosely and submerge them completely in still or slow-moving water, weighting them down.
2. Wait for retting (3-14 days depending on water temperature: Warm (3-5 days), Moderate (7-10 days), Cold (10-14+ days)).
3. Check daily. Retting is complete when soft tissue feels slimy and separates easily.
4. Remove bark and rinse thoroughly; scrape away rotted tissue.
5. Dry the fibers, then re-wet before use.

**Fiber Quality by Tree Species**
| Species | Fiber Length | Fiber Strength | Ease of Processing | Retting Required? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Basswood / Linden | 30-90 cm | High | Easy — peels cleanly | Recommended but not essential |
| Willow | 30-60 cm | High | Moderate | Not essential — usable fresh |
| Western red cedar | 60-120 cm | High | Easy | Minimal — fibers separate naturally |
| Mulberry | 60-120 cm | Very high | Moderate | Recommended for best results |
| Elm | 30-60 cm | Moderate | Difficult — bark is tough | Yes — makes a big difference |
| Tulip poplar | 30-60 cm | Moderate | Easy | Recommended |
| Hickory | 20-40 cm | High | Difficult — bark is very tough | Yes |

### Leaf Fibers
**Yucca**
1. Harvest mature leaves from the outer ring of the plant; cut at the base.
2. Pound the leaves repeatedly with a smooth rock or mallet to crush fleshy tissue without cutting fibers.
3. Scrape away the pulp with a dull blade or bone scraper, pulling toward you.
4. Rinse the fibers in water.
5. Dry the fibers.

**Stinging Nettle**
1. Harvest dead, dry stalks when late fall or early winter.
2. Split the stalk lengthwise and remove the woody core (pith).
3. Scrape or peel the outer bark layer; the fibers are between the bark and the pith.
4. Bundle and store dry; re-wet before twisting.

**Cattail**
1. Harvest the longest, most mature leaves (green or dry).
2. Dry them flat, then re-wet when ready to twist.
3. Twist into cordage using the reverse-wrap method, using at least three leaves per ply.

**Dogbane (Indian Hemp)**
1. Harvest dead stalks when dry and brittle.
2. Crush the stalk by rolling it between palms or stepping on it to shatter the woody core.
3. Open the stalk and shake or pick out broken core pieces.
4. Peel the bark away in long strips.
5. Separate individual fibers by pulling the bark strips apart lengthwise.
6. Bundle and store; re-wet before twisting.

### Grass Fibers
**Processing Grass for Cordage**
1. Gather the longest, thickest grass; dried grass is stronger.
2. If using green grass, dry it first (1-2 days in the sun).
3. Soften dried grass by soaking in water for 15-30 minutes.
4. Twist into cordage using the reverse-wrap technique, using thick bundles (at least 8-10 grass blades per ply), adding new blades by overlapping them 5-10 cm.
5. Dry the finished cordage under tension.

**Improving Grass Cordage**
*   **Three-ply construction:** Twist three bundles instead of two (40-60% stronger).
*   **Braiding:** Use a flat braid of 3+ bundles for more abrasion resistance.
*   **Mixing:** Add strands of bark or yucca fiber to each ply to dramatically increase strength.
*   **Waterproofing:** Rub rendered fat or pine resin into finished cordage.

## Storage and Shelf Life
**Storage Rules**
*   Dry thoroughly before storing to prevent mold/rot.
*   Store off the ground to prevent moisture wicking.
*   Keep out of direct sun to prevent UV degradation.
*   Bundle loosely to prevent moisture trapping.
*   Check periodically for mold or insect damage.

**Shelf Life When Stored Dry**
| Fiber Type | Shelf Life |
| :--- | :--- |
| Bark fibers | 2-5 years |
| Yucca/agave | 3-5 years |
| Nettle/dogbane | 2-3 years |
| Grass | 6-12 months (degrades fastest) |
| Cattail | 1-2 years |

## Notes
*   **Bark Warning:** Fresh bark cordage shrinks as it dries and may loosen lashings; retighten critical structural bindings after 24 hours or use re-wetted bark.
*   **Retting Warning:** Over-retting destroys fibers; check daily and pull when soft tissue separates but fibers remain strong and white/cream colored.
*   **Grass Strength:** Green grass loses up to 70% of strength at critical joints when dried; thick bundles and mixing improve strength significantly.
*   **Key Takeaway:** Bark fibers are the most versatile, but retting dramatically improves them; dead nettle and dogbane stalks yield fiber comparable to commercial hemp with minimal processing.
*   **Storage Key:** Proper drying and bundling are crucial; moisture is the primary enemy of natural fiber storage.
