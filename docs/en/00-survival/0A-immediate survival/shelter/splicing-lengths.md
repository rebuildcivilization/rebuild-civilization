# Splicing Lengths | Survival Wiki

## Objective
To join fiber bundles seamlessly to turn short plant fibers into a rope of unlimited length. A good splice is invisible and as strong as the rope itself.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- Short plant fibers (e.g., nettle stalks, inner bark strips, grass blades), which have inherent length limits.

## Material
- Plant fibers
- Cordage
- Rope

## System
- Splicing is a friction joint, not a knot.
- The mechanism relies on laying new fibers alongside the thinning end of an existing ply and twisting them together so friction locks them into a single unit.
- The overlap zone is where old and new fibers share the same ply, and the splice strength depends on overlap length, twist pressure, and fiber taper.

## Technology
- **Standard Splice:** Involves adding one bundle of new fiber to an existing ply.
- **Three-Fiber Splice:** Used for maximum strength in heavy cordage, where the new fiber is split into two sub-bundles (one at 2/3 thickness and one at 1/3 thickness), staggered within the overlap zone.

## Tools
- A small marker (tiny knot of contrasting fiber) to visually track stagger.

## Process
### Critical Measurements (Overlap Lengths)
| Cordage Diameter | Minimum Overlap Length | Maximum Overlap Length |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Under 2 mm (fine twine) | 5 cm (2 in) | 8 cm (3 in) |
| 2-5 mm (light cord) | 8 cm (3 in) | 12 cm (5 in) |
| 5-10 mm (utility rope) | 12 cm (5 in) | 18 cm (7 in) |
| Over 10 mm (heavy rope) | 18 cm (7 in) | 25 cm (10 in) |

### Stagger Maintenance
- **Rule:** Never splice both plies at the same location.
- **Minimum Stagger:** The minimum stagger distance between splices on Ply A and Ply B is three times the overlap length.
- **Practical Stagger Method:** Start with an offset fiber length (e.g., 5 cm offset). When splicing new fibers, choose a bundle length that runs out at a different point than the current fibers.

### Step-by-Step Splicing Technique
**Preparing the New Fibers**
1. Select fibers of approximately the same thickness as the ply being joined.
2. Taper the butt ends of the new fibers, fanning out the bundle and cutting or tearing the ends at an angle to ensure gradual thinning.
3. Dampen the new fibers slightly to improve grip during twisting.

**Executing the Splice**
4. Continue twisting until the short ply has about 5 to 8 cm of fiber remaining.
5. Lay the tapered end of the new fiber bundle alongside the remaining old fiber, with the taper pointing toward the finished rope.
6. Pinch the old and new fibers together firmly at the overlap point.
7. Twist the combined fibers with slightly more pressure for the first 3 to 4 twists to establish the initial grip.
8. Continue the normal twist-and-wrap rhythm through the entire overlap zone.
9. After the overlap zone, check that the transition is smooth; there should be a slight thickening, but no lumps or loose ends.

**Finishing the Splice**
10. If old fiber ends poke out, lay them along the ply and twist them in during subsequent cycles instead of cutting them flush.
11. Apply a light smear of moisture (saliva works in the field) over the splice zone to swell the fibers and increase friction.

### Splice Strength Testing
- **Field Test:** Isolate the section 15 cm on either side of the splice and pull steadily, watching for fibers pulling out, thinning at the splice, or visible gaps.
- **Serviceable Threshold:** A firm two-handed pull (roughly 10 to 15 kg / 22 to 33 lbs for pencil-thickness cord).
- **Critical Application Test:** Test to at least 50% of the rope’s expected working load, or the full working load for critical applications.

### Troubleshooting
- **Problem:** New fibers pull out under moderate load.
  - **Cause:** Insufficient overlap length or not enough twist in the overlap zone.
  - **Solution:** Re-splice with at least 50% more overlap and add 1 to 2 extra twists per wrap cycle in the overlap zone.
- **Problem:** Rope is lumpy and stiff at the splice.
  - **Cause:** New fiber bundle is too thick, or the old fibers were not tapered.
  - **Solution:** Use a thinner bundle and ensure both old and new fiber ends are tapered.

## Notes
- A splice is fundamentally a friction joint, and its strength is determined by overlap length, twist pressure, and fiber taper.
- The key to preventing failure is ensuring the overlap zone has sufficient friction to hold the new fibers under load.
- Cosmetic variations (color or texture change) do not affect strength, but uniformity is preferred for certain applications like net-making.
- When tracking stagger, if the count is lost, splice one ply and then twist at least 30 cm of rope before checking the other ply.
