# Ridgepole Selection | Survival Wiki

## Objective
To select the single most important structural element of any debris shelter, the ridgepole, ensuring the entire structure successfully withstands the night.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- The ridgepole must pass the strength test before building.
- The ridgepole must pass the integrity tests (snap and press tests).
- The correct pole must be sourced (fallen trees are the first choice).
- The high end of the ridgepole must be secured with a solid support at approximately waist height (90-110 cm above ground).

## Material
**Best Choices and Rationale:**
- Oak (dead but dry): Extremely strong, resists rot.
- Maple: Hard, straight-grained.
- Ash: Straight, strong, light for its strength.
- Birch: Straight, good strength.
- Hickory: Nearly indestructible.
- Conifer (recently fallen): Straight, abundant.

**Worst Choices and Rationale:**
- Willow: Bends, rarely straight.
- Poplar/Aspen: Rots fast, soft wood.
- Elderberry: Hollow centers, brittle.
- Pine (dead, bark gone): Often rotten inside.
- Alder: Soft, breaks under load.

**Optimal Wood Condition:** Recently fallen hardwood trees that are dry enough to be lighter than green wood but not yet rotten, ideally seasoned (bark still attached, wood still hard).

## System
Debris Shelter (The structure reliant on the ridgepole for structural integrity).

## Technology
The structural integrity of the debris shelter depends entirely on the ridgepole's ability to carry the entire weight of the shelter (50-100+ kg of wet debris).

## Tools
- Thumbnail (for the press test).
- Measurement tools (for body length and height).

## Process
### Selection Criteria
1. **Length:** Should be approximately 1.5 times the body length (2.5 to 3 meters for most adults) to allow for body space and support.
2. **Diameter:** Aim for 8-15 cm (wrist-thick to slightly thicker) as the ideal balance between strength and weight.
3. **Straightness:** Must not have a pronounced curve or S-bend that creates sideways forces. A slight bow is acceptable if oriented correctly.
4. **Integrity (Testing):**
    - **Snap Test:** Lift one end about 30 cm off the ground and drop it. A sound pole makes a solid thud or crack.
    - **Press Test:** Push a thumbnail into the wood. If it sinks in easily, the wood is soft and rotten.

### Sourcing
1. **Fallen trees:** First and best source (blowdowns).
2. **Dead standing trees:** Use only if they pass the snap and press tests.
3. **Live saplings:** Last resort; green wood is heavy but strong.
4. **Riverbanks/Shorelines:** Check for rot in waterlogged wood.

### Preparation
- **Strip protruding branches:** Snap or twist off side branches so ribs can lie flat against the pole. Leave small stubs (2-3 cm) to prevent ribs from sliding down.
- **Trim the ends:** Keep a fork if possible to wedge onto a support.

### Final Validation
- **Load Test:** Prop the pole in its final position and press down with both hands (hard). Apply full body weight if possible. If it flexes alarmingly or cracks, reject the pole.

## Notes
- **Key Takeaways:**
    - The ridgepole must support 50-100+ kg of wet debris and must be tested with full body weight.
    - Ideal specifications: 8-15 cm diameter, 1.5x body length, straight, and hard.
    - Recently fallen hardwood is the preferred material.
    - The risk of failure increases by 30-50% if the pole is wet from rain; always add a safety margin.
    - Choosing the pole saves 3 hours of rebuilding time after a collapse.
    - **Common Mistakes:** Too thin (snaps), rotten inside (catastrophic failure), too long (wastes resources), setting too high (impossible to heat), and no load test.
- **Support Options:** Tree fork, stump, bipod (X-shape), or a large rock, all used to secure the high end at waist height.
