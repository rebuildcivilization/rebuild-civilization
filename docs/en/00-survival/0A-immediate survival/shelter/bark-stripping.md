# Inner Bark Preparation Guide

## Objective
To strip the inner bast layer from Tier 1 cordage trees to obtain fibers suitable for cordage, food, medicine, tinder, and construction material, while avoiding the killing of the living tree.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Understanding of bark anatomy (Outer bark, Inner bark (bast), Cambium, Sapwood).
*   Knowledge of seasonal timing, particularly the ideal window of late spring through early summer when sap flow is active.
*   Understanding of sustainable harvesting rules to prevent girdling trees.

## Material
*   Tier 1 Cordage Trees: Basswood (American linden), European linden (lime), Willow (various species), Western red cedar, Mulberry.
*   Inner Bark (Bast Layer): The living layer containing long, strong fibers.
*   Sapwood: The living wood itself, which must not be cut into during stripping.
*   Outer Bark: The dead, rough exterior, useful for tinder and containers.

## System
The system relies on the extraction of the inner bark, which is one of the most important raw materials in a post-collapse world, providing cordage fiber, food (cambium), medicine, tinder, and construction material.

## Technology
*   **Peeling Mechanics:** The inner bark separates cleanly when the tree is actively growing (late spring/summer) because the sap flow causes the bark to 'peel like butter'.
*   **Retting Process:** Soaking inner bark strips in water for 3–14 days to rot away soft tissue between fibers, producing finer, stronger, more flexible fibers.

## Tools
*   Knife or sharp stone flake.
*   Flat stick or bone wedge for prying (optional but helpful).
*   Container or flat surface for collecting bark strips.
*   Water source nearby (for soaking if bark is dry).

## Process
### Inner Bark Stripping Steps (From a Living Tree)
1.  **Select Tree:** Confirm the species and choose a tree at least 15 cm (6 inches) in diameter.
2.  **Choose Location:** Select a section of trunk with smooth, undamaged bark, avoiding branches, knots, or wounds.
3.  **Make Cuts:** Make two vertical cuts through the outer and inner bark, 10–20 cm apart, as tall as comfortable, cutting just deep enough to reach the sapwood.
4.  **Connect Cuts:** Make a horizontal cut connecting the tops of the two vertical cuts.
5.  **Peel:** Pry the bark strip away from the wood, starting at the top horizontal cut and peeling downward. The inner bark should separate cleanly.
6.  **Final Cut:** Peel the strip down to the bottom and cut it free with a horizontal cut.
7.  **Separate Layers:** Separate the outer bark from the inner bark by peeling; the inner bark is softer and often lighter in color.

### Processing After Stripping
*   **For Immediate Cordage:** Split inner bark into strips 5–15 mm wide and twist directly into rope.
*   **For Superior Fiber (Retting):** Soak the strips in water for 3–14 days.
*   **For Strips and Lashing:** Wide strips (2–5 cm) can be used directly as flat lashing material, preferably wet.
*   **For Storage:** Coil dried strips loosely and store off the ground in a dry location. Re-wet before use. Proper storage allows fiber to last 2–5 years.

## Technology (Anatomy)
| Layer | Description | Utility | Key Takeaway |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Outer bark | Dead, rough exterior. | Tinder, containers, shingles (especially birch). | Useless for cordage. |
| Inner bark (Bast) | Living layer transporting nutrients. | Cordage fiber, food (cambium), medicine, tinder, construction. | The target layer. |
| Cambium | Thin, slippery, often greenish layer between inner bark and wood. | Edible (high in sugars and starch). | Removing it damages the tree's growth ability. |
| Sapwood | The living wood itself. | Provides structural strength. | Do not cut into this when stripping bark. |

## Tools (Tree Selection)
### Tier 1: Best Cordage Trees
| Tree | Identifying Features | Inner Bark Quality | Region |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Basswood (American linden) | Heart-shaped leaves, smooth grey bark on young trees | Excellent — long, strong, easy to peel | Eastern N. America |
| European linden (lime) | Similar to basswood | Excellent | Europe |
| Willow (various species) | Narrow leaves, grows near water, flexible branches | Very good — flexible fibers | Worldwide |
| Western red cedar | Scale-like leaves, fibrous reddish bark | Very good — long fibers, naturally rot-resistant | Pacific Northwest |
| Mulberry | Lobed or unlobed leaves, milky sap | Excellent — among the strongest | Temperate worldwide |

### Tier 2: Good Alternatives
| Tree | Inner Bark Quality | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Elm (especially slippery elm) | Good | Bark is tough to peel but fiber is strong. |
| Tulip poplar | Good | Large trees, easy to find in eastern N. America. |
| Cottonwood | Moderate-good | Common near rivers. |
| Hickory | Good | Bark is very difficult to remove but fiber is excellent. |
| Paper birch | Moderate | Outer bark is more useful (containers, tinder) than inner bark fiber. |
| Aspen | Moderate | Thin inner bark, short fibers. |

### Trees to Avoid for Cordage
*   **Pine, spruce, fir (conifers in general):** Resinous, short fibers, poor cordage. |
*   **Oak:** Inner bark contains heavy tannins; fibers are short and brittle. |
*   **Maple:** Inner bark is thin with weak fibers. |
*   **Beech:** Very thin inner bark, not worth the effort. |

## Process (Seasonal Timing and Sustainability)
### Seasonal Timing
*   **Ideal Window:** Late spring through early summer when sap is flowing and the inner bark separates cleanly from the sapwood.
*   **Early Spring (Sap Rising):** Excellent stripping ease ('peels like butter').
*   **Late Spring to Early Summer:** Excellent quality (fibers at peak).
*   **Mid to Late Summer:** Good quality (sap slowing).
*   **Fall:** Difficult (bark tightens); requires more effort or soaking.
*   **Winter:** Very difficult (bark frozen or bonded tightly); avoid unless desperate and soak logs first.

### Sustainable Harvesting Rules
1.  **Never girdle a living tree:** Remove bark from no more than one-third of the circumference to allow the tree to heal.
2.  **Prefer Fallen Trees/Dead Limbs:** Freshly fallen trees (within days of falling) are best due to moist, peelable bark.
3.  **Use Branches/Saplings:** Strip a 5 cm diameter branch for practice material without threatening a mature tree.
4.  **Spread Impact:** If stripping a living tree, distribute the effort across multiple trees.

## Other Uses for Inner Bark
*   **Emergency Food (Cambium):** Scrape the thin cambium layer off the wood and eat raw or cook (Species: Pine, birch, willow, basswood, elm).
*   **Tinder:** Shred dry inner bark into fine fibers (Species: Cedar, basswood, cottonwood).
*   **Bandage Material:** Flatten fresh strips to use as wound wrapping (Species: Basswood, willow [salicin content], slippery elm [sore throat]).
*   **Container/Basket:** Weave wide strips into baskets or boxes (Species: Birch [outer bark], cedar, basswood).
*   **Insulation:** Shred and stuff into clothing or bedding (Species: Cedar, basswood).
*   **Tea/Medicine:** Steep inner bark in hot water (Species: Willow [pain relief], slippery elm [sore throat], pine [vitamin C]).

## Troubleshooting
| Problem | Cause | Solution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Bark will not peel from the wood | Wrong season, bark is dry, or wrong tree species | Soak in water 24–48 hours, or try a different tree. |
| Bark tears into short pieces | Peeling too fast, or fibers are short (wrong species) | Slow down; peel at a shallower angle. |
| Inner bark is too thin to use | Tree is too young or wrong species | Use larger, older trees or switch to a Tier 1 species. |
| Fibers break when twisted | Bark was over-dried or over-retted | Soak thoroughly before twisting; fibers need moisture to flex. |
| Bark strip includes sapwood | Cut too deep into the trunk | Use lighter pressure; stop when feeling hard wood. |
| Tree sap makes bark sticky | Conifer species or fresh wound | Avoid conifers for cordage; let sap dry and peel it off. |

## Key Takeaways
*   The inner bark (bast layer) is the target; separate it by peeling after cutting to the wood.
*   Late spring through early summer is the best window for clean stripping.
*   Never girdle a tree; prioritize fallen trees or branches.
*   Basswood, willow, cedar, and mulberry are the best cordage choices.
*   Use retting (soaking) for superior, stronger cordage fiber.
*   Always re-wet dried bark before twisting to prevent breakage.


Notes
- The primary danger in stripping bark is girdling the tree, which kills it because the inner bark is the nutrient highway. 
- The ideal season is late spring through early summer because sap flow ensures the inner bark separates cleanly from the sapwood, allowing for the best quality.
- For cordage, fresh inner bark can be split into 5–15 mm strips and twisted immediately. For superior fiber, retting (soaking) for 3–14 days is recommended.
- The inner bark provides more than just cordage; it is crucial for food (cambium), medicine, tinder, and insulation.
