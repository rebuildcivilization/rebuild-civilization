# Lashing Techniques | Survival Wiki

## Objective
To lash two poles together rigidly.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   **Cordage Calculation:** Measure the combined diameter of the poles being joined and multiply by 25–30 to determine the minimum length of cordage needed.
*   **Tension:** Every turn of cordage must be pulled tight before adding the next, using full hand strength.
*   **Cordage Selection:** Use the right cordage type based on the required load capacity.

## Material
*   **Poles:** Varying lengths (e.g., 5 cm, 8 cm, 150 cm, 120 cm), 5-8 cm diameter.
*   **Cordage Types and Capacities:**
    *   Plant fiber (2-ply): 3-5 mm diameter, 10-20 kg capacity.
    *   Braided plant fiber: 6-10 mm diameter, 30-60 kg capacity.
    *   Rawhide strips: 5-10 mm diameter, 50-80 kg capacity.
    *   Paracord (scavenged): 4 mm diameter, 250 kg capacity.
    *   Vine (grape, wisteria): 5-15 mm diameter, 15-40 kg capacity.

## System
Lashing is the art of binding poles together with cordage to create rigid structures. It is the fundamental construction technique for everything from shelters and bridges to furniture and watchtowers when nails, screws, and bolts no longer exist. Lashing works because it distributes force across multiple wraps of cordage rather than concentrating stress at a single point.

## Technology
*   **Core Principles:**
    1.  **Start and Finish with a Clove Hitch:** Every lash begins and ends with a clove hitch around one pole to anchor the cordage securely.
    2.  **Constant Tension:** Every turn must be pulled tight to prevent loose lashing, which fails under load.
    3.  **Frapping Turns:** Wraps go around the poles, and frapping turns go between the poles, perpendicular to the wrapping turns, to cinch everything tight.
    4.  **Material Advantage:** A well-lashed joint in green wood can outperform a nailed joint because the cordage flexes with the wood instead of splitting it.

## Tools
*   Hand strength (to pull wraps tight).
*   Measuring tools (implied for cordage calculation).

## Process
### General Lashing Procedure
1.  Start and finish with a clove hitch on one pole.
2.  Make wrapping turns around both poles.
3.  Add frapping turns (wraps between the poles, perpendicular to wrapping turns) to cinch everything tight.
4.  Ensure every wrap is pulled tight with full hand strength.

### Essential Lashing Types
**1. Square Lashing:**
*   **Definition:** Joins two poles at a right angle (90 degrees).
*   **Use for:** Shelter frames, tables, ladders, racks, fences.

**2. Diagonal Lashing:**
*   **Definition:** Joins two poles that cross at an angle other than 90 degrees.
*   **Steps:**
    1.  Begin with a timber hitch around both poles at their crossing point, pulling them tightly.
    2.  Wrap three turns around one pair of opposite angles.
    3.  Wrap three turns around the other pair of opposite angles.
    4.  Make two frapping turns between the poles, perpendicular to the wrapping turns.
    5.  Finish with a clove hitch on the most convenient pole.
*   **Use for:** Diagonal braces on walls, X-frame supports, angled roof members.

**3. Shear Lashing:**
*   **Definition:** Binds two parallel poles side by side with a gap between them, creating a simple hinge that opens into an A-frame or shear legs.
*   **Steps:**
    1.  Lay two poles side by side. Tie a clove hitch around one pole near the top.
    2.  Wrap the cordage around both poles together, making 8-10 loose wrapping turns.
    3.  Make frapping turns between the two poles, wrapping 3-4 times to create a pivot point.
    4.  Finish with a clove hitch on the second pole.
    5.  Spread the legs apart.
*   **Use for:** A-frame shelters, shear legs for hoisting, bipod supports.

**4. Tripod Lashing:**
*   **Definition:** Binds three poles together to create a self-standing three-legged support.

**5. Round Lashing:**
*   **Definition:** Extends the length of a pole by binding two poles end-to-end with overlapping sections.
*   **Steps:**
    1.  Overlap the two poles by at least 30 cm (12 inches).
    2.  Tie a clove hitch near one end of the overlap.
    3.  Wrap tightly around both poles for the full length of the overlap, keeping wraps parallel and snug (use at least 15-20 wraps).
    4.  Finish with a clove hitch at the other end of the overlap.
*   **Use for:** Extending ridgepoles, building long beams, creating flagpoles.

### Cordage Warning
*   **Wet Cordage:** Lash while cordage is slightly damp for the tightest joint, as natural cordage shrinks as it dries, tightening the joint.
*   **Dry Cordage Warning:** If dry cordage gets soaked later, it will swell and then shrink unevenly, loosening the joint.
*   **Strength Limit:** A round-lashed extension is never as strong as a single pole; reinforce by lashing a third splint pole.

### Troubleshooting
| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Joint rotates or wobbles | Insufficient frapping turns | Add more frapping turns between poles |
| Wraps slide along pole | Poles too smooth or polished | Carve shallow notches where poles cross |
| Cordage breaks during lashing | Cordage too thin or weak for the load | Double the cordage or use thicker rope |
| Joint loosens over days | Cordage dried out and shrank unevenly | Re-wet and re-tighten, or relash |
| Poles split at the joint | Poles too dry and brittle | Use green (freshly cut) poles for heavy loads |

### Building a Simple Lashed Frame
*   **Materials:** 4 vertical poles (150 cm long, 5-8 cm diameter), 4 horizontal poles (120 cm long, 5-8 cm diameter), 2 diagonal braces (180 cm long, 3-5 cm diameter), 20 m of cordage.
*   **Assembly:**
    1.  Square-lash the four horizontal poles to the four vertical poles, forming a rectangle (start at top corners).
    2.  Stand the frame upright (it will wobble).
    3.  Diagonal-lash one brace from the top-left corner to the bottom-right corner.
    4.  Diagonal-lash the second brace from the top-right corner to the bottom-left corner (frame is now rigid).

## Notes
*   Every lashing must start and end with a clove hitch (non-negotiable).
*   Frapping turns are essential for rigidity; never skip them.
*   The principle that triangles are rigid while rectangles wobble is the foundation of structural engineering.
*   For the tightest possible joint, lash while cordage is slightly damp.
*   When calculating cordage, always cut more than the minimum required amount.
