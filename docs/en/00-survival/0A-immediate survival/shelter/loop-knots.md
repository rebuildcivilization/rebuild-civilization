# Loop Knots

## Objective
Loop knots create a fixed or adjustable eye in the rope. They are the foundation of rescue systems, anchor points, and load-bearing connections.

## Dependencies and prerequisites

*   Skill in tying the knots.
*   Knowledge of knot properties and selection guides.
*   Material (rope or cordage).

## Material

*   Rope
*   Cordage
*   Paracord
*   Natural fiber cordage
*   Shoelaces
*   Vines

## System

The system involves converting a passive length of cordage into an active tool. Loop knots serve as essential elements for rescue systems, anchor points, and load-bearing connections.

## Technology

*   **Rope Strength Retention:** The figure-eight retains about 75-80% of rope strength, while the bowline retains about 60-65%. Every knot weakens the rope at the knot point.

## Process

### Essential Loop Knots

**1. The Bowline**
*   **Purpose:** Creates a fixed loop that will not slip or tighten under load, yet unties easily after being loaded.
*   **Step-by-step:**
    1.  Hold the rope with the working end (free end) in your dominant hand. The standing part runs to your other hand.
    2.  Form a small overhand loop in the standing part about 30 cm (12 inches) from the working end. The working end should cross *over* the standing part (the “rabbit hole”).
    3.  Pass the working end up through the loop from underneath. The “rabbit comes out of the hole.”
    4.  Route the working end behind the standing part. The “rabbit goes around the tree.”
    5.  Pass the working end back down through the same small loop. The “rabbit goes back in the hole.”
    6.  Hold the working end and the near side of the large loop together in one hand. Pull the standing part with the other hand to tighten. The loop should remain fixed in size.
*   **Safety Check:** Verify that the working end exits *inside* the loop, running parallel to the near side of the loop. If it exits outside, the knot can capsize under shock loading.
*   **Common Mistakes:** Forming the initial loop backward (working end goes *under* instead of *over*) produces a left-handed bowline that is less stable; leaving too short a tail on the working end (leave at least 15 cm or 6 inches).

**2. The Figure-Eight Loop (Figure-Eight on a Bight)**
*   **Purpose:** Strongest knot, standard tie-in knot for climbing and high-load applications.
*   **Step-by-step:**
    1.  Take a bight (a U-shaped fold) of rope about 45 cm (18 inches) long. Work with this doubled section as if it were a single strand.
    2.  Form a loop by crossing the bight over the standing parts.
    3.  Bring the bight under and around the standing parts (completing a full wrap).
    4.  Pass the bight through the original loop from the top, just as with a basic figure-eight stopper knot.
    5.  Dress the knot by pulling the loop and standing parts in opposite directions. The knot should clearly show the figure-eight pattern with parallel strands throughout.
*   **Properties:** Retained rope strength ~75-80%; Difficult to untie after loading.

**3. The Alpine Butterfly Loop (Midline Loop)**
*   **Purpose:** Creates a fixed loop in the *middle* of a rope without needing access to either end; indispensable for multi-person teams and midline anchor points.
*   **Step-by-step:**
    1.  Drape the rope over your open hand with the rope crossing your palm. Need about 60 cm (2 feet) of slack.
    2.  Wrap the rope around your hand three times, creating three parallel wraps.
    3.  Take the middle wrap (the second one) and pull it over the wrap closest to your fingertips.
    4.  Tuck the same strand under the remaining two wraps from the palm side.
    5.  Slide the whole assembly off your hand and pull the loop and both standing ends to tighten. The knot is symmetrical and loads equally from both directions.
*   **Key Advantage:** Can be loaded in three directions (standing end or the loop) without failing.

### Loop Knot Selection Guide

| Scenario | Best Knot | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Rescue loop around a person | Bowline | Won’t tighten; easy to untie |
| Tying into a harness | Figure-eight loop | Strongest; won’t work loose |
| Anchor point at rope’s end | Bowline or figure-eight | Both work; bowline unties easier |
| Midline attachment point | Alpine butterfly | Only option for midline loops |
| Adjustable loop for snares | Running bowline | Slides to tighten, holds under tension |
| Quick temporary loop | Slip knot with stopper | Fast but only for light loads |

### Inspection Checklist

Before trusting any loop knot with a load:

*   The knot is dressed neatly with no crossing strands.
*   The tail (working end) is at least 15 cm (6 inches) long.
*   The loop is the correct size for the intended use.
*   A hard tug on the loop does not cause the knot to slip or deform.
*   For bowlines on slippery cordage, a stopper knot (overhand) is tied in the tail.

### Practice Drills

1.  **Speed drill:** Tie 10 bowlines in a row, aiming for under 10 seconds each. Then do it behind your back.
2.  **Blind drill:** Tie each loop knot with your eyes closed.
3.  **Material variety:** Practice on paracord, natural fiber cordage, shoelaces, and vines.
4.  **Load testing:** Tie each knot, hang a heavy rock from the loop, and verify it holds. Then untie it.

### The Running Bowline: A Sliding Loop

*   **Description:** Converts the bowline into a sliding noose that tightens under pull but releases easily. Tie a standard bowline, but pass the standing part of the rope *through* the bowline loop.
*   **Use for:** Retrieving objects from a distance, snares (for medium game), cinching around bundles for hauling.
*   **Warning:** Never use a running bowline as a rescue loop; it will constrict under load and can cause serious injury or death. Always use a standard bowline for rescue.

## Notes

*   The alpine butterfly is the only reliable midline loop.
*   Muscle memory is considered the only reliable path for proficiency.
*   The key takeaway is that the bowline is the default loop knot due to its combination of speed, lack of constriction, and ease of untying.
