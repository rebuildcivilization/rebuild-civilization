# Tension Control

## Objective
Consistent twist strength is what separates usable cordage from a pile of fibers that snaps at the first real load. Tension control is the single most important skill in hand-made rope.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Prepare more fiber than initially planned. Stopping to gather material mid-rope disrupts rhythm and creates weak points.
*   Sit in a comfortable, stable position, using a leg as a consistent surface for rolling.
*   Dampen fibers slightly; they must be pliable and grip each other when twisted. A damp cloth rubbed along the fibers is sufficient.

## Material
*   Cordage (Two-ply cordage is the primary focus).
*   Fibers.

## System
Every piece of cordage is a balance of opposing forces. Strength is derived from the interlocking friction created when twisting plies, which stores elastic energy that releases when a load is applied. A poorly tensioned rope made from excellent fibers will always lose to a well-tensioned rope made from average fibers.

## Technology
### The Physics of Twist
Twisting fibers achieves two simultaneous actions:
1.  **Compressing fibers together:** Helical wrapping forces adjacent fibers into tighter contact, increasing surface friction.
2.  **Storing elastic energy:** Each twist acts as a tiny spring, wanting to unwind when a load is applied.

**Optimal Twist Angle:** Approximately 20 to 30 degrees relative to the rope’s long axis.
*   Less than 15 degrees: Plies barely grip.
*   More than 40 degrees: Rope becomes stiff, kinked, and prone to hockling.

### Recognizing Good Tension
| Characteristic | Under-Twisted | Correct | Over-Twisted |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Appearance | Fibers visibly loose, gaps between plies | Smooth, uniform spiral, plies snug | Tight corkscrew, rope kinks when slack |
| Feel | Soft, spongy, plies separate easily | Firm but flexible, slight spring | Rigid, fights being straightened |
| Sound when plucked | Dull thud | Low hum | High-pitched ping |
| Under load | Stretches dramatically, then fails | Minimal stretch, holds load | Snaps suddenly at kink points |
| Cross-section | Oval or flat | Round | Figure-eight or irregular |

### Cordage Diameter and Method
| Cordage Diameter | Recommended Method | Twists per cm |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Under 2 mm | Finger twist only | 3-4 |
| 2-5 mm | Finger twist or thigh roll | 2-3 |
| 5-10 mm | Thigh roll | 1.5-2 |
| Over 10 mm | Lay up from smaller cordage | 1-1.5 |

### The Thigh-Rolling Technique (For cordage thicker than about 4 mm)
1.  Lay both plies across the thigh, parallel and side by side.
2.  Press the open palm across both plies and roll forward (away from the body) in one smooth stroke.
3.  At the end, lift the palm and reset, pinching the newly twisted section to prevent unwinding.
4.  Consistency requires the same palm pressure and stroke length every time.

### The Twist-and-Wrap Rhythm
*   **Step 4 (Twist):** Fold a fiber bundle with a 5 cm offset, pinch the fold between thumb and forefinger of the non-dominant hand.
*   **Step 5 (Rhythm):** Use a five-count cycle for each ply segment:
    *   **Count 1-2-3:** Twist the near ply away from you with three firm rolls.
    *   **Count 4:** Wrap the twisted ply over the other ply toward you.
    *   **Count 5:** Re-grip, sliding the pinch point forward to the new cross-over.
*   **Step 6 (Pressure):** Maintain a consistent pinch pressure—firm enough to prevent unwinding, gentle enough not to crush fibers.
*   **Step 7 (Buffer):** Keep approximately 5 to 8 cm (2 to 3 inches) of untwisted fiber ahead of the working point.

### Correcting Tension Problems in Real Time
*   **Under-twisted section detected:** Stop. Back up to the loose section by untwisting the last wrap, then add 2 to 3 extra twists to the ply before wrapping again.
*   **Over-twisted section detected:** Stop immediately. Let the ply unwind by relaxing the pinch, or roll the section against the thigh in the opposite direction to release twist. Resume with slightly fewer twists per cycle (try 2 instead of 3).
*   **One ply thicker than the other:** Add a few fibers to the thin ply before the next splice point, and give the thinner ply one fewer twist per cycle.

## Tools
*   Fingers/Hands (for pinching and twisting).
*   Damp cloth (for preparing fibers).
*   Measuring ability (for the 5 cm offset and the 30 cm calibration test).

## Process
### Setting Up Your Workspace
1.  Prepare sufficient fiber.
2.  Ensure a comfortable, stable position.
3.  Dampen fibers.

### Calibration Test (Practice Drill)
1.  Make a 30 cm (12 inch) section of two-ply cordage.
2.  Cut it into three 10 cm sections.
3.  Untwist each section and count the number of twists per centimeter in each ply.
4.  **Good Tension Check:** All three sections must have the same twist count (within half a twist per cm).
5.  **Action:** If counts vary by more than one twist per cm, slow down and focus on the counting rhythm.

### Common Tension Mistakes
*   **Rushing:** Speed leads to alternating tight and loose sections.
*   **Gripping too close:** The pinch point should be 1 to 2 cm behind the current twist.
*   **Ignoring fatigue:** Take a break every 20 to 30 minutes.
*   **Twisting both plies simultaneously:** Each ply must be twisted individually.

## Notes
*   Consistent tension is prioritized over fiber quality.
*   The five-count rhythm should become automatic.
*   Tension defects are nearly impossible to fix once locked in by subsequent twists.
*   Different fiber types require different twist counts (e.g., stiff bark fibers need fewer twists than soft grass fibers).
*   The final calibration step is crucial before starting a critical task.
