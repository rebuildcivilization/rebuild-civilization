# Cordage Applications

## Objective
Part of Knots and Cordage: Knowing how to make rope and tie knots is only valuable if you know what to build with them. This guide covers the practical, life-sustaining applications of cordage — from trapping food and hauling water to building tools and securing shelter. These are the projects that turn raw rope into survival.
To treat cordage as a multifunctional survival resource rather than only as shelter material.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
Cordage is limited in a post-collapse world, necessitating a priority focus on production. Tools must be tested for safety before use to prevent injury.

## Material
*   Rawhide strips
*   Sinew
*   Plant fiber (2-ply)
*   Wet inner bark
*   Paracord

## System
**The Cordage Priority List**
When cordage is limited, focus production on the highest-impact applications first:

| Priority | Application | Cordage Needed | Impact |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Shelter lashing | 15-30 m | Immediate — exposure kills in hours |
| 2 | Snares and traps | 5-15 m | Food within 24-48 hours |
| 3 | Fishing line | 5-10 m | Reliable protein source |
| 4 | Tool hafting | 2-5 m per tool | Multiplies labor efficiency |
| 5 | Carrying/hauling | 10-20 m | Transport water, wood, game |
| 6 | Net making | 50-100 m | Large-scale food gathering |
| 7 | Clothesline/drying line | 5-10 m | Food preservation, hygiene |
| 8 | Defensive perimeter | 20-50 m | Trip lines, alarms, barriers |

## Technology
### Application 1: Tool Hafting
**Process: Basic Hafting Method**
1. Select a handle: a straight hardwood stick, 40-80 cm long, 3-4 cm diameter. Green wood is acceptable but dry hardwood is stronger.
2. Prepare the head socket: Use a knife or sharp rock to split the top 5-8 cm of the handle (split-end haft) or carve a notch in the side of the handle (notch haft).
3. Seat the tool head into the split or notch, ensuring a snug fit without wobbling.
4. Bind with cordage: Wrap tightly below the split (to prevent further splitting), then wrap over and around the tool head in a crisscross pattern, using at least 10-15 tight wraps.
5. For a permanent bond, apply pine resin or hide glue over the binding to waterproof the joint.

**Hafting Cordage Selection**
*   Rawhide strips: Excellent (shrinks tight when dry, very durable)
*   Sinew: Excellent (strongest natural fiber, dries hard)
*   Plant fiber (2-ply): Good (adequate for light tools, replace regularly)
*   Wet inner bark: Good (shrinks as it dries, good for initial binding)
*   Paracord: Excellent (ideal for all hafting)

**Tool Safety**
A hafted tool with a loose head is more dangerous than no tool at all. Test every hafted tool by swinging it firmly 10 times before use. Re-check bindings before every work session.

### Application 2: Carrying and Hauling
**The Travois**
*   **Function:** Simplest load-hauling device (two long poles with a platform lashed between them, dragged by a person or animal).
*   **Steps:** 1. Cut two poles (250-300 cm long, 5-8 cm diameter). 2. Lash the narrow ends together with a shear lashing, leaving 30 cm for the pull handle. 3. Spread the butt ends apart. 4. Lash 3-5 crossbars between the poles, starting 80 cm from the pull end, spaced 30 cm apart (use square lashing). 5. Weave additional cordage or branches between the crossbars to create a platform. 6. Load cargo and lash it down. Pull from the narrow end.
*   **Capacity:** 50-100 kg.

**The Tumpline**
*   **Function:** Carrying strap that transfers pack weight from shoulders to forehead/neck.
*   **Steps:** 1. Braid a wide strap (5-8 cm wide, 60-80 cm long) using the widest, flattest cordage available.
2. Attach two longer cords (150-200 cm each) to the ends of the strap.
3. Use the long cords to wrap and secure the load (bundle of firewood, basket, pack).
4. Place the wide strap across the forehead and lean forward.

**Rope-Based Water Carry**
*   **Method:** Braid a net cradle for a large leaf bundle, hollowed log section, animal stomach, or clay-lined basket to prevent breakage.
*   **Alternative:** Weave a very tight basket from cordage and line it with pine pitch to make it waterproof.

### Application 3: Fishing Systems
**Hand Lines**
*   **Process:** 1. Make a fine 2-ply cord (3-5 m long, 1-2 mm diameter). 2. Attach a hook (carved bone, bent wire, thorns, or gorge hook). 3. Add a weight (small stone) 30 cm above the hook. 4. Wrap the line around a wooden frame (hand reel).

**Trotlines**
*   **Process:** 1. String a main line (10-30 m) across a water body, tied to stakes at both ends. 2. Tie dropper lines (30-50 cm each) every 1-2 m along the main line using a simple overhand loop. 3. Attach a baited hook to each dropper. 4. Check the trotline frequently. A 20-hook trotline can catch 5-10 fish overnight.

**Gill Nets**
*   **Principle:** A wall of mesh suspended in water that catches fish by their gills.
*   **Mesh Size vs. Target Fish:**
    *   2-3 cm: Minnows, baitfish
    *   5-7 cm: Panfish, perch, trout
    *   8-12 cm: Bass, catfish, pike
    *   15+ cm: Large fish, salmon

### Application 4: Defensive and Camp Security
**Perimeter Trip Lines**
*   **Process:** 1. Run cord at ankle height (15-20 cm) around the perimeter, tied between trees or stakes. 2. Hang noise-makers (tin cans with pebbles, shells, dry seed pods, or small bells) every 2-3 meters.
*   **Effect:** A single trip or touch alerts the camp.

**Snare Alarms**
*   **Process:** 1. Set a spring snare at approach paths. 2. Attach a noise-maker to the trigger mechanism. When tripped, the bent sapling releases with a snap, rattling the noise-maker loudly.
*   **Dual Duty:** Catches small game and alerts to movement.

**Overhead Storage**
*   **Process:** 1. Throw a line over a high branch (5+ meters up) using a rock weight. 2. Attach food bag with a bowline. 3. Hoist the bag to branch level, tying off the line at the base. The bag should hang at least 3 meters off the ground and 2 meters from the trunk.

### Application 5: Shelter and Construction Cordage
*   **Ridge Lines:** Used to hang tarps, lean-to roofs, or drying lines. Best knot combination: Bowline at one end (fixed anchor), trucker’s hitch at the other end (adjustable tension) for a 3:1 mechanical advantage.
*   **Guy Lines:** Angled lines from shelter peaks to ground stakes to prevent lateral movement in wind. Best knot: Taut-line hitch at the shelter end (adjustable), half hitch at the stake end (allows re-tensioning).
*   **Tarp Grommets:** When a tarp lacks grommets, wrap a small stone in the corner and tie cordage around the bulge to create a secure attachment point.

## Process
**Cordage Maintenance**
*   **Threats and Prevention:**
    *   UV sunlight: Fibers become brittle. Prevention: Store rope in shade when not in use.
    *   Repeated wetting/drying: Fibers weaken and fray. Prevention: Dry rope thoroughly before storage.
    *   Abrasion: Outer fibers wear through. Prevention: Pad rope where it contacts sharp edges.
    *   Mold and rot: Fibers decompose. Prevention: Never store wet rope in a bundle.
    *   Insect damage: Fibers are eaten. Prevention: Store with aromatic herbs (cedar, sage).
*   **Inspection Routine:** Before every critical use, run the rope through hands slowly to feel for thin spots, stiff sections, or separated fibers. Cut out damaged sections and re-splice, or retire the rope to non-critical uses.
*   **Replacement Schedule:** Natural plant-fiber rope used outdoors should be replaced every 2-4 weeks for load-bearing applications. Rope used only for tying bundles or non-critical binding can last months.

## Process
**Key Takeaways**
1. Prioritize cordage production by survival impact: shelter first, then snares, then fishing, then tools.
2. A travois can haul 50-100 kg of material. Build one early.
3. Trotlines fish for you while you sleep (20 hooks can catch 5-10 fish overnight with zero active effort).
4. Haft every cutting and striking tool; test before every use.
5. Natural cordage degrades rapidly outdoors; inspect before every critical use and replace load-bearing rope every 2-4 weeks.
6. A perimeter trip line with noise-makers is the simplest camp alarm system.
7. The trucker’s hitch combined with a bowline is the most powerful line-tensioning system (use for ridge lines and guy lines).

## Notes
*   The basic hafting method requires 10-15 tight wraps of cordage.
*   The travois capacity is defined as 50-100 kg.
*   For the taut-line hitch, the mechanical advantage is stated as 3:1.
*   The priority list dictates that shelter is the highest immediate priority.
*   Carry cordage because replacing it from natural materials requires significant time, energy and skill.
