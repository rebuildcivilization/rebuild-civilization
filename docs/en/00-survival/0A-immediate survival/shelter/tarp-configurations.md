# Tarp Configurations | Survival Wiki

## Objective
Part of Emergency Shelter. A flat sheet of waterproof material is one of the most versatile survival tools in existence. Knowing five or six configurations lets you adapt to any weather, terrain, or group size with a single piece of fabric.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   **Material Minimum:** A roughly square or rectangular sheet, 2.5 m × 3 m (8 × 10 feet) minimum.
*   **Essential Knots:** Exactly two knots are needed for all tarp configurations:
    *   **Bowline:** Creates a fixed loop that does not slip under load. Used for attaching guy lines to anchor points.
    *   **Taut-line hitch:** An adjustable friction knot that slides to tension a line but holds under load. Used for all guy lines so the tarp can be tensioned after pitching.
*   **Attachment without Grommets Method:** If grommets are absent, place a small smooth stone (2-3 cm / 1 inch) on the underside of the tarp at the desired attachment point, pinch the fabric around the stone to form a ball, and tie cordage around the neck of the ball with a clove hitch or simple overhand knot.

## Material
Any waterproof or water-resistant flat sheet works. Examples include purpose-made tarps, plastic sheeting, shower curtains, painter’s drop cloths, space blankets, garbage bags split open, or a tightly woven poncho.

## System
### Configurations
*   **The Diamond Fly (A-Frame):**
    *   **Best for:** Rain protection, moderate wind, general-purpose shelter.
    *   **Time to pitch:** 5-10 minutes.
    *   **Capacity:** 1-2 people.
    *   **Setup:** Tie a ridgeline between two trees or vertical supports (90-120 cm / 3-4 feet high). Drape the tarp over the ridgeline. Stake or weight the four corners outward at 45-degree angles from the ridgeline. Adjust the pitch angle: steeper sides for better rain shedding, shallower for more interior space.
    *   **Wind Management:** Orient the ridgeline parallel to the prevailing wind.
*   **The Plow Point:**
    *   **Best for:** Strong wind from a consistent direction, driving rain, solo shelter.
    *   **Time to pitch:** 5-10 minutes.
    *   **Capacity:** 1 person.
    *   **Setup:** Stake one corner of the tarp to the ground on the windward side (the "plow point"). Run a line from the opposite corner diagonally across the tarp to a support (tree, branch, or trekking pole) at about 90-100 cm (3 feet) height. Stake the two remaining corners out to the sides.
    *   **Advantages:** Extremely wind-resistant; rain runs off angled sides. Easy to pitch solo.
    *   **Disadvantage:** Only one open end, limited interior space, entry is from the rear.
*   **The Lean-To:**
    *   **Best for:** Fire reflection setups, mild weather, group shelter.
    *   **Time to pitch:** 5 minutes.
    *   **Capacity:** 2-4 people.
    *   **Setup:** Tie a ridgeline between two trees (120-150 cm / 4-5 feet high). Drape the tarp so roughly two-thirds hangs on one side and one-third on the other. Stake the long side to the ground at a 30-45 degree angle from the ridgeline. The short side can be folded under bedding or staked out.
    *   **Fire Integration:** Excels when paired with a fire and fire wall. Place the fire 90-120 cm (3-4 feet) from the open face.
*   **The C-Fly (Enclosed A-Frame):**
    *   **Best for:** Cold nights, wind from one end, maximum warmth retention.
    *   **Time to pitch:** 10-15 minutes.
    *   **Capacity:** 1 person.
    *   **Setup:** Pitch a standard diamond fly. Drop one end to the ground by pulling the stake on the windward end inward until the fabric reaches the ground, forming a closed triangular wall. Stake that end firmly to the ground.
    *   **Thermal Advantage:** The closed end blocks wind and traps warm air inside.
*   **The Cornet (Full Enclosure):**
    *   **Best for:** Heavy rain, snow, maximum protection from elements.
    *   **Time to pitch:** 10-15 minutes.
    *   **Capacity:** 1 person.
    *   **Setup:** Lay the tarp flat on the ground in a diamond orientation. Place a support (trekking pole, forked stick, suspended line) at the center of the far edge, raising it 60-90 cm (2-3 feet). Fold the two side corners underneath to create a floor. Stake the near corner to the ground (entrance flap). Stake the side edges down along their length.
    *   **Tradeoff:** Maximum weather protection but minimum interior space and ventilation.

### Tips for All Configurations
*   **Tension is everything:** A taut tarp sheds water. A saggy tarp pools water, flaps in wind, and eventually tears or collapses. Re-tension after rain.
*   **Drip lines:** If water runs down the ridgeline, tie a short piece of string to the ridgeline where it enters the tarp to direct water.
*   **Gutter channels:** In heavy rain, dig a shallow trench (5-8 cm / 2-3 inches deep) around the uphill side of the shelter to divert runoff.
*   **Site Selection:** A good site with a mediocre pitch beats a perfect pitch on a bad site. Avoid valley bottoms (cold air pools), hilltops (wind exposure), and areas beneath dead branches.
*   **Double Tarp Advantage:** Use one tarp as a ground sheet and one as a roof to prevent moisture wicking from below and extend insulation life.

## Tools
*   Tarp (waterproof or water-resistant flat sheet).
*   Cordage (for guy lines and knots).
*   Small smooth stone (2-3 cm / 1 inch) for attachment points without grommets.
*   Support elements (trees, trekking poles, forked sticks, suspended lines).
*   Measuring tools (implied by required dimensions).

## Process
1.  **Select Configuration:** Choose a configuration based on expected weather and group size.
2.  **Material Preparation:** Ensure the tarp meets the minimum size requirement (2.5m x 3m).
3.  **Execution (Setup):** Follow the specific setup steps for the chosen configuration (e.g., establishing ridgelines, staking corners, creating the enclosure).
4.  **Tensioning:** Ensure all lines and fabric are taut to maximize water shedding.
5.  **Post-Pitch Adjustment:** Re-tension the tarp after rain.

Notes
- The core philosophy centers on adaptability using minimal materials.
- The Lean-To configuration is explicitly noted to pair best with a fire and fire wall setup for reflected warmth.
- Synthetic tarps melt and drip burning plastic; natural-fiber tarps ignite. Sparks from popping wood can reach a 150 cm (5 feet) spark zone when burning softwood like pine or spruce.
- The primary takeaway is that site selection often outweighs the perfection of the pitch.
