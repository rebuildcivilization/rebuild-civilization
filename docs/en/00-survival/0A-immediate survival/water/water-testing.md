# Water Testing: Assessing Water Safety

## Objective
To systematically assess water safety by identifying obvious dangers, ranking multiple sources from least risky to most risky, and making informed decisions about which water sources to prioritize and which to avoid. The ultimate goal is to improve the odds of safe water consumption.

## Dependencies and prerequisites

*   **Senses:** Systematic application of sight, smell, taste, touch, and environmental sound.
*   **Purification:** The cardinal rule dictates that testing alone does not confirm safety; all tested water must be boiled, filtered, or treated before drinking.
*   **Upstream Survey:** The ability to walk upstream (or uphill) for at least 200 meters to assess potential contamination sources.

## Material

*   Water samples.
*   Clear containers (glass or clear plastic).
*   Rocks/Sediment samples.
*   Macroinvertebrates (mayflies, stoneflies, caddisfly larvae, tubifex worms, leeches, rat-tailed maggots).
*   Air/Volatile compounds (for smell assessment).

## System

The water safety assessment system involves a hierarchical approach:

1.  **Source Risk Assessment:** Evaluating the environment upstream before testing the water itself.
2.  **Field Testing:** Using physical observations and sensory checks to identify immediate risks.
3.  **Biological Indicators:** Using living organisms to confirm the water quality over time.
4.  **Sediment Testing:** Observing what settles to reveal organic matter or mineral load.
5.  **Comparative Ranking:** Ranking multiple sources based on risk to guide purification effort.
6.  **Monitoring:** Daily checks on stored water to catch degradation.

## Technology

*   **Field Testing:** Non-laboratory methods used to identify obvious dangers.
*   **Sensory Analysis:** Utilization of five senses to detect contaminants.
*   **Biological Monitoring:** Use of macroinvertebrates to indicate long-term water quality.

## Tools

*   **Visual Inspection Aids:** To check for clarity, color, surface film, and sediment.
*   **Olfactory Tools:** The nose for detecting volatile compounds (e.g., sulfur, solvents).
*   **Tactile Tools:** Fingers for assessing texture (slippery, gritty, oily).
*   **Charcoal Filter:** Used to aid in removing metallic tastes.
*   **Clear Container:** For the sediment test.

## Process

### The Five-Sense Assessment

*   **Sight:** Observe clarity, tint (green/brown), surface film (oily/foamy), and staining.
*   **Smell:** Step 1: Sniff directly. Step 2: Agitate the water to release volatile compounds and re-smell.
*   **Taste (Last Resort):** Step 1: Take a small sip. Step 2: Hold on the tongue for 5-10 seconds. Step 3: Spit out.
*   **Touch:** Rub a small amount of water between fingers to detect slipperiness, grit, or oil.
*   **Sound (Environmental Context):** Listen for flowing water sounds (indicating oxygenation) versus still water (indicating stagnation), and for industrial/mechanical sounds nearby.

### Source Risk Assessment Process

1.  **Upstream Survey:** Walk upstream for at least 200 meters to identify contamination sources (e.g., agricultural fields, human settlements, industrial sites).
2.  **Bank Inspection:** Examine the water’s edge for tracks, refuse, discolored soil, and pipe outlets.
3.  **Substrate Check:** Inspect the bottom for gravel/sand (healthy) versus black, foul-smelling mud (anaerobic decomposition).

### Biological Indicator Process

*   Examine rocks in the stream bed to lift a few and examine the underside for macroinvertebrates to determine water quality.

### Sediment Test Process

1.  Fill a clear container with water.
2.  Let it sit undisturbed for 30 minutes.
3.  Observe the settled material to interpret the result.

### Comparative Ranking Process

When multiple sources exist, rank them from Best to Worst based on criteria such as natural filtration (Underground spring), oxygenation (Fast-moving stream), and potential contamination level (Industrial sites, Landfills).

### Monitoring Stored Water Process

Daily Check Protocol:
1.  Visual inspection (cloudiness, color change, floating particles, surface film).
2.  Smell check (for new odors indicating degradation).
3.  Container exterior check (for leaks, cracks, or insect activity).

## Notes

*   **Pathogen Reality:** No field-accessible method can detect viruses, most bacteria, or dissolved chemical contaminants.
*   **Cyanobacteria Warning:** Blue-green algae produces toxins that survive boiling, UV treatment, or simple filtration. A thick, paint-like green/blue-green surface scum demands avoidance.
*   **Chemical Contaminant Rule:** A chemical smell (solvents, fuel, industrial runoff) means the water contains something that heat and filtration cannot fix. Boiling kills bacteria but does not remove chemical contaminants.
*   **Biological Indicator Summary:** The presence of mayfly nymphs, stonefly nymphs, and caddisfly larvae indicates good, oxygenated water quality. Only tubifex worms, leeches, or rat-tailed maggots indicate poor, polluted conditions.
*   **Cardinal Rule:** Field testing tells you which water to start with and when something has gone wrong—it does not tell you when water is safe. Prioritize purification over testing certainty.
*   **Ranking Hierarchy (Best to Worst):** 1. Underground spring; 2. Fast-moving stream; 3. Rainwater; 4. Deep well; 5. Large lake; 6. Slow-moving stream; 7. Pond/small lake; 8. Urban runoff; 9. Swamp/marsh; 10. Water near industrial sites/dumps.
