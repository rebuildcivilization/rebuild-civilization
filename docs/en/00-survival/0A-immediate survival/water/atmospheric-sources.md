# Atmospheric Sources: Dew, Rain, and Solar Stills

## Objective
To utilize the atmosphere as a water source when conventional water sources like rivers, lakes, or wells are unavailable.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Patience and knowledge of when and where each technique works best.
*   Minimal equipment.
*   The ability to combine multiple techniques for maximum daily yield.

## Material
*   Absorbent fabric (cotton t-shirt, towel, bandana, dry grass).
*   Clear plastic sheet or plastic bag.
*   Container for collecting water.
*   Mesh material (window screen, shade cloth, mosquito netting, loosely woven fabric).
*   Tree or shrub branches with healthy, green leaves.
*   Rocks or soil (for condensation points and pit base).
*   Drinking tube or flexible hose.

## System
The atmosphere acts as a water source through four primary methods: dew collection, solar stills, transpiration bags, and fog collectors.

## Technology
The core technology relies on the principle that if air is cooled below its dew point, water condenses out of it. Every technique exploits this principle in a different way.

## Tools
*   Absorbent cloth (for dew collection).
*   Clear plastic sheet (for solar stills).
*   Clear plastic bag (for transpiration stills).
*   Fine mesh material (for fog collectors).
*   Gutter or trough (for channeling water).
*   Container (for storage).
*   Flexible tube (for optional drinking).

## Process

### Method 1: Dew Collection
**The Cloth-Drag Method**
1.  **Prepare your cloth:** Use an absorbent material with maximum surface area.
2.  **Wake before dawn:** Dew accumulation peaks in the last 1–2 hours before sunrise.
3.  **Drag the cloth through vegetation:** Walk through tall grass, brush, or leafy ground cover, dragging or sweeping the cloth across plant surfaces.
4.  **Wring into a container:** Collect the saturated cloth.
5.  **Repeat:** Make multiple passes to collect more.

**Dew Collectors (Passive)**
1.  **Find or create a smooth, angled surface:** Use metal, plastic tarp, or a large flat stone, propped at a 30–45 degree angle facing the open sky.
2.  **Position a collection channel:** Attach a gutter or trough at the bottom to direct condensed water into a container.
3.  **Maximize radiative cooling:** Ensure the collector surface has a clear view of the sky and is slightly elevated.
4.  **Collect each morning:** Check and empty the container at dawn.

### Method 2: Solar Stills
**Ground Solar Still (Pit Still)**
1.  **Choose your site:** Pick a spot with maximum sun exposure and moist soil.
2.  **Dig the pit:** Dig a bowl-shaped hole approximately 90 cm across and 60 cm deep. Add green vegetation, wet cloth, or contaminated water to increase moisture.
3.  **Place the container:** Set a clean container in the center of the pit bottom.
4.  **Run a drinking tube (optional):** Place a tube in the container and run the other end outside the pit.
5.  **Cover with plastic:** Stretch a clear plastic sheet over the pit and seal the edges completely with soil, rocks, or sand to ensure an airtight seal.
6.  **Create the condensation point:** Place a small rock or handful of soil on the center of the plastic, directly above the container, to create an inverted cone.
7.  **Wait and collect:** Check and collect water in the evening. Avoid opening the still during peak sun hours.

**Output Expectations (Solar Stills)**
| Condition                          | Daily Output per Still | 
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------------- | 
| Moist soil, full sun, warm climate | 1.0–1.5 L              | 
| Dry soil with added vegetation     | 0.5–1.0 L              | 
| Desert sand, no additives          | 0.1–0.3 L              | 
| Contaminated water added to pit    | 1.0–2.0 L              |

**Improving Output Strategies**
*   **Add moisture sources:** Urine, saltwater, muddy water, or crushed green vegetation placed in the pit.
*   **Build multiple stills:** Build 3–5 stills to approach a survivable daily intake.
*   **Use dark-colored pit bottoms:** Absorb more heat and increase the evaporation rate.
*   **Insulate the collector plastic:** Cover the plastic with an insulating layer after sunset to reduce reverse condensation loss.

**Transpiration Still (Vegetation Bag)**
1.  **Select a branch:** Choose a non-toxic tree or shrub with healthy, green leaves.
2.  **Enclose the branch:** Tie a clear plastic bag around a leafy section of the branch and seal the opening tightly.
3.  **Position for sun:** Ensure the bagged section faces full sun. Place a small rock inside the bag at the lowest point to create a collection pool.
4.  **Collect:** Water accumulates after 4–8 hours.
5.  **Rotate branches:** Move the bag to a fresh branch every 1–2 days.

### Method 3: Fog Collection
**Building a Fog Collector**
1.  **Obtain mesh material:** Use a fine mesh (window screen, shade cloth, mosquito netting) that allows air to pass through while intercepting water droplets.
2.  **Build a frame:** Stretch the mesh vertically between two poles, ensuring the collector faces perpendicular to the prevailing wind direction.
3.  **Install a collection channel:** Attach a gutter or trough at the bottom to channel water into a container.
4.  **Position for maximum exposure:** Place the collector on ridgelines, hilltops, or anywhere fog is densest.

**Where Fog Collection Works**
Fog collection is most productive in:
*   Coastal deserts (where fog rolls in from the ocean).
*   Mountain ridges (where clouds intercept ridgelines).
*   Tropical highlands (where morning fog is common).
*   Near large bodies of water (due to temperature differences).

## Combining Methods
No single atmospheric source reliably meets daily needs; combining methods is the most effective strategy.

| Time of Day      | Method                        | Expected Contribution |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Pre-dawn         | Dew collection (cloth drag)   | 0.2–0.5 L             |
| Morning          | Fog collector (if applicable) | 0.5–5 L               |
| Midday-afternoon | Solar stills (passive)        | 0.5–1.5 L per still   |
| All day          | Transpiration bags            | 0.1–0.5 L per bag     |
| Evening          | Collect from solar stills     | (included above)      |

With 3 solar stills, 2 transpiration bags, and morning dew collection, survival-level water (3–5 liters per day) can be approached in favorable conditions.

## Purification of Atmospheric Water
*   **Solar still and Transpiration bag water:** Already distilled and generally safe to drink without further treatment.
*   **Dew and Fog water:** Usually clean but may pick up contaminants from collection surfaces; should be boiled if collection surface cleanliness is uncertain.
*   **Rain (roof runoff):** Requires purification due to bird droppings, dust, or debris on roofs.

## Notes
*   Atmospheric water sources can sustain life when conventional sources are unavailable, but require patience and simultaneous operation of multiple collectors.
*   The most effective strategy combines all available techniques.
*   Dew collection is the simplest technique.
*   Solar stills are the most widely taught survival still.
*   The primary challenge is volume, as atmospheric methods rarely produce large volumes quickly.
