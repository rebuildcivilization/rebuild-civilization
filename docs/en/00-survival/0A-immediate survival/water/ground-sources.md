# Ground Sources: Wells and Seepage

## Objective
To cover how to find subsurface water, assess its depth, and extract it using low-technology methods ranging from simple seepage pits to hand-dug wells, establishing groundwater as a long-term water strategy.

## Dependencies and prerequisites

*   **For Groundwater:** The availability of subsurface water sources (groundwater) when surface water is unavailable, contaminated, or unreliable.
*   **For Seepage Pits:** Water table must be within 1-2 meters of the surface, and proximity to a dry streambed, lake shore, or slope base.
*   **For Well Construction:** Requires more labor and materials for permanent access.

## Material

*   Groundwater (groundwater is the lifeline).
*   Soil (sand, gravel, clay, rock).
*   Vegetation (willows, poplars, cattails, rushes, sedges, mesquite).
*   Water (surface water, groundwater, seepage water).

## System

*   **Water Purification:** Groundwater still requires purification as natural soil filtration reduces but does not eliminate pathogens.
*   **Sustainability:** Groundwater is more reliable and better protected than surface water, making it the long-term water strategy.

## Technology

*   **Groundwater Assessment:** Using terrain features, vegetation indicators, and soil indicators to estimate water table location.
*   **Extraction Methods:** Seepage pits (simplest), hand-dug wells (permanent access).
*   **Aquifer Mechanics:** Understanding the water table (underground boundary below which pore spaces are saturated) and the aquifer (permeable layer that holds and transmits groundwater).

## Tools

*   Digging stick (for seepage pits).
*   Test hole (30-60 cm deep) for soil testing.
*   Containers or cloth (for collecting water).
*   Lining materials (rocks) for improved seepage pits.

## Process

### Finding Groundwater Without Equipment

1.  **Terrain Indicators:** Identify the lowest point in the landscape (valley floors, river bends). Look for dry riverbeds and streambeds. Note terrain transitions from slope to flat. Observe existing wells, springs, and seeps.
2.  **Vegetation Indicators:** Observe trees like willows and poplars (water table within 3-5 meters). Look for rushes, sedges, and cattails (water table at or within 0.5 meters). Check for greener grass patches.
3.  **Soil Indicators:** Dig a test hole 30-60 cm deep. Wait 30 minutes. If water seeps in, the water table is nearby. Examine the soil type:
    *   Sand and gravel: excellent water-bearing material.
    *   Coarse sand: good aquifer material.
    *   Clay: poor aquifer, acts as a barrier.
    *   Rock: fractured rock can transmit water.

### Seepage Pits and Collection Holes

1.  **When to Use:** When the water table is within 1-2 meters of the surface, near a dry streambed/lake shore, or when quick water is needed.
2.  **Construction:**
    *   Choose location 2-3 meters back from water body or at slope base.
    *   Dig hole 60 cm in diameter, as deep as necessary.
    *   Stop digging when the hole fills faster than it can be removed (hole fills to the water table level).
    *   Collect water by dipping a container or using a cloth.

### Improving Yield from Ground Sources

*   **Deepening:** Continue digging below the initial water level (standing in water while digging increases hydraulic head).
*   **Widening:** Doubling the diameter roughly doubles the yield for shallow excavations.
*   **Multiple Pits:** Dig 3-4 shallow pits spaced 3-5 meters apart to distribute extraction.
*   **Pre-filtering:** Place gravel at the bottom and sand above to slow fine sediment entry.
*   **Rest and Recovery:** Leave dry pits for several hours to allow the water table to equalize.

### Progression from Seepage to Well

1.  **Seepage pit:** Emergency solution, 5-20 L/day.
2.  **Improved seepage pit:** Lined with rocks, covered, 20-50 L/day.
3.  **Shallow hand-dug well:** 3-10 meters deep, lined, 100-500 L/day.
4.  **Deep hand-dug well:** 10-30 meters deep, fully lined and capped, 500+ L/day.

### Identifying Aquifer Quality

| Indicator | Good Sign | Bad Sign |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Taste | Clean, possibly slightly mineral | Strongly metallic, salty, or bitter |
| Color | Clear to slightly yellowish | Orange, brown, or milky |
| Smell | None | Sulfur (rotten eggs), chemical |
| Staining | None on container or hands | Orange-red stains (iron), black stains (manganese) |

**Minimum Separation Distances (for contamination):**
*   Latrine or outhouse: 30 meters and downhill
*   Animal pen or feedlot: 30 meters
*   Garbage dump: 50 meters
*   Cemetery or burial site: 50 meters
*   Agricultural field (pesticides/fertilizer): 30 meters
*   **Rule:** Contamination source must be downhill from the collection point.

## Notes

*   Groundwater is considered the long-term water strategy due to its reliability and protection compared to surface water.
*   The water table is shallowest in valley floors, dry streambeds, near water bodies, and at the base of slopes.
*   The progression from seepage pit to a well is necessary for permanent, protected access.
*   Seepage pits have a low yield (5-20 L/day) and require purification.
