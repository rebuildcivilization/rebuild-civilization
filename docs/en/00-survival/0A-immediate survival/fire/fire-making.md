# Fire Making

## Objective
To make fire without matches, using the fastest reliable method being the bow drill.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
Fire requires the simultaneous presence of three elements, known as the Fire Triangle: Heat, Fuel, and Oxygen. Removing any one element will cause the fire to die.

## Material
### Fuel Progression
*   **Tinder (catches spark/ember, burns 5-30 seconds):**
    *   Dry grass
    *   Cattail fluff
    *   Milkweed down
    *   Shredded birch bark (papery outer layer)
    *   Cedar bark (shredded finely)
    *   Dryer lint, cotton balls (if scavenged)
    *   Char cloth (burned cotton)
    *   Fatwood shavings (resinous pine heartwood)
*   **Kindling (catches from tinder flame, burns 1-5 minutes):**
    *   Pencil-thin dry twigs and sticks
    *   Split wood shavings
    *   Small pieces of dry bark
    *   Pine needles in bundles
*   **Fuel Wood (sustains the fire):**
    *   Wrist-thick sticks and up
    *   Split logs (catches faster than round logs)
    *   Hardwood (oak, maple, hickory) for long-lasting coals
    *   Softwood (pine, spruce) for quick heat but faster burnout

### Specific Materials for Methods
*   **Bow Drill:**
    *   Spindle: Dry, straight stick ~60 cm long, ~2 cm diameter (Good woods: cedar, willow, cottonwood, basswood, poplar).
    *   Fireboard/Hearth: Flat board ~30 cm long, ~2 cm thick.
    *   Bow: Curved stick or branch ~45 cm long.
    *   Cordage: Shoelace, paracord, plant fiber rope, rawhide strip.
    *   Bearing Block/Handhold: Palm-sized rock, hardwood knot, or shell with a dimple.
*   **Flint and Steel:**
    *   Flint/Stone: Flint, chert, quartz, jasper, agate, or obsidian.
    *   Steel: Any HIGH-CARBON steel (old file, spine of a carbon steel knife, old saw blade). Stainless steel does not work.
    *   Tinder: Char cloth or extremely fine, dry tinder (birch bark scrapings, dried fungus, cattail fluff).
*   **Hand Drill:**
    *   Spindle: Dry, straight stick ~60 cm long, ~1-1.5 cm diameter (Mullein stalks, cattail stalks, yucca stalks, clematis stems).
    *   Fireboard: Dry, soft-wood.

## System
### The Fire Triangle
1.  **Heat:** The initial ignition source (friction, spark, lens).
2.  **Fuel:** Something that burns (wood, dried grass, bark).
3.  **Oxygen:** Air flow to sustain combustion.

### Fire Lay Structures
*   **Teepee/Cone:** Lean kindling sticks in a cone shape over the tinder for good airflow and fast ignition.
*   **Log Cabin:** Stack kindling in alternating layers like Lincoln Logs, best for cooking as it burns down into a solid coal bed.
*   **Long Fire:** Two parallel logs with fire between them, good for warmth.
*   **Star Fire:** Arrange logs in a star/spoke pattern with the fire in the center, pushing logs inward for low fuel consumption.

## Technology
*   **Friction Fire:** The principle of using effort (bow or hand) to generate heat through rubbing (spindle against fireboard).
*   **Spark Fire:** The principle of using a hard edge (flint) to shave tiny particles off a fuel source (steel), creating sparks that ignite tinder.
*   **Lens Fire:** The principle of using a convex lens to focus sunlight into a point hot enough to ignite tinder.

## Process
### Fire Progression

**Tinder:** Catches spark/ember and burns 5-30 seconds.
**Kindling:** Catches from tinder flame and burns 1-5 minutes.
**Fuel Wood:** Sustains the fire.

### Method 1: Bow Drill (Most Reliable Friction Method)
1.  **Carve the spindle:** Find a dry, straight stick ~60 cm long and 2 cm in diameter. Both ends must be carved (bottom slightly rounded, top pointed for the bearing block).
2.  **Prepare the fireboard:** Split or carve a flat board ~30 cm long, 2 cm thick, and at least as wide as the spindle diameter plus 1 cm. Carve a small starting dimple ~1 cm from the edge.
3.  **Make the bow:** Find a sturdy, slightly curved branch. Tie cordage from end to end with slack sufficient to twist the spindle with one wrap.
4.  **Prepare the bearing block:** Use a rock with a natural dimple, hardwood knot, bone, or shell. Lubricate the dimple with earwax, pine sap, or grease so the top of the spindle spins freely (friction at the bottom).
5.  **Burn in the dimple:** Wrap the spindle once around the bowstring. Place the bottom end in the fireboard dimple. Press down with the bearing block and saw the bow back and forth to spin the spindle, applying moderate downward pressure. Stop when the dimple is roughly the diameter of the spindle.
6.  **Cut the notch:** Carve a V-shaped notch from the edge of the fireboard into the center of the burned dimple (about 1/8th of the circle). This collects hot dust.
7.  **Place catch:** Place a thin piece of bark, leaf, or flat chip under the notch to catch the ember.
8.  **Position:** Kneel with one foot on the fireboard. The wrist holding the bearing block should lock against the shin.
9.  **Sawing:** Start medium-speed with moderate pressure. Increase speed and pressure for 10-15 more strokes when thick smoke pours from the notch.
10. **Ember Transfer:** Carefully lift the spindle and fireboard. If a dark pile of dust smokes on its own, an ember is present. Gently fan or blow softly to transfer the ember (on its bark catch-plate) into the tinder bundle.
11. **Ignition:** Hold the tinder bundle and blow gently, steadily, into the center where the ember sits until it bursts into flame, then place it under kindling.

### Method 2: Flint and Steel
1.  **Find flint:** A hard stone with a sharp edge (flint, chert, quartz, jasper, agate, or obsidian).
2.  **Find steel:** Any HIGH-CARBON steel. Stainless steel will fail.
3.  **Prepare tinder:** Prepare char cloth (ideal) or use extremely fine, dry tinder (birch bark scrapings, dried fungus, cattail fluff).
4.  **Strike:** Hold the flint with char cloth on top. Strike the steel DOWN against the flint edge at a steep angle to shower sparks onto the char cloth.
5.  **Ignition:** When char cloth catches a spark (glows orange), transfer it to a tinder bundle and blow into flame.

### Method 3: Hand Drill
1.  **Find spindle:** Dry, straight stick ~60 cm long, 1-1.5 cm diameter (Mullein stalks, cattail stalks, yucca stalks, clematis stems).
2.  **Prepare fireboard:** Dimple and notch exactly as in the bow drill method.
3.  **Drill:** Place the spindle in the dimple. Press palms flat against the spindle near the top. Rub hands back and forth rapidly while pressing downward. Reposition hands quickly to the top and continue until a smoking ember is found.

### Method 4: Fire Plow
1.  **Find wood:** Flat piece of dry softwood (hibiscus, sotol, or cedar).
2.  **Carve groove:** Carve a shallow groove about 15-20 cm long.
3.  **Plow:** Take a hardwood stick with a blunt point and push forward with heavy, rapid pressure. Hot dust accumulates at the far end.
4.  **Ignition:** Continue until the dust pile begins smoking on its own.

### Method 5: Lens / Magnifying Glass
1.  **Find lens:** Reading glasses, binocular lens, magnifying glass, bottom of a glass bottle filled with water, a clear bag of water shaped into a sphere, or clear ice.
2.  **Focus:** Focus the sunlight through the lens onto the tinder, adjusting distance to get the smallest, brightest point of light.
3.  **Ignition:** Hold steady until the tinder smokes, then blow gently to coax it into flame.

## Notes
*   **Fire Triangle Principle:** The most common beginner failure is smothering the fire with too much fuel before it has enough heat, or not providing enough airflow.
*   **Wood Testing:** Dry wood snaps when tested; wet wood bends.
*   **Fuel Gathering:** Gather significantly more fuel than initially planned, as running to find sticks while tinder burns out is the most common failure.
*   **Bow Drill Tension:** Ensure cordage tension allows the spindle to spin freely, requiring only one wrap around the spindle.
*   **Tinder Blow:** Blow gently and steadily from 15-20 cm away to prevent blowing the ember out of the tinder.
*   **Wind Management:** Use rocks, logs, or the body as a windbreak to prevent wind from dissipating heat or scattering sparks.
*   **Wood Selection:** Spindle and fireboard should be similar softwoods for friction fire to work best.
*   Practice is essential: Practice often and never take fire for granted.
