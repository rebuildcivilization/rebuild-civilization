# Heat Reflection

## Objective
To make a basic shelter livable in freezing conditions by using a fire wall to bounce radiant heat back toward the shelter and using heat sinks to store residual warmth.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Fire
*   Shelter
*   Suitable materials (Green logs, dense, dry rocks, mud/clay)

## Material
*   Green logs (for basic log fire walls)
*   Dense, dry rocks (Granite, basalt, sandstone)
*   Flat stones (for the face of stone walls)
*   Mud or clay (to chink gaps in stone walls)
*   Aluminum foil or emergency blankets (up to 95% radiant heat reflection)
*   Sheet metal (for reflective surfaces)
*   Vehicle windshield sun shades (as an alternative reflector)

### Thermal Management Components
*   **Fire Walls (Reflector Walls):** Vertical barriers placed on the far side of the fire to reflect radiant heat back toward the shelter.
*   **Rock Heat Sinks:** Rocks placed beneath sleeping insulation to store and radiate warmth for 4-8 hours after the fire dies.
*   **Long Fire (Sustained Heat):** Two green logs laid parallel with a gap filled with tinder and kindling, burning slowly for 6-8 hours.

## Technology
*   **Heat Reflection:** Using a fire wall to redirect wasted energy (which otherwise goes into the sky) back toward the shelter.
*   **Heat Storage (Sinks):** Rocks absorb heat slowly and release it slowly, providing overnight warmth.
*   **Heat Sinking:** The principle that heated rocks continue radiating warmth after the fire dies.

## Tools
*   Stakes (for driving into the ground to establish fire wall boundaries).

## Process
### Fire Walls (Reflector Walls)
**Basic Log Fire Wall:**
1. Drive two pairs of stakes into the ground 90-120 cm apart on the far side of the fire pit.
2. Stack green logs horizontally between the stake pairs, using logs 8-15 cm in diameter, stacked 60-90 cm high.
3. Angle the wall slightly (10-15 degrees) toward the fire.
4. Wall width should be at least as wide as the shelter opening (120-180 cm ideal).
5. Place the wall 30-45 cm (12-18 inches) behind the fire.

**Stone Fire Wall:**
1. Select dense, dry rocks (Granite, basalt, sandstone).
2. Build a dry-stack wall 60-90 cm high, angling it toward the fire.
3. Use flat stones for the face to improve reflection.
4. Chink gaps with mud or clay to prevent heat passage.

### Rock Heat Sinks
**Heated Rock Bed:**
1. Build the fire directly on the ground where the sleeping spot is.
2. Heat additional fist-to-head-sized rocks around the perimeter of the fire.
3. Rake away the fire and coals, sweeping the ground clean.
4. Place heated rocks in a single layer where the torso will rest.
5. Cover with 8-10 cm (3-4 inches) of earth or sand, then add normal ground insulation.

**Personal Heat Stones:**
1. Heat smooth, dense stones (fist-sized) for 30-60 minutes at the fire's edge.
2. Wrap each stone in multiple layers of cloth, grass, or bark.
3. Place near the core (between thighs, against abdomen, or under armpits).

### Long Fire (Sustained Heat Through the Night)
1. Find or cut two green logs at least 15 cm (6 inches) in diameter and 180-200 cm (6-7 feet) long.
2. Lay them parallel with a 10-15 cm (4-6 inch) gap between them.
3. Fill the gap with tinder and kindling, then light.
4. The fire burns slowly along the length of the gap.
5. Place the fire wall behind the long fire and sleep on the opposite side, 60-90 cm (2-3 feet) away.

## Notes
*   **Rock Safety:** Never use river rocks or wet rocks near fire because trapped water turns to steam and can shatter the rock violently.
*   **Rock Testing:** Test rocks before reliance by tapping two rocks together firmly; a clear ring indicates a solid, safe rock.
*   **Rock Safety Buffer:** Always use a soil buffer between heated rocks and the body to prevent burns from direct contact with fire-heated rock.
*   **Key Takeaways:**
    *   A fire wall doubles the heat received from a campfire by reflecting wasted energy.
    *   Angle the reflector wall 10-15 degrees toward the fire so heat directs downward to the sleeping level.
    *   Rock heat sinks provide 4-8 hours of warmth after the fire dies.
    *   Position the shelter, fire, and reflector wall in a line with the wind hitting the reflector first.
