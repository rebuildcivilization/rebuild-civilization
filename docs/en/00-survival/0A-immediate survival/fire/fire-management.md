# Fire Management | Survival Wiki

## Objective
To master the three skills of survival fire management: maintaining a fire through varying conditions, banking it to preserve coals, and transporting fire to new locations. Starting a fire is identified as the first challenge.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- Correct fuel ratios (fuel, oxygen, and heat) are required for a fire to survive.
- Materials must be appropriately staged (e.g., having the next size of fuel ready).
- For banking, dense hardwood coals (oak, hickory, maple, beech) are preferred over softwood.
- For buried fires, mineral soil (clay, sand, gravel) must be used, avoiding forest duff or peat.

## Material
**Fuel Staging Materials:**
- Tinder: Bark shavings, dry grass, char cloth (Hair-thin to matchstick).
- Kindling: Small sticks, split wood (Pencil to thumb thickness).
- Fuel wood: Split logs, thick branches (Wrist to forearm thickness).
- Long-burn fuel: Dense hardwood logs, large rounds (4-8 inches (10-20 cm) diameter).

**Fire Lay Structures:**
- Teepee/Cone: Kindling and fuel leaned together in a cone shape.
- Log Cabin: Fuel stacked in alternating layers.
- Long Fire: Two parallel logs with fire burning between them.
- Star Fire: Several long logs arranged like spokes of a wheel.

**Banking Materials:**
- Thick layer of ash (2-3 inches (5-7 cm)) to insulate coals.
- Dry dirt (not wet soil) for buried fire insulation.

**Transport Materials:**
- Slow-burning material (tightly rolled bark of birch, cedar, or poplar; dried grass; punk wood).
- Hot coal (to embed in bundles).
- Green leaves or damp bark (to wrap bundles for insulation).
- Clay fire pots (4-6 inches / 10-15 cm diameter) filled with sand or grite.
- Shelf fungus (like Ganoderma) used as natural fire pots.

## System
**Fire Structure Basics:**
Every fire requires three proportional elements: fuel, oxygen, and heat. Failure to maintain the correct ratio leads to fire death.
The size of the fire must be determined by the amount of fuel available.

**Fire Behavior Readings:**
- Orange/red flames with white tips: Healthy combustion.
- Yellow/smoky flames: Incomplete combustion (too much fuel, not enough air).
- Blue flames at the base: Excellent, indicates very hot combustion of gases.
- Popping and crackling: Moisture in the wood.
- Hissing from log ends: Green or wet wood (wasting energy evaporating water).

**Fire Safety System:**
- Fireproofing requires clearing a 10-foot (3 m) radius around the fire of dry leaves and branches.
- Fire rings must be built from dry, dense, non-porous rocks (Granite, sandstone, basalt) to prevent fracture.
The location must take into account the wind, vegetation and the direction of the smoke.
A fire lit near shelter must also be planned with safety whilst sleeping in mind.

## Technology
**Fire Lay Technologies:**
- Teepee/Cone: Burns hot and fast, good for boiling water or light.
- Log Cabin: Burns evenly and collapses into a bed of coals, best for cooking over coals.
- Long Fire: a linear heat source running parallel to the body. Excellent for sleeping beside due to radiant heat.
- Star Fire: Very fuel-efficient for an all-night fire.
- Dakota fire pit: two chambers connected by a tunnel, making use of the natural draught.
- Keyhole fire: firebox with a side channel that allows the embers to be moved into the cooking area.

**Transport Technology:**
- Smoldering Bundle: A method using slow-burning material wrapped around an embedded hot coal.
- Fire Pots: Dedicated carriers (clay pots, fungus carriers, metal cans with ventilation).

## Tools
- Fire Lay Structures (Teepee, Log Cabin, Long Fire, Star Fire).
- Clay fire pots and carriers.
- Smoldering bundles.
- Tools for digging fire pits (for buried fires).

## Process
**1. Maintaining a Fire:**
   a. Ensure the three elements (fuel, oxygen, heat) are in proper proportion.
   b. Select the appropriate fire lay based on the required heat/duration.
   c. Read the fire's behavior (color, sound) to determine necessary adjustments.

**2. Banking a Fire:**
   a. Let the fire burn down to a thick bed of coals.
   b. Push coals into a compact pile.
   c. Cover with a thick layer of ash.
   d. (Advanced) Bury the coals in mineral soil with a small air gap for long-term preservation.

**3. Reviving a Banked Fire:**
   a. Carefully remove the ash.
   b. Blow gently to check for an orange glow.
   c. Add fine tinder directly onto the coals.
   d. Build back up through the fuel stages.

**4. Transporting Fire:**
   a. Use a Smoldering Bundle for short-term transport, checking and blowing periodically.
   b. Use dedicated Fire Pots (clay, fungus, metal) for regular transport.

**5. Fuel planning:**
   a. Estimate how long the fire is likely to last.
   b. Gather fuel before lighting the fire.
   c. Separate tinder, kindling and fuel.
   d. Keep a dry supply sheltered from the rain.
   e. Do not start a high-consumption fire if there is not enough fuel available.

### Long fire
   1. Arrange two rows of wood parallel to the body.
   2. Leave sufficient space between the wood and the resting area.
   3. Use large pieces of wood.
   4. If necessary, build a wall of logs on the opposite side to improve heat retention.
   5. Make sure you have plenty of fuel ready well in advance, as consumption is high.

### Dakota fire pit
   1. Dig two cavities.
   2. Connect them with a tunnel.
   3. Put the fire in the leeward cavity.
   4. Leave the windward opening clear to improve the draught.
   5. Consider fuel consumption before choosing this configuration.

### Keyhole fire
   1. Create the main fire pit.
   2. Dig a channel at the same depth to the side.
   3. Transfer the embers to the channel.
   4. Use this area for cooking.


## Notes
- Buried fires must be located in mineral soil (clay, sand, gravel) to prevent underground ignition of root systems.
- River stones and porous rocks should be avoided in fire rings due to the risk of explosion from trapped moisture.
- Smoke management dictates sleeping upwind of the fire or using a dedicated smoking fire to separate smoke from living spaces.
- The core principle is that a well-managed fire can run for weeks, months, or indefinitely by mastering maintenance, banking, and transport.
- A long fire can require a very large amount of wood: the energy cost of gathering it must also be taken into account.
- The Dakota fire is not automatically more efficient: the draught also increases fuel consumption.
- The choice of fire lay should depend on heat, duration, cooking and fuel availability, not on a universally ‘best’ configuration.
- Conserving energy and resources is part of survival; it’s not just about being able to light a fire.
