# Hot Stone Method

## Objective
To boil water when no metal container is available.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Dense, dry rocks from high ground (river stones, sandstone, flint, limestone must be avoided).
*   A fire to heat the stones.
*   Any waterproof vessel (e.g., wooden bowl, bark bucket, hide-lined pit).

## Material
*   Dense, dry rocks (Granite, Basalt).
*   Fire.
*   Waterproof vessels (Wooden bowl/trough, birch bark container, hide-lined pit, woven basket).

## System
The solution is to heat the medium (rocks) instead of heating the container. By stacking enough hot stones into a vessel, the water reaches a rolling boil without the container ever touching the direct flame.

## Technology
The method relies on thermal energy transfer; a fist-sized stone pulled from a fire holds enough thermal energy to raise the temperature of a liter of water by 30-50 degrees Celsius in seconds.

## Tools
*   Transfer tools: Split-stick tongs, two sticks used as chopsticks, looped cordage (wet rawhide or plant-fiber rope), flat bark shovel.

## Process
**Step 1 — Build a strong fire.** Establish a bed of hot coals, sustained by a fire burning for 30-45 minutes with hardwood.
**Step 2 — Place hot stones.** Place 6-10 fist-sized stones directly into the fire, nesting them into the coal bed, and leave them for at least 20-30 minutes until they glow faintly red or are too hot to approach closely.
**Step 3 — Prepare transfer tools.** Wet transfer tools before each use to prevent catching fire upon contact with glowing rocks.
**Step 4 — Rinse ash.** Briefly dip the hot stone in a small amount of sacrificial water or brush off loose ash with a wet stick.
**Step 5 — Transfer the first stone.** Swiftly transfer the first stone into the vessel, lowering it to the bottom center, away from the walls if using bark or wood.
**Step 6 — Add more stones.** Add stones one at a time, spacing them apart in the container. For 2-3 liters, typically 4-6 hot stones are needed to reach a rolling boil.
**Step 7 — Maintain the boil.** Remove cooling stones with tongs and replace them with fresh hot stones from the fire. Maintain a rolling boil for at least **1 minute** (3 minutes above 2,000m elevation).
**Step 8 — Remove stones.** Fish out every stone before drinking to avoid swallowing grit or chipping teeth.

### Rock Safety Table
| Rock Type | Safe? | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Granite | Yes | Dense, common, excellent heat retention |
| Basalt | Yes | Dark volcanic rock, very dense |
| Sandstone | No | Porous, crumbles and may burst |
| Slate | Caution | Can flake along layers, avoid thin pieces |
| Limestone | No | Cracks, may release calcium compounds |
| River cobbles | No | Often waterlogged internally despite dry surface |
| Quartz | Caution | Can fracture; use only if fully dry and non-porous |
| Flint/Chert | No | Fractures explosively when heated |

## Quantities and Timing
| Water Volume | Stones Needed | Time to Boil | Fire Prep Time |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 liter | 2-3 stones | 3-5 minutes | 30 min |
| 2-3 liters | 4-6 stones | 5-10 minutes | 30 min |
| 5 liters | 8-12 stones | 10-15 minutes | 45 min |
| 10+ liters | 15-20 stones (rotation) | 20-30 minutes | 45+ min |

## Common Mistakes
| Mistake | Consequence | Prevention |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Using river stones | Violent explosion, shrapnel injuries | Only use dense, dry rocks from high ground |
| Not pre-filtering cloudy water | Heat energy wasted on sediment, stones cool faster | Filter through cloth first |
| Moving stones slowly | Stone cools during transfer, need more rotations | Position fire within arm’s reach of vessel |
| Dropping stones from height | Cracks bark containers, splashes scalding water | Lower stones gently using tongs |
| Forgetting to ash-rinse | Gritty, foul-tasting water | Quick dip in sacrificial water before adding to drinking water |
| Too few stones in rotation | Water heats but never reaches full boil | Keep at least 6-8 stones cycling between fire and vessel |

## Scaling Up: Stone Boiling for Groups
For large volumes (20-50 liters in a clay-lined pit), a two-person team can manage the fire and transfer. With 20+ stones in constant rotation, the system can purify enough water for 15-20 people per hour once running.

## When to Use This Method
*   When metal containers are absent.
*   When the only vessel is flammable (wood, bark, hide, plastic).
*   When boiling large quantities in a ground pit is necessary.
*   When building proper infrastructure for a long-term camp.

## Key Takeaways
*   Heat dense, dry rocks from high ground — **never** use river stones, sandstone, flint, or limestone.
*   Pre-dry candidate stones near the fire to test for cracking before using them at full heat.
*   Fist-sized stones in a rotation of 6-10 will boil 2-3 liters of water in under 10 minutes.
*   Rinse ash off stones before adding to drinking water.
*   Maintain a rolling boil for 1 minute minimum (3 minutes above 2,000m elevation).
*   The method requires no manufactured tools, only rocks, fire, and a waterproof container.

## Notes
*   The critical safety rule is rock selection: porous rocks trap moisture, which converts to steam and expands explosively when heated.
*   The primary historical function of this method predates metal cookware, being used for boiling water, rendering fat, cooking soups, and processing hides.
*   The efficiency of the method hinges on pre-drying candidate stones to prevent explosive failure during the heating phase.
