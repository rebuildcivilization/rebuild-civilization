# Net Fishing

## Objective
To employ the most efficient fishing method ever devised, resulting in high yield by catching dozens of fish per effort hour, while allowing the fisher to work on other tasks.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   **Cordage:** A large supply of thin, strong cordage is required before net making.
*   **Net Material:** Plant fibers (nettle, hemp, flax, bark) or modern scavenged line (Paracord, unraveled nylon rope, fishing line, dental floss, thin synthetic string).
*   **Tools for Construction:** A shuttle (notched flat piece of wood 15–20 cm long) and a mesh gauge (flat piece of wood or stiff bark).
*   **Technique Mastery:** Proficiency in the sheet bend knot is required for all net junctions.

## Material
*   **Cordage:** Thinner cordage is preferred; consistency in thickness is necessary for uniform mesh.
*   **Netting Material:** Plant fibers (nettle, hemp, flax, bark) or modern scavenged line.
*   **Mesh Gauge Material:** A flat piece of wood or stiff bark, sized according to the desired mesh opening.

## System
The net fishing system leverages the power of multiplication, where one net can catch more fish in a night than a hook-and-line fisher catches in a week. The system is designed to maximize fishing output per hour of effort.

## Technology
*   **Net Efficiency:** Nets multiply fishing output per hour of effort by a factor of 10 or more.
*   **Core Knot:** The sheet bend knot is the single knot required for all net junctions.
*   **Construction Aid:** The shuttle speeds up construction by holding the working line without tangles.

## Tools
*   Shuttle (piece of wood or bone, 15–20 cm long, 2–3 cm wide, with notches).
*   Mesh Gauge (piece of wood or stiff bark used to determine mesh size).
*   Measuring tools (implied for precise line length and mesh opening).

## Process
### Making Basic Cordage
1.  Ensure cordage is thinner than the fish perceive.
2.  Maintain consistency in thickness to ensure uniform mesh.
3.  Use plant fibers or modern scavenged line.

### Making a Net Shuttle
1.  Find a flat piece of wood or bone (15–20 cm long, 2–3 cm wide).
2.  Cut a notch or fork at each end.
3.  Wind cordage around the shuttle in a figure-eight pattern between the two notches.
4.  Use the shuttle to pull line when tying knots to prevent tangles.

### Making a Mesh Gauge
1.  Cut a flat piece of wood or stiff bark wide enough to match the desired mesh opening.
2.  Ensure the gauge is smooth for easy sliding of the line after knotting.

### Net Types and Construction
| Net Type | How It Works | Best Water | Effort to Make | Yield | 
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | 
| Gill net | Fish swim into mesh and get tangled by their gills | Rivers, lakes, coast | High (hours of weaving) | Very high — passive | 
| Cast net | Thrown over fish, weights trap them | Shallow water, visible schools | High (complex construction) | High — active | 
| Seine net | Dragged through water by two people | Beaches, shallow bays, ponds | High | Very high — active | 
| Dip net | Scooped under fish | Streams, runs, near structures | Low | Low to moderate — active | 
| Scoop net | Small hand net | Tidal pools, small streams | Very low | Low — active | 

### The Netting Knot (Sheet Bend)
1.  Hold the existing mesh loop in your left hand.
2.  Pass the working line up through the loop from behind.
3.  Wrap the working line around behind both sides of the loop.
4.  Tuck the working line under itself (under the part that came up through the loop).
5.  Pull tight until the knot locks.

### Building Simple Nets
*   **Simple Dip Net:**
    1.  Find a forked branch (1m long) and bend a flexible branch/wire into a hoop.
    2.  Lash the hoop across the fork opening to create a frame.
    3.  Tie a starting line across the top.
    4.  Hang loops of equal length from the starting line, spaced by mesh width.
    5.  Connect adjacent loops with sheet bend knots at the mesh gauge distance.
    6.  Continue row by row until the net bag is 30–40 cm (12–16 inches) deep.

*   **Seine Net:**
    1.  Weave a flat rectangular net (3–5m long, 1–1.5m deep) using the shuttle and gauge.
    2.  Thread a strong top line (headline) and attach floats every 30–50 cm.
    3.  Thread a strong bottom line (lead line) and attach weights every 30–50 cm.
    4.  Attach sturdy poles to each end, lashed vertically.
    5.  Deployment: One person holds a pole while the other makes a wide arc, pulling the net onto shore.

### Maintenance
*   **Dry after every use:** Spread nets on bushes or hang them to prevent rot.
*   **Repair immediately:** Sheet bend patches should be used for holes.
*   **Treatment:** Soak natural fiber nets in bark tea (oak, willow, or pine bark) to resist rot.
*   **Storage:** Store dry and folded, not ball up, to prevent tangles.

### Setting Strategy
*   **Best Locations:** Where streams narrow (funnels fish), river bends (where fish follow the outer bank), inlet/outlet of pools (natural travel corridors), weed edges in lakes, and tidal channels.
*   **Best Timing:** Set nets at dusk and check at dawn (when fish move most), during rain (rising water stirs fish), and during spawning runs (predictable fish travel).

## Notes
*   **Efficiency Metric:** A net multiplies fishing output by a factor of 10 or more per hour of effort.
*   **Material Quality:** The thinnest cordage is better because fish avoid thick cordage.
*   **Safety Warning:** When setting a seine net, never deploy in water deeper than the chest or in strong currents, as the net can catch on obstacles and pull the fisher underwater. A knife must always be ready.
*   **Longevity:** Tannin treatment extends the life of natural fiber nets significantly.
*   **Key Takeaway:** Start with a dip net to learn, then scale up to gill nets or seines.
