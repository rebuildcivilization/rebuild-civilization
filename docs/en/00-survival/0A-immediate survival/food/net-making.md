# Net Making

## Objective
A fishing net is a force multiplier because it works passively while you attend to other tasks. A single gill net can catch more fish overnight than a hook and line in a week. Nets also serve beyond fishing: they can carry loads, trap small game, strain sediment from water, or become a hammock. The skill of netting provides a manufacturing capability that scales, allowing for nets of any size.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
Cordage production must start days before netting begins. Budget 3-4 hours of cordage making for every hour of netting.

## Material
### Cordage Selection
| Material | Strength | Durability in Water | Availability |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Nettle fiber | High | Good (improves wet) | Temperate forests, spring-fall |
| Dogbane fiber | High | Very good | Fields, roadsides |
| Linden/basswood inner bark | Medium | Fair | Hardwood forests |
| Cattail leaves | Low | Poor | Wetlands, pond edges |
| Sinew (animal tendon) | Very high | Fair (softens) | Any hunted animal |
| Salvaged string/twine | Varies | Varies | Abandoned buildings, farms |

Your cordage must be strong when wet and thin enough to remain somewhat invisible to fish. Two-ply twisted cord of 1-2 mm diameter works well for most fishing nets. Thicker cord (3-4 mm) suits cargo nets and game traps.

### The Gauge (Mesh Stick)
The gauge determines mesh size. It is a flat, smooth stick, usually 1/2 to 3 inches wide, 4-6 inches long, and 1/4 inch thick. The mesh opening is always twice the gauge width because the cord wraps around both sides.

**Gauge Width by Target Species**
| Target Species | Gauge Width | Mesh Opening |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Minnows (bait) | 1/2 inch (1.3 cm) | 1 inch (2.5 cm) |
| Panfish, perch | 1 inch (2.5 cm) | 2 inches (5 cm) |
| Trout, bass | 1.5 inches (3.8 cm) | 3 inches (7.6 cm) |
| Large fish (salmon, catfish) | 2.5 inches (6.4 cm) | 5 inches (12.7 cm) |
| Small game (rabbits) | 2 inches (5 cm) | 4 inches (10 cm) |
| Cargo/carrying | 2-3 inches (5-7.6 cm) | 4-6 inches (10-15 cm) |

### The Netting Needle
A netting needle holds the working cord and passes through the mesh loops. It can be carved from hardwood or bone.

**Netting Needle Carving Steps**
1. Start with a flat piece 6-8 inches long, 3/4 inch wide, 1/4 inch thick
2. Carve a fork (open prong) at one end — a 1-inch deep notch
3. Carve a pointed tongue at the other end with a small notch to catch cord
4. Smooth all edges so cord slides freely
5. Load cord by winding in a figure-eight between the fork and tongue

If a proper netting needle cannot be made, a simple shuttle (a flat stick with notches at both ends) can be used, though it will be slower.

## Material
Cordage selection, the gauge stick, and the netting needle are the primary materials required.

## System
The core technique relies on a single knot: the sheet bend (mesh knot or netter’s knot). Every net is built from this one knot, which tightens under load and does not slip when correctly tied.

## Technology
The technology involves mastering the sheet bend knot and using a gauge stick to ensure consistent mesh size, which is determined by the target species.

## Tools
The key tools include the gauge stick, the netting needle, and the cordage.

## Process
### Tying the Mesh Knot — Step by Step
1. Hold the gauge horizontally in your left hand (reverse if left-handed).
2. Bring the working cord down in front of the gauge.
3. Pass the needle up through the mesh loop above (the loop from the previous row).
4. Pull cord until the mesh loop sits snug against the top of the gauge.
5. Hold the junction with your left thumb against the gauge.
6. Swing the needle to the left, behind both the mesh loop cords.
7. Bring the needle forward, passing between the mesh loop and your working cord.
8. Pull tight — the knot cinches on the bottom of the mesh loop.
9. Slide the gauge to the right to the next position and repeat.

**Tension Control**
Every knot must be pulled to the same tension. Too loose and the mesh sags unevenly; too tight and the net cannot open properly in water. The gauge ensures consistent size, but the pull determines consistent shape. The rhythm to develop is: loop, pass, cinch, slide.

### Building a Complete Net
**Step 1: Set Up the Header Line**
Tie a taut horizontal cord between two fixed points (trees, stakes, a frame). This is the header line; its length determines the net’s width.

**Step 2: Cast On the First Row**
Create the first row of loops by tying half hitches or lark’s head knots along the header line at intervals equal to your gauge width times two. For a 6-foot net with 2-inch mesh, cast on 36 loops.

**Step 3: Work Subsequent Rows**
Turn the work and net back across. Each row, pass the needle through each loop of the previous row and tie a mesh knot. The gauge sits below the previous row to ensure consistent depth.

**Row Count by Depth**
| Desired Depth | Mesh Size | Rows Needed |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 2 feet (60 cm) | 2 inches (5 cm) | 12 rows |
| 3 feet (90 cm) | 2 inches (5 cm) | 18 rows |
| 4 feet (120 cm) | 3 inches (7.6 cm) | 16 rows |

**Step 4: Finish the Bottom Edge**
Run a cord through all bottom loops and tie off. This is the footer line. For a gill net, leave the bottom free or weight it lightly with small stones tied at intervals.

**Step 5: Rig for Use**
**Gill Net Setup**
- Tie small floats (bark, sealed hollow reeds, carved wood) to the header line every 12-18 inches.
- Tie small weights (stones wrapped in cord) to the footer line every 18-24 inches.
- The net hangs vertically in the water like a wall.
- Set across current at dusk; fish swim into the mesh and are caught by their gills.

**Scoop/Dip Net Setup**
- Bend a green sapling into a hoop (18-24 inches diameter).
- Lash the ends together and attach to a pole.
- Net a small bag-shaped net onto the hoop using the same mesh knot technique.

### Repair
To repair a tear:
1. Cut away damaged mesh cleanly — remove broken knots entirely.
2. Tie new cord to an intact mesh knot adjacent to the hole.
3. Rework the mesh through the hole using the same knot pattern.
4. Tie off to an intact knot on the far side.
5. Trim excess cord to 1/4 inch from knots.

**Maintenance Note:** Dry your net after every use, as cordage rots when stored wet.

## Notes
- **Force Multiplier:** The passive harvesting ability is the primary benefit, allowing the user to attend to other survival tasks.
- **Bottleneck:** Net making is bottlenecked by cordage supply, requiring days of planning ahead.
- **Common Mistakes and Fixes:**
    - *Mesh holes uneven:* Caused by inconsistent gauge use. Fix: Keep the gauge pressed flat and don’t rush.
    - *Knots slipping:* Caused by the sheet bend being tied backward. Fix: The working cord must pass under itself.
    - *Net tangles constantly:* Caused by mesh being too fine for the material. Fix: Use stiffer cord or a larger gauge.
    - *Net tears at edges:* Caused by no reinforcement. Fix: Double the cord on edge meshes.
- **Legal and Ethical Note:** In a non-emergency context, gill nets are illegal in most jurisdictions because of their effectiveness; this knowledge is for genuine survival situations only.
- **Key Takeaways:**
    - One knot does everything: the sheet bend is the only knot needed.
    - The gauge controls quality: consistent mesh size is essential.
    - Plan cordage first: start making cord days in advance.
    - Match mesh to target: ensures efficiency.
    - Maintenance is crucial: dry nets and repair immediately.
