# Snare Building

## Objective
To build a snare, form a small lock loop, create an adjustable running loop, size the loop to the target animal, and anchor the free end to a stake or tree.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Cordage or wire.
*   Stakes, heavy logs, or trees for anchoring.
*   Knowledge of animal signs and the funnel principle.
*   Gloves or leaf barriers for scent control.

## Material
*   Cordage or wire (for the loop and trigger lines).
*   Stake (for anchor or trigger).
*   Flexible sapling (for the spring snare).
*   Heavy log (for the drag snare).
*   Carved sticks (for the trigger mechanism).

## System
*   **Simple Loop Snare:** A basic loop set on the ground or at animal-head height along a trail.
*   **Spring Snare (Powered Snare):** Uses a bent sapling to lift the caught animal, which kills it more quickly and keeps the catch away from scavengers.
*   **Drag Snare:** Uses a heavy log to slow down or stop the animal, causing it to drag in brush.

## Technology
*   **Efficiency:** Snares are the most calorie-efficient hunting method, working 24 hours a day with low time investment relative to active hunting.
*   **Probability:** Snaring is a probability game; more snares yield a higher expected catch rate.
*   **Durability:** Wire is superior to plant cordage for the loop due to high chew resistance and longevity.

## Tools
*   Gloves or leaf barriers (for scent control).
*   A sharp club (for dispatch).
*   Measurement tools (implied by sizing loop dimensions).

## Process
### Snare Construction (General)
1.  Form a small lock loop (2-3 cm diameter) at one end of 60-90 cm of cordage.
2.  Thread the free end through the lock loop to create an adjustable running loop.
3.  Size the loop according to the target animal's requirements.
4.  Anchor the free end to a stake or tree.

### Specific Snare Processes
**Simple Loop Snare Construction:
1.  Form a small loop (2-3 cm diameter) at one end of the cordage by tying an overhand knot (the lock loop).
2.  Thread the other end of the cordage through this small lock loop.
3.  Size the snare loop to the target animal (e.g., 10-15 cm diameter at 8-10 cm height for rabbits).
4.  Tie the free end of the cordage to the anchor.
5.  Set the loop in the animal’s path, supporting it with small twigs on either side for rabbits.

**Spring Snare Construction (Two-Stick Trigger):
1.  Drive a sturdy stake (3-4 cm diameter, 30 cm long) firmly into the ground.
2.  Carve a notch on the top of the stake, angled slightly downward toward the sapling.
3.  Cut a toggle stick (15 cm long, 1-2 cm diameter).
4.  Tie the trigger line from the bent sapling to the center of the toggle stick.
5.  Tie the snare loop line to one end of the toggle stick.
6.  Hook the toggle stick under the notch in the stake.
7.  When an animal enters, the toggle stick is pulled free, causing the sapling to spring upright, tightening the loop and lifting the animal.

**Drag Snare Construction:
1.  Build a standard loop snare.
2.  Tie the free end to a log heavy enough to slow but not stop the animal (e.g., 3-5 kg for rabbits).
3.  Set the snare so the caught animal drags the log, which catches on brush, roots, and rocks.

### Placement and Control
*   **Placement Strategy (Funnel Principle):** Find a game trail, build a small fence of sticks on both sides, leaving a gap the width of the loop. Set the snare in the gap.
*   **Reading Animal Signs:** Use signs like worn trails, droppings, chewed vegetation, tracks, and burrow entrances to determine the best placement.
*   **Scent Control:** Handle components with gloves or leaf barriers. Rub the snare with local vegetation to mask human scent. Approach the site from downwind.

### Checking and Processing
*   **Check Frequency:** Check snares every 12-24 hours.
*   **Action upon Finding:**
    *   If alive: Dispatch quickly with a club strike to the head.
    *   If dead: Remove, reset the snare, and process the catch.
    *   If triggered but empty: Check for cordage damage, reset.
    *   If undisturbed after 48 hours: Move the snare to a better trail.
*   **Processing the Catch:**
    1.  Dispatch immediately if alive (sharp blow to the base of the skull).
    2.  Bleed the animal by cutting the throat and hang by the hind legs to drain.
    3.  Skin while warm (within 1-2 hours).
    4.  Gut by making a careful incision from the pelvis to the ribcage, avoiding the intestines and bladder.
    5.  Cook thoroughly to at least 70C (160F) internally.

## Notes
*   **Wire Superiority:** Wire is highly recommended for the snare loop due to superior durability and chew resistance compared to plant cordage.
*   **Numbers Game:** The investment in multiple snares is justified, with 20 snares providing a reliable daily protein system (65-80% catch rate for 10 snares).
*   **Scent Advantage:** Natural cordage has a less alarming scent than synthetic materials, but human urine repels most prey species.
*   **Placement Precedence:** The text explicitly states that placement is more important than design.
