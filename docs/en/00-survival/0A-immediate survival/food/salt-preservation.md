# Salt Preservation Guide

## Objective
To preserve meat and fish through the use of salt, relying on osmotic pressure to draw water out of bacterial cells and inhibit pathogen growth.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   **Salt:** Essential commodity for preservation.
*   **Temperature Control:** Curing methods (dry and brine) must occur at temperatures below 50°F (10°C) to prevent rapid bacterial multiplication.
*   **Fresh Water:** Required for desalting cured meat.

## Material
*   Meat (various cuts, fish fillets, whole chickens).
*   Salt (table salt, rock salt, sea water, salt deposits).
*   Water (for brining and desalting).
*   Flavoring agents (Sugar, black pepper, crushed juniper berries, garlic, bay leaves, thyme, rosemary, sage).
*   Containers (non-reactive, ceramic crock, food-grade plastic tub, wooden box).

## System
Salt preserves food by drawing water out of bacterial cells through osmosis. Above 20% salt concentration virtually all pathogens, including *Clostridium botulinum*, are inhibited. Salt also denatures proteins on the meat surface, creating a firm, dry barrier that resists bacterial colonization.

## Technology
*   **Osmosis:** The core principle where salt draws water out of cells.
*   **Dry Curing:** Physical packing of meat in salt.
*   **Brine Curing (Wet Cure):** Submerging meat in a salt solution.
*   **Cold Smoking:** Used in combination with salt curing to achieve the longest shelf life.

## Tools
*   Knife (for trimming meat and making slashes).
*   Measuring tools (for salt ratios).
*   Weights (rocks or plates for pressing meat in containers).
*   Container (for curing).
*   Test for saturation (potato or egg).

## Process
### Salt Curing Methods

**Method 1: Dry Curing (Rubbing)**
1.  **Preparation:** Trim excess fat, blood clots, and damaged tissue from the meat.
2.  **Application:** Rub salt aggressively into every surface and pack it into cuts, joints, and crevices.
3.  **Layering:** Spread a 1/2-inch bed of salt, layer the meat, and repeat.
4.  **Pressure:** Top with a weight (board/plate and rocks, 15-20 lbs for a full crock).
5.  **Drainage:** Tip the container daily to pour off accumulated liquid.
6.  **Re-salt:** Add fresh salt over exposed surfaces after 3-4 days.
7.  **Cure Time:** Varies by cut (e.g., Pork belly: 7-10 days; Ham: 2 days per pound of meat).

**Method 2: Brine Curing (Wet Cure)**
1.  **Brine Preparation:** Standard ratio is 1 pound of salt per 1 gallon of water (approx. 26% salinity).
2.  **Testing:** A raw potato or egg floats in properly concentrated brine.
3.  **Application:** Place meat in a container and pour cooled brine over it until fully submerged.
4.  **Weighting:** Weight the meat down to ensure full submersion.
5.  **Replacement:** Replace the brine entirely for cures longer than 5 days as it becomes diluted.

**Method 3: Salt Box Method (Quick Cure for Fish)**
1.  **Preparation:** Fillet fish or slice meat to a maximum thickness of 1/2-inch.
2.  **Coating:** Coat pieces generously with salt until white.
3.  **Stacking:** Stack pieces in a shallow container, salt between layers.
4.  **Time:** Cure for 6-12 hours (thin fillets) or 24 hours (thicker pieces).
5.  **Finishing:** Rinse off excess salt and hang to dry for 12-24 hours to form a pellicle.

### Flavor Addition
To improve flavor and appearance, add sugar (1 part sugar to 4 parts salt), black pepper, crushed juniper berries, garlic, bay leaves, thyme, rosemary, or sage.

### Desalting
1.  **Soak:** Soak cured meat in fresh water for 12-24 hours, changing the water every 4-6 hours.
2.  **Verification:** Taste a small piece; it should be pleasantly savory, not aggressively salty.

### Storage After Curing
| Storage Method | Temperature | Expected Shelf Life |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hung in cool, dry, dark space | 35-50°F (2-10°C) | 3-6 months |
| Packed in dry salt (buried) | Below 60°F (15°C) | 6-12 months |
| Salt-cured then cold-smoked | Below 60°F (15°C) | 6-18 months |
| Submerged in brine (maintained) | Below 50°F (10°C) | 3-6 months |
| Salt-cured then sealed in fat | Below 70°F (21°C) | 6-12 months |

### Signs of Spoilage in Cured Meat
*   **Mold:** Green, blue, or black mold requires discarding; white mold is generally harmless.
*   **Smell:** Sour, ammonia, or rotten smells indicate bacterial growth inside.
*   **Texture:** Slimy texture indicates bacterial colonization; the surface should be dry and firm.
*   **Brine Bubbles:** Gas bubbles in brine mean fermentation or bacterial gas production; replace the brine immediately.
*   **Discoloration:** Pink or gray discoloration spreading from the interior means salt did not penetrate fully.

## Notes
*   Salt is considered one of the most valuable commodities to stockpile because it does not spoil.
*   Road salt (halite) often contains anti-caking agents, sand, or ferrocyanide additives and requires thorough purification before use in food preservation.
*   The combination of salt curing followed by cold smoking is noted to produce the longest shelf life achievable without modern technology.
*   For thick cuts, the rule of thumb for dry curing is 2 days of curing per pound of meat at temperatures below 50°F.
