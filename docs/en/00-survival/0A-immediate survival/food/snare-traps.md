# Snare Traps | Survival Wiki

## Objective
> Part of Hunting and Trapping
> The snare is the single most important survival trap. It works silently, requires minimal materials, and catches food while you sleep. Master this one skill and you can feed yourself indefinitely.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
In a survival situation, you cannot afford to spend all day hunting. You need shelter, water, fire, and a dozen other tasks competing for daylight.

## Material
| Material | Best For | Availability | Durability |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Snare wire (brass/steel) | All game up to fox-sized | Salvage from fences, cables, picture frames | Excellent — holds shape, resists chewing |
| Paracord inner strands | Rabbits, squirrels | Paracord, cordage stashes | Good — strong but animals can chew through |
| Plant-fiber cordage | Small game only | Made from nettles, dogbane, inner bark | Fair — weakens when wet, breaks under strain |
| Fishing line (heavy) | Rabbits, birds | Tackle boxes, sporting goods | Good — nearly invisible but slippery knots |
| Electrical wire | All small game | Buildings, vehicles, appliances | Excellent — copper or aluminum holds shape well |

> **Wire vs. Cordage**
> Wire is always superior to cordage for snares. Wire holds its loop shape without support sticks, resists chewing, and tightens under tension without loosening. If you find any wire during scavenging, save it for snares before anything else.

## System
The snare solves the problem of needing continuous food acquisition by working 24 hours a day without the user.

## Technology
Mechanics are simple: a loop of cordage or wire sits in an animal’s path. The animal walks through, the loop tightens around its neck or body, and the animal cannot back out. There are no moving parts to fail, no complex triggers to carve, and the materials are available almost everywhere.

## Tools
*   Snare wire
*   Paracord
*   Plant-fiber cordage
*   Fishing line
*   Electrical wire
*   Stout wooden stake
*   Heavy branch or drag log
*   Pole (for Squirrel Pole Snare)

## Process
### The Simple Ground Snare (Passive)
1.  **Cut:** Cut wire or cordage 60-90 cm (2-3 feet) long for rabbit-sized game. For larger animals like foxes or raccoons, use 90-120 cm (3-4 feet).
2.  **Form Loop:** Form a small fixed loop at one end. For wire, twist the end around itself 3-4 times to create a permanent eye. For cordage, tie a small overhand loop or bowline.
3.  **Thread:** Thread the free end through the fixed loop to create the running noose. Pull it through—the noose should slide freely and tighten smoothly.
4.  **Size Noose:**
    *   Rabbit: 10 cm (4 inches) diameter
    *   Squirrel: 6-7 cm (2.5 inches) diameter
    *   Fox/raccoon: 18-20 cm (7-8 inches) diameter
5.  **Anchor:** Drive a stout wooden stake deep into the ground and tie or twist the wire around it. Alternatively, tie to a heavy log or fixed root. The anchor must hold against panicked pulling.
6.  **Set Height:** The bottom edge of the loop should be:
    *   Rabbit: 4 finger-widths (7-8 cm) above the ground
    *   Squirrel: 3 finger-widths (5-6 cm) above the ground
    *   Fox/raccoon: One hand-width (10-12 cm) above the ground
7.  **Support:** Wire holds its shape naturally. For cordage snares, push two small twigs into the ground on either side of the trail to hold the loop upright and open.

### The Drag Snare
*   **When to use:** When stakes cannot be driven (frozen ground, rock) or when the animal needs to move away from the trail.
*   **Steps:**
    1.  Follow Steps 1-6 above for the loop.
    2.  Instead of staking, tie the free end to a branch roughly 3-4 times the animal’s weight.
    3.  Place the drag branch loosely beside the trail.

### The Squirrel Pole Snare
1.  **Find Pole:** Find or cut a pole 2-3 meters (6-10 feet) long. Lean it against a tree where squirrel activity is observed.
2.  **Set Snares:** Set 4-6 small wire snares along the pole’s length, looping them so they stand upright, perpendicular to the pole surface. Size: 6-7 cm (2.5 inches) diameter.
3.  **Mechanism:** A squirrel running up or down the pole puts its head through a loop, panics, falls off, and the noose tightens, wrapping the wire around the pole.
> **Check Squirrel Poles Frequently:** Squirrels caught on poles are visible and attract raptors, foxes, and other predators. Check every few hours if possible.

### Setting Multiple Snares Efficiently
*   **Circuit Approach:**
    1.  Scout a large area (500m radius from camp) for trails, runs, and feeding signs.
    2.  Set snares along a walking loop you can check efficiently.
    3.  Number your snare sites mentally or mark them with small stone cairns.
    4.  Walk the circuit twice daily—dawn and dusk.
    5.  Reset sprung snares, replace damaged ones, relocate empty ones after 3 days.
*   **Expected Yield:**
    *   5 Snares: 0.5-1.0 animals daily (250-500 kcal)
    *   10 Snares: 1-2 animals daily (500-1,000 kcal)
    *   20 Snares: 2-4 animals daily (1,000-2,000 kcal)

### Common Mistakes
*   **Loop too large:** The animal passes through without triggering, or the noose catches the body instead of the neck. A body-caught animal can often chew free.
*   **Loop too high:** The animal walks under it. Get down on the ground and look along the trail from the animal’s perspective.
*   **Weak anchor:** The animal pulls the stake out and drags the snare away. Bury stakes deep and pack the soil firmly.
*   **Human scent:** Handle snares with gloves or muddy hands. Rub snares with local vegetation before setting. Approach trails from downwind.
*   **Setting on human trails:** Animals use narrow, low paths—tunnels through brush, runs along fence lines, paths between burrow and water—which are not the same as human footpaths.

### Maintenance and Reuse
*   Wire snares can be reused 5-10 times before the wire work-hardens and becomes brittle at bend points. Inspect the wire after each catch—if you see kinks or cracks, discard that section and make a new loop.
*   Cordage snares typically survive one catch and need to be replaced.
*   **Scent Management:** After catching an animal, blood scent on the snare can attract other animals. Do not clean the wire unless the smell is attracting predators to your trap line.

## Notes
- Trapping is a numbers game: Twelve snares give near certainty within 2 days.
- The Squirrel Pole is the most efficient multi-snare setup for forested environments.
- The primary key takeaway is that wire is superior to cordage and should be salvaged first.
- The location of the snare (height relative to the ground) is critical for success.
- The circuit approach and checking twice daily are essential for efficiency.
