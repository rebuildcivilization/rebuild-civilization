# Foraging Edible Plants

## Objective
The single most important foraging rule is: if you cannot positively identify a plant as safe, do not eat it — starvation takes weeks, but the wrong mushroom takes days and the wrong seed takes hours. The overall goal is to teach the reader how to identify safe food, avoid deadly mistakes, and test unknown plants systematically.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- Eyes and patience (most important)
- A knife or sharp edge for cutting and processing
- A container for collecting
- Water for soaking and leaching
- Fire for cooking
- Time — the Universal Edibility Test takes 24 hours

## Material
**Reliably Safe Plant Categories:**
- **Dandelion (*Taraxacum officinale*):** Entire plant edible (leaves, flowers, roots).
    - Leaves: Eat raw in salads (young leaves less bitter) or boil to reduce bitterness. High in vitamins A, C, and K.
    - Flowers: Eat raw, fry in batter, or make into tea.
    - Roots: Roast and grind for coffee substitute, or boil as a root vegetable.
- **Cattail (*Typha*):** Nearly every part is edible in some season.
    - Young shoots (spring): Peel outer layers, eat the white inner core raw or cooked (tastes like cucumber).
    - Green flower heads (early summer): Boil and eat like corn on the cob.
    - Pollen (summer): Shake yellow pollen from the spike into a bag, mix with flour, or eat directly (high in protein).
    - Roots/rhizomes (any season): Peel, then pound in water to release starch, which can be dried into flour.
- **Pine Trees (*Pinus*):** All species are edible in some form.
    - Pine needle tea: Steep fresh green needles in hot water for 10-15 minutes (high in vitamin C).
    - Inner bark (cambium): Scrape off and eat raw, dried, or ground into flour (calorie-dense).
    - Pine nuts: Seeds inside the cones (calorie-rich and high in fat).
- **Acorns (*Quercus* — Oak Trees):** One of the most calorie-dense wild foods.
    - Edible parts: Acorn meat (requires processing).

**Danger Signs to Avoid:**
- Milky or discolored sap (unless positively identified like dandelion).
- Umbrella-shaped flower clusters (umbels) (e.g., carrot/parsley family includes poison hemlock).
- Shiny or waxy leaves.
- Three-leaf groupings (e.g., poison ivy, poison oak).
- Bitter almond smell (may indicate cyanide compounds).
- Beans/seeds in pods from unknown plants.
- White or yellow berries (majority poisonous).
- Fine hairs or spines.

**Deadly Plants to Recognize:**
- **Poison hemlock (*Conium maculatum*):** Looks like wild carrot/parsley; purple-blotched hollow stem; kills by respiratory paralysis.
- **Water hemlock (*Cicuta*):** Grows near water; smells like carrot; most violently toxic plant in North America.
- **Deadly nightshade (*Atropa belladonna*):** Shiny black berries, bell-shaped flowers; causes hallucinations then death.
- **Castor bean (*Ricinus communis*):** Spiny seed pods; contains ricin.
- **Death camas (*Zigadenus*):** Looks like wild onion but has NO onion smell.
- **Foxglove (*Digitalis*):** Tall spikes of bell-shaped flowers; contains cardiac glycosides.

## System
**The Cardinal Rule:** If you cannot positively identify a plant as safe, do not eat it. Starvation takes weeks to kill you. The wrong mushroom takes days. The wrong seed takes hours. Override the impulse to eat based on knowledge.

**The Universal Edibility Test (UET):** A protocol for testing unknown plants when a calculated risk is taken. It takes about 24 hours.

**Reliably Safe Berry Categories (Rules of Thumb):**
- Blue/Black Berries: ~90% safe (Blueberries, blackberries, elderberries (cooked), mulberries).
- Red Berries: ~50% safe (Strawberries, raspberries, cranberries).
- White/Yellow Berries: ~90% dangerous (Baneberry, poison ivy berries, mistletoe).
- Aggregate berries (raspberry/blackberry shape): Almost always safe.

## Process
**Universal Edibility Test (UET) Protocol (24 Hours):

1. **Step 1:** Test only ONE plant part at a time (root, stem, leaves, flowers, fruit).
2. **Step 2:** Fast for 8 hours before starting the test.
3. **Step 3:** During the 8-hour fast, hold a small piece against the inside of your wrist for 15 minutes. Discard if skin reaction occurs (redness, rash, burning, itching, swelling).
4. **Step 4:** Touch a small piece to the corner of your lip. Wait 15 minutes. Discard if burning, tingling, or numbness occurs.
5. **Step 5:** Touch a small piece to your tongue. Wait 15 minutes. Discard if burning, tingling, or numbness occurs.
6. **Step 6:** Chew a small piece and hold it in your mouth for 15 minutes WITHOUT swallowing. Discard if extremely bitter, soapy, or causes burning/numbness.
7. **Step 7:** If no reaction after 15 minutes of chewing, swallow. Wait 8 hours. If nausea, vomiting, cramps, or discomfort occur, induce vomiting and drink plenty of water.
8. **Step 8:** If no reaction after 8 hours, eat a small handful of the same plant part prepared the same way. Wait another 8 hours.
9. **Step 9:** If still no reaction, this specific part of this specific plant, prepared in this specific way, is likely safe to eat in moderate quantities.

**Acorn Processing Process:
1. Collect ripe acorns (brown, falling from the tree); discard those with visible holes (weevils).
2. Shell the acorns and break the nutmeat into small pieces.
3. Leach the tannins: Place crushed acorn meat in a container of water. Change the water repeatedly (cold leaching via soaking or hot leaching via boiling water). The process continues until the acorn meat no longer tastes bitter.
4. Taste-test: when the acorn meat no longer tastes bitter, it is ready. Dry it and grind into flour, or roast the pieces.

**Processing Plants That Need It:
- Acorns: Must leach tannins.
- Elderberries: Must be cooked (raw cause nausea and vomiting).
- Fiddlehead ferns: Boil thoroughly (raw can cause food poisoning).
- Stinging nettles: Cook or dry completely (cooking destroys stinging compounds).
- Pokeweed young shoots: Must be boiled in 2-3 changes of water; mature plants are toxic.

## Notes
- Many animals tolerate toxins that kill humans (e.g., birds eating poison ivy berries), so trust human-verified identification.
- The UET is a LAST RESORT and is not foolproof; some toxins build up over time.
- When eating plants from roadsides or industrial areas, beware of heavy metals, pesticides, and pollutants.
- Dandelion leaves should be boiled to reduce bitterness.
- White oak acorns leach tannins slower than red oak acorns.

## Quick Reference Card
> Foraging Edible Plants — At a Glance
> **Rule zero:** When in doubt, DON’T eat it. Starvation takes weeks. Poisoning takes hours.
> **Danger signs — AVOID plants with:**
> - Milky/colored sap (unless positively ID’d like dandelion)
> - Umbrella-shaped flower clusters
> - Bitter almond smell
> - Three-leaf groupings
> - White or yellow berries
> **Safe bets (widespread, easy to identify):**
> - Dandelion — entire plant edible
> - Cattail — shoots, pollen, roots
> - Pine — needle tea, inner bark, pine nuts
> - Acorns — must leach tannins first
> - Aggregate berries (raspberry/blackberry shape) — almost always safe
> **Universal Edibility Test:** Skin → lip → tongue → chew (15 min each) → swallow → wait 8 hours → eat small amount → wait 8 hours. One plant part at a time.

> **Never eat wild mushrooms** unless trained by someone who already knows them.
