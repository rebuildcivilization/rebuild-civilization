# Fermentation

## Objective
Fermentation preserves food by cultivating salt-tolerant Lactobacillus bacteria, which produce lactic acid that drops the pH below 4.6 — the threshold below which Clostridium botulinum, E. coli, Salmonella, and Listeria cannot survive. The objective is to turn bacteria into allies, cultivating specific organisms that produce acids, alcohol, or gases — creating an environment where dangerous pathogens cannot survive. The result is food that lasts months, tastes better, and is more nutritious than the original.

## Dependencies and prerequisites

*   Salt (2–3% of vegetable weight for shredded vegetables, or 2-5% by weight of the food being fermented).
*   A non-reactive container (ceramic crock, glass jar, wooden bucket, food-grade plastic).
*   A weight to keep food submerged.
*   Vegetables, grains, or legumes.
*   Salt-tolerant Lactobacillus bacteria (which are already on the food).

## Material

*   **Vegetables**: Cabbage (sauerkraut), carrots, radishes, beets, turnips, green beans, cucumbers, onions, garlic, peppers, cauliflower, broccoli, wild greens, kohlrabi.
*   **Grains/Legumes**: Sourdough starter (flour and water), millet, sorghum, oats, corn, beans.

## System

Fermentation works by giving beneficial microbes (primarily *Lactobacillus*) an early advantage over harmful ones. Salt suppresses most bacteria but *Lactobacillus* is salt-tolerant. In the absence of competition, it multiplies rapidly, consuming sugars in the food and producing lactic acid. As acidity rises (pH drops below 4.6), the environment becomes hostile to virtually all dangerous organisms: *Clostridium botulinum*, *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, and *Listeria* — none can survive below pH 4.6. The food is preserved by ensuring the right ones win.

## Technology

*   **Key Bacteria**: *Lactobacillus*.
*   **Key Product**: Lactic acid (which drops pH below 4.6).
*   **Preservation Mechanism**: Creation of an acidic, anaerobic environment where dangerous pathogens cannot survive.
*   **Nutritional Enhancement**: Synthesis of B vitamins (B1, B2, B3, B6, B12, and folate); Vitamin C preservation; breakdown of phytic acid, increasing mineral bioavailability (iron, zinc, calcium).

## Tools

*   **Container**: Any non-reactive vessel (ceramic crock, glass jar, wooden bucket, food-grade plastic). Must avoid bare metal.
*   **Salt**: For the brine solution.
*   **Weight**: A clean rock, water-filled jar, or plate to keep food submerged.
*   **Knife**: For chopping and shredding.
*   **Cover**: Cloth, a loose lid, or leaves to keep insects out while allowing CO2 to escape. A proper airlock is necessary for airtight sealing.

## Process

### Method 1: Lacto-Fermented Vegetables (The Foundation)

1.  **Select vegetables**: Cabbage (sauerkraut), carrots, radishes, beets, turnips, green beans, cucumbers, onions, garlic, peppers, cauliflower, broccoli, wild greens, kohlrabi.
2.  **Clean but don’t sterilize**: Wash off dirt but don’t scrub aggressively or use soap. Use filtered or boiled-and-cooled water if possible.
3.  **Chop, shred, or slice**: Smaller pieces ferment faster. For sauerkraut, shred cabbage to 1/8-inch (3 mm) thickness. For pickles, leave cucumbers whole or halve them lengthwise.
4.  **Salt the vegetables**: Critical ratio based on desired outcome:
    *   2% salt: Light salt, fast fermentation (3-5 days), mild flavor (best for summer ferments eaten within 2-4 weeks).
    *   3% salt: Standard — best balance of speed and preservation (most vegetables, sauerkraut).
    *   5% salt: Slow fermentation, very long shelf life (winter storage, warm climates).
    *   *Practical Measurement Example*: 3% means about 1.5 teaspoons of fine salt (or 2 teaspoons of coarse salt) per pound of vegetables.
5.  **Massage and squeeze**: Work the salt into the chopped vegetables for 5-10 minutes to break cell walls and release liquid.
6.  **Pack into the container**: Push handfuls of salted vegetables into the container, pressing down hard after each addition to eliminate air pockets.
7.  **Submerge**: The liquid must cover the vegetables completely. Add small amounts of salt water (1 teaspoon salt per cup of water) until everything is covered by at least 1/2 inch (1 cm).
8.  **Weight down**: Place a weight on top to keep vegetables below the brine (e.g., a plate with a rock). 
9.  **Cover loosely**: Cloth secured with a rubber band or string, or a loose-fitting lid.

### Method 2: Whole Vegetable Fermentation (Pickles)

1.  Wash vegetables. Leave whole or halve — do not shred.
2.  **Make a brine**: 5% salt solution (3 tablespoons per quart of water).
3.  **Add flavor**: Garlic cloves, dill, peppercorns, mustard seed, grape leaves (grape leaves contain tannins that keep pickles crunchy).
4.  Pack vegetables into the container. Pour brine over them until submerged.
5.  Weight and cover as above.
6.  Ferment at 65-75°F for 1-3 weeks depending on size and desired sourness.
7.  Transfer to cold storage when flavor is right.

### Method 3: Grain and Legume Fermentation

*   **Sourdough**: Mix flour and water (equal parts by weight), leave loosely covered at room temperature. Wild yeast and bacteria colonize within 3-7 days. Feed daily (discard half, add equal parts fresh flour and water). The starter is ready to leaven bread after 5-7 days of feeding.
*   **Fermented porridge**: Soak grain (millet, sorghum, oats, corn) in water for 2-3 days at room temperature until sour. Drain and cook. Reduces phytic acid by 50-80%.
*   **Fermented legumes**: Soak beans for 24-48 hours, changing water once. This reduces gas-causing oligosaccharides and makes protein more available.

### Monitoring the Ferment

*   **Day 1-2**: Bubbles begin appearing; liquid may become slightly cloudy (CO2 from active fermentation).
*   **Day 3-5**: Vigorous bubbling; brine becomes distinctly cloudy. Taste: noticeably sour, salt is mellowing.
*   **Day 7-10**: Bubbling slows. Flavor is fully sour and complex. Ideal harvest point.
*   **Day 14+**: Fermentation continues slowly. Flavor deepens. Vegetables remain safe for months if kept submerged.

### Temperature Matters

| Temperature Range | Fermentation Speed | Flavor Profile | Best For |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 55-65°F (13-18°C) | Slow (2-4 weeks) | Complex, nuanced, mild | Long-term storage, best flavor |
| 65-75°F (18-24°C) | Medium (1-2 weeks) | Balanced sour and salt | Standard everyday ferments |
| 75-85°F (24-29°C) | Fast (3-7 days) | Sharp, aggressive sour | Quick ferments, warm climates |
| Above 85°F (29°C) | Too fast | Mushy texture, off-flavors | Avoid — texture deteriorates |

## Nutritional Benefits

*   **B Vitamins**: *Lactobacillus* synthesizes B1, B2, B3, B6, B12, and folate. Fermented cabbage contains significantly more B vitamins than raw cabbage.
*   **Vitamin C**: Preserved through fermentation while it degrades in dried or cooked food (prevented scurvy).
*   **Mineral Bioavailability**: Fermentation breaks down phytic acid, delivering more usable minerals (iron, zinc, calcium).
*   **Probiotics**: Live *Lactobacillus* colonize the gut, improving digestion and immune function.
*   **Digestibility**: Cell walls are partially broken down, proteins are pre-digested, and complex sugars are simplified.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| No bubbles after 3 days | Too cold, not enough salt, chlorinated water | Move to warmer spot; verify salt %; use non-chlorinated water |
| White film on surface | Kahm yeast — harmless | Skim off, ensure submersion, move to cooler location |
| Pink mold | Too warm, too little salt | Skim thoroughly; add salt to brine; move to cooler location |
| Soft/mushy texture | Too warm, fermented too long | Harvest sooner; add grape or oak leaves for tannins |
| Slimy brine | Normal in some ferments (okra, beans) | Usually safe if sour and good-smelling. Skim if excessive |
| Too salty after 2 weeks | Not enough fermentation time | Give it another week — bacteria consume some salt |

## Notes

*   **Key Takeaways**: Fermentation requires only salt (2-3% by vegetable weight), a container, and a weight to keep food submerged. The brine line is sacred: anything above it molds, everything below it is preserved by lactic acid and anaerobic conditions. Cooler temperatures (55-75°F) produce better flavor and texture.
*   **Sensory Check**: Sour and tangy is safe; putrid and slimy is not. Humans evolved to detect putrefaction at parts-per-billion concentrations.
*   **Equipment Specifics**: The non-reactive requirement for containers is critical as acid corrodes bare metal.
*   **Microbial Success**: The core principle is giving beneficial microbes an advantage to ensure they multiply and produce lactic acid, creating the hostile pH environment for pathogens.
