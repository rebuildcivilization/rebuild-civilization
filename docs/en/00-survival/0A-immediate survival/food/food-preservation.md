# Food Preservation

## Objective
Food preservation works by attacking what causes spoilage — bacteria, enzymes, oxidation, mold — through removing moisture (drying), adding antimicrobial agents (salt, smoke), excluding oxygen (fat sealing), lowering temperature (cold storage), or cultivating safe microbes to outcompete dangerous ones (fermentation).

**Why This Matters** Finding food is only half the problem. Without preservation, meat spoils in hours in warm weather, berries mold in days, and fish catch is inedible by tomorrow. These techniques let you stockpile food for lean seasons, carry rations on multi-day journeys, and feed groups larger than one hunt can serve.

**General Prerequisites:

* A container: clay pot, hollowed log, animal stomach, or any waterproof vessel
* Drying rack: green wood frame with cordage
* Smoking setup: frame + fire pit + cover (tarp, bark, or earth)
* Rendered animal fat (for pemmican)

**Specific Prerequisites (Examples):
* Water Purification: Boiling, filters, solar still
* Fire Making: Bow drill, flint, friction
* Fishing: Hooks, nets, traps, weirs

## Material
* Meat or Fish
* Vegetables (Cabbage, carrots, radishes, green beans, cucumbers, beets, wild greens)
* Salt (Boiled sea water crystals, mineral deposits, trade)
* Wood (Green wood for drying racks, hardwoods for smoking)
* Animal fat (Suet/hard fat around kidneys)
* Berries (Blueberries, cranberries, saskatoon berries, for pemmican)
* Container materials (Clay pot, glass jar, wooden vessel, bark containers, animal skin pouches)

## System
**Spoilage Causes:
1. Bacteria: Microorganisms eat food and produce toxins. Thrive in warm, moist, oxygen-rich environments (Most dangerous between 4°C–60°C / 40°F–140°F).
2. Enzymes: Natural chemicals in food causing browning, softening, and breakdown.
3. Oxidation: Exposure to air causes fats to go rancid and nutrients to degrade.
4. Mold and yeast: Fungi that colonize damp food surfaces, some producing dangerous toxins.

**Preservation Attack Methods:
* Removing moisture (Drying)
* Adding antimicrobial agents (Salt, Smoke)
* Excluding oxygen (Fat sealing)
* Lowering temperature (Cold storage)
* Cultivating safe microbes (Fermentation)

**Root Cellar System:
* Digging a hole at least 1.5 meters (5 feet) deep in a shaded area with good drainage.
* Lining walls with rocks, logs, or bark.
* Creating shelving from poles and planks (keeping food off the floor).
* Adding a roof of logs covered with earth (at least 30 cm / 12 inches of soil for insulation).
* Ensuring ventilation via two pipes or channels (one low for cold air in, one high for warm air out).

## Technology
**Preservation Techniques:
* Sun Drying
* Hot Smoking
* Cold Smoking
* Salt Curing
* Lacto-Fermentation
* Pemmican Making

**Core Scientific Principles:
* Osmosis: Salt draws moisture out of food.
* Microbial Action: Using beneficial bacteria (lactobacillus) to produce lactic acid.
* Thermal Control: Using low heat (cold smoking) or moderate heat (hot smoking) to control spoilage.
* Oxygen Exclusion: Fat sealing (excluding oxygen) to prevent rancidity.

## Tools
* Knife or sharp edge
* Mortar and pestle (for grinding meat into powder)
* Weights (to press moisture out)
* Containers (Clay pot, jars, vessels, bark containers)
* Fire source (for smoking or rendering fat)

## Process
**Method 1: Sun Drying (Jerky and Dried Fruit)**
1. Cut meat into thin strips (no thicker than 5 mm), cutting with the grain for jerky, against the grain for brittle jerky. Remove all visible fat.
2. Build a drying rack from green wood (two Y-shaped uprights with crossbars), elevating it at least 60 cm (2 feet) off the ground.
3. Rub a thin layer of salt into each strip if available.
4. Hang or lay strips in direct sunlight with good airflow, spacing them so they don’t touch.
5. Protect from insects (small smoky fire nearby or fine mesh).
6. Turn strips every few hours. Drying takes 1–3 days.
7. Test for doneness: properly dried jerky bends and cracks but doesn’t snap in half, feeling leathery, not squishy, with no moisture when pressed.
8. Store in a dry, cool place in a breathable container.

**Method 2: Hot Smoking**
1. Build a smoking structure: dig a small fire pit, build a tripod frame over it (about 1 meter / 3 feet tall), and drape a covering over the frame.
2. Start a fire in the pit and let it burn down to coals (no open flames).
3. Add green or damp wood chips (hardwoods like oak, hickory, maple, apple, cherry work best; avoid resinous softwoods).
4. Hang meat, fish, or other food on crossbars inside the structure, keeping food at least 60 cm (2 feet) above the heat source.
5. Maintain a steady stream of thick smoke for 4–8 hours. Internal temperature inside the structure should be warm enough to cook (60–80°C / 140–175°F). Add more damp wood as smoke thins out.
6. Food is done when it’s cooked through, firm, and has a dark, glossy surface.

**Method 3: Cold Smoking**
1. Separate smoke source from food: Dig a fire pit 2–3 meters (6–10 feet) away from the chamber, connecting them with a trench or tunnel covered with bark, rocks, or earth.
2. Build a smoking chamber (enclosed box, barrel, or frame).
3. Pre-cure the food with salt (critical): Rub salt generously into meat or fish and let it cure for 12–24 hours.
4. Hang the salt-cured food in the chamber.
5. Light a small, smoldering fire in the pit. Smoke should arrive at the chamber cool (below 30°C / 85°F);
6. Smoke continuously or in cycles for 1–5 days depending on food thickness.

**Method 4: Salt Curing**
1. Acquire salt (boiled sea water crystals or mineral deposits).
2. Prepare the food: Cut meat into portions no thicker than 5–8 cm (2–3 inches); gut and butterfly fish.
3. Ratio: Use salt equal to 10–20% of the meat’s weight (5 mm / 1/4 inch layer of salt per surface).
4. Layer the food with salt in a container, ensuring a salt cap on top.
5. Place a weight on top to press moisture out.
6. Liquid (brine) seeps out over 24–48 hours; drain it and add more salt to exposed surfaces.
7. After 5–7 days, the food is cured.

**Method 5: Lacto-Fermentation**
1. Choose vegetables (Cabbage is classic, but any vegetable works).
2. Chop or shred vegetables.
3. Salt the vegetables: Use about 2–3% salt by weight (1 tablespoon per 500g/1 lb), massage for 5 minutes.
4. Pack vegetables tightly into a container, pressing down hard.
5. Ensure all vegetables are submerged below the liquid (brine). Add salt water if needed.
6. Place a weight on top.
7. Cover with cloth but don’t seal airtight (allow CO2 to escape).
8. Store at cool room temperature (15–22°C / 60–72°F).
9. Taste after 3–7 days; sourness indicates proper fermentation.

**Method 6: Pemmican**
1. Make jerky first (Method 1): Dry meat completely until it snaps.
2. Grind the jerky into a powder or very fine shreds using two rocks as a mortar and pestle.
3. Render fat (tallow): Heat animal fat slowly until melted and solid bits float.
4. Optionally, grind dried berries and mix with the powdered jerky.
5. Mix: Combine meat powder (and berries) with warm liquid tallow in a ratio of roughly 1:1 by weight.
6. Press the mixture into molds (bark containers, pouches, or shaped bars/balls).
7. Store in a cool, dry place.

**Method 7: Root Cellar (Cold Storage)**
1. Dig a hole at least 1.5 meters (5 feet) deep in a shaded area with good drainage.
2. Line walls with rocks, logs, or bark.
3. Create shelving from poles and planks (keep food off the floor).
4. Add a roof of logs covered with earth (at least 30 cm / 12 inches of soil for insulation).
5. Ensure ventilation with two pipes/channels (low for cold air in, high for warm air out).
6. Store root vegetables in layers of dry sand or straw. Separate apples and pears from vegetables.

## Notes
*   **Critical Rule:** When in doubt, use more salt, more smoke, and thinner cuts. Over-preserved food tastes strong but is safe; under-preserved food can kill you.
*   **Fat Handling:** Fat must be rendered (purified) before pemmican making, as fat on jerky will go rancid.
*   **Wood Selection:** Always use hardwoods for smoking; softwoods (pine, spruce, cedar) produce resinous, acrid smoke.
*   **Cold Smoking Precaution:** Cold smoking without sufficient salt curing is dangerous because the low temperatures are warm enough to allow bacteria to grow.
*   **Fermentation Sealing:** Ferments must not be sealed airtight because they produce CO2 which needs to escape.
*   **Drying Storage:** Dried food reabsorbs moisture from the air; store in the driest place in a breathable but protected container.
*   **Shelf Life Summary (Quick Reference):**
    *   Sun drying: Weeks–months.
    *   Hot smoking: 1–2 weeks.
    *   Cold smoking: Months.
    *   Salt curing: Months–1 year+.
    *   Lacto-fermentation: Months.
    *   Pemmican: Years.
    *   Root cellar: 3–6 months for root vegetables.
