# Harvest Techniques

## Objective
The goal of proper harvest technique is threefold:
1. Maximize yield — Get the most usable food from each plant.
2. Preserve the plant — Leave enough behind for regrowth so the patch produces again.
3. Maintain quality — Handle food so it lasts as long as possible after harvest.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
Knowing which plants are edible is only half the challenge; the other half is determining the harvest technique. Proper technique requires understanding timing, maturity cues, and using appropriate tools.

## Material
*   Leafy Greens (Dandelion, Plantain, Nettle, Lamb’s Quarters)
*   Roots and Tubers (Burdock, Cattail, Wild Carrot, Jerusalem Artichoke)
*   Nuts and Seeds (Acorns, walnuts, chestnuts, sunflower seeds, grasses)
*   Berries and Fruit (Blackberry, raspberry, crabapples, wild plums)
*   Bark and Cambium (for calories or material)

## System
**Core Principle: Cutting vs. Pulling**
*   **Cut rather than pull:** A clean cut at the base leaves the root system intact, allowing the plant to regrow.
*   **Pulling issues:** Pulling uproots plants, destroying the root system and killing the organism, and disturbing soil.
*   **Clean Cuts:** Use a sharp edge, cut at a slight angle to prevent water pooling, and for woody plants, cut just outside the branch collar.

## Technology
**Timing Cues:**
*   **General Harvest Time:** Harvest in the morning after dew has dried but before the midday heat for peak crispness.
*   **Seasonal Window:** Most plants have a period of 2-6 weeks when a given part is at its best.
*   **Maturity Stages:**
    *   Leafy greens: Young and tender, before flowering.
    *   Roots/tubers: After the plant dies back (autumn/winter).
    *   Flowers: Just opened, before they start to wilt.
    *   Fruits/berries: Fully colored and separate easily from the stem.
    *   Seeds/nuts: When they fall naturally or the seed head is dry and brown.
    *   Bark (cambium): Spring, when sap is flowing and the cambium layer is thick and moist.

## Tools
| Tool | Use | How to Make |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Knife/sharp edge | Cutting greens, bark, stems | Flaked stone, broken glass, sharpened bone |
| Digging stick | Roots and tubers | Fire-hardened sharpened branch |
| Carrying basket | Transporting harvest without crushing | Woven from flexible branches or bark strips |
| Cloth/hide | Catching fallen nuts, carrying greens | Salvaged fabric or processed animal skin |
| Forked stick | Reaching high branches for fruit/nuts | Cut a naturally forked branch |

## Process
### General Harvesting Process
1. **Timing:** Harvest in the morning after dew has dried but before the midday heat.
2. **Technique:** Always cut rather than pull.
3. **Quality:** Use a sharp edge and cut at a slight angle.

### Specific Plant Harvesting Processes
**Leafy Greens:**
1. Identify outer, mature leaves first.
2. Cut or pinch each leaf at its base.
3. Take no more than one-third of the leaves from any individual plant.
4. For nettles, grasp the stem from the bottom and strip leaves upward.
5. Place harvested greens in a container immediately.

**Roots and Tubers:**
1. Loosen soil around the base using a digging stick (60-90 cm long).
2. Work the soil in a circle, levering outward, or dig a trench for deep roots.
3. Pull gently straight up while working soil away from the sides.
4. Brush off soil immediately; do not wash until ready to process.
5. For cattail rhizomes, cut the connecting rhizome rather than pulling the entire network.

**Nuts and Seeds:**
1. Ground-fallen nuts (acorns, walnuts): Collect from the ground.
2. Seed heads: Cut the entire seed head and transport it.
3. Pine cones: Collect closed or barely-open cones.

**Berries and Fruit:**
1. Pick berries one at a time.
2. For aggregate berries, a ripe berry separates with a gentle tug.
3. Collect into a rigid container.
4. Keep harvested berries out of direct sunlight.
5. Process or eat within 24-48 hours in warm weather.

**Bark and Cambium:**
1. Never ring a tree; leave at least two-thirds of the circumference intact.
2. Make two horizontal cuts and two vertical cuts to define a rectangular section, then peel.
3. Scrape the moist, soft cambium layer from the inside of the bark with a knife.
4. Take bark from branches rather than the main trunk when possible.

### Post-Harvest Handling
1. **Sort Immediately:** Remove damaged, insect-eaten, or wilted items.
2. **Separate:** Keep different plant types separate, especially ethylene-producing fruits, to slow spoilage.
3. **Store:** Store in a cool, shaded, ventilated location.
4. **Prioritize Processing:** Process perishables first (leafy greens), or dry them immediately.
5. **Washing:** Wash roots and tubers only when ready to eat or process, as the dirt layer protects against mold.

## Notes
*   The core takeaway is that harvest technique determines survival: 'Cut rather than pull — leave roots intact so plants regrow.'
*   Bark harvesting must be justified by caloric value, and no more than one-third of any single tree’s bark surface should be taken.
*   Post-harvest sorting and shading are crucial; one rotting item can spoil a whole container.
*   The best digging stick is a hardwood branch about wrist-thickness, 60-90 cm long, sharpened to a chisel edge.
*   The dirt layer on roots and tubers should be retained during storage as it protects against mold and decay.
