# Stave Selection

## Objective
To provide a comprehensive guide on choosing the right wood and preparing it correctly for bow making.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Knowledge of wood properties (tension vs. compression).
*   Tools for cutting and splitting (wedges, baton).
*   Tools for sealing ends (wax, pine pitch, hide glue, or mud).
*   Assessment skills for evaluating stave quality.

## Material
### Wood Species Guide

**Tier 1 — Outstanding Bow Woods**
| Species | Key Advantage | Density (kg/m³)
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Yew (Taxus baccata / Taxus brevifolia) | Two layers: sapwood resists tension, heartwood resists compression.
| Osage Orange (Maclura pomifera) | Extremely dense, elastic, and rot-resistant.

**Tier 2 — Excellent Bow Woods**
| Species | Key Advantage
| :--- | :--- |
| Hickory (Carya spp.) | Extremely strong in tension (backs rarely fail).
| Ash (Fraxinus spp.) | Forgiving, strong enough for hunting bows, easy to work with.
| Elm (Ulmus spp.) | Extremely tough, interlocked grain resists catastrophic failure.

**Tier 3 — Serviceable Bow Woods**
| Species | Notes
| :--- | :--- |
| Maple (Acer spp.) | Decent bows, slightly brittle.
| Black Locust (Robinia pseudoacacia) | Dense, strong, extremely rot-resistant.
| Birch (Betula spp.) | Yellow birch serviceable; paper birch marginal.
| Hazel (Corylus spp.) | Makes acceptable light bows.

**Emergency / Avoid Woods**
| Wood | Problem
| :--- | :--- |
| Willow | Too flexible, won’t store energy, bow feels “mushy”.
| Poplar / Aspen | Too soft, compression failure immediate.
| Pine / Spruce / Fir | Weak in tension, splinters on back (only Douglas fir is marginal).
| Alder | Soft, brittle, poor in both tension and compression.
| Sycamore | Interlocked grain tears out; difficult to follow a single ring.

### Stave Quality Evaluation

**The Grain Test**
*   Tight, even rings: Good (consistent density).
*   Wide rings (2-4 mm): Good (fast growth, tougher fibers for ring-porous species).
*   Very narrow rings (< 1 mm): Fine for yew/osage, poor for ash/hickory.
*   Uneven rings: Workable but requires careful tillering.

**The Flex Test**
*   Good: Bends smoothly through length.
*   Problem: Hinges at one spot (likely knot or grain irregularity).
*   Reject: Makes crackling sounds (internal damage).

**Checking for Defects**
| Defect | Severity | Work Around?
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Small pin knot | Minor | Yes (leave extra wood around it).
| Large knot (> 1 cm) | Major | Only if near the handle.
| Twist in grain | Moderate | Steam-straighten before tillering.
| Checking (surface cracks) | Depends | Shallow checks can be scraped away; deep checks = reject.
| Insect holes | Minor to major | Superficial holes on bark side are fine; holes through sapwood = reject.
| Compression damage | Major | Reject unless very mild.

### Harvesting and Cutting Process

**When to Harvest**
*   Ideal: Winter (low moisture, tighter bark, faster seasoning).
*   Acceptable: Summer and fall.
*   Avoid: Spring (sap rising, wood waterlogged).

**Selecting the Tree Characteristics**
1.  Straight trunk or branch (minimum 180 cm / 6 feet).
2.  Diameter: 10-20 cm (for splitting) or 5-8 cm (for whole sapling).
3.  No visible knots in the section to be used.
4.  No spiral grain (bark furrows should run straight).
5.  Healthy, living wood (no dead branches, fungal growths, woodpecker holes).
6.  Open-grown trees (grow faster, produce wider rings).

**Cutting and Splitting**
1.  Fell the tree or cut the branch cleanly.
2.  Cut to length: 180-200 cm (72-80 inches), slightly longer than the intended bow.
3.  Split immediately if trunk > 10 cm diameter, using wedges and a baton.
4.  Seal the ends with wax, pine pitch, hide glue, or mud to prevent cracking.

**Whole Saplings**
*   A sapling 5-7 cm in diameter can be used whole, but limits performance to lighter bows (30-40 lbs).

**Seasoning (Drying)**
*   **Goal:** Remove moisture until equilibrium (typically 8-12% moisture content).
*   **Method:** Store in a cool, dry, shaded location, elevated off the ground with air circulation, and weight or tie the stave straight.

**Emergency Bows (Green Wood)**
*   Performance reduction: 30-40% less draw weight; significant string follow; shorter lifespan.
*   Best for: Ash and hickory.

### Storing Staves
*   Store horizontally on a rack or vertically leaning against a wall.
*   Label with species and harvest date if possible.
*   Rotate every few months to prevent warping.
*   Keep away from rodents.
*   A stave improves with age up to about 5 years before becoming too brittle.

## System
The underlying principle of wood choice is the requirement that the stave must resist both tension (back stretching) and compression (belly compressing) equally. The ideal wood possesses a high modulus of rupture, moderate density, and straight, even grain to distribute stress uniformly.

## Technology
*   **Modulus of Rupture:** Resistance to breaking.
*   **Density:** Ability to store energy without excessive weight.
*   **Grain Structure:** Straight, even grain ensures uniform stress distribution.
*   **Moisture Content:** Seasoning to the equilibrium point (8-12% MC) to prevent permanent set (string follow).

## Tools
*   Stone, antler, or hardwood wedges (for splitting).
*   Baton (for splitting).
*   Wax, pine pitch, hide glue, or mud (for sealing ends).
*   Light source (for grain testing).

## Process
1.  **Selection:** Choose wood based on required tension/compression characteristics.
2.  **Harvesting:** Select straight, open-grown, healthy trees in winter.
3.  **Cutting:** Cut the wood to length (180-200 cm).
4.  **Splitting:** Split the stave immediately if the trunk is large.
5.  **Sealing:** Seal the ends to ensure the end-grain dries fastest.
6.  **Seasoning:** Dry the stave in a cool, dry, shaded, well-ventilated area until moisture content reaches equilibrium.
7.  **Evaluation:** Test grain, flex, and check for defects before tillering.

## Notes
*   **Yew Toxicity:** All parts are highly poisonous except the red berry flesh; seeds are lethal. Wear gloves, avoid inhaling sawdust, and keep wood away from food preparation.
*   **Back Priority:** The back of the stave must have one continuous growth ring, undamaged.
*   **Emergency Use Caveat:** Green wood bows perform poorly, require over-sizing, and have a shorter lifespan, though ash and hickory tolerate green use best.
*   **Storage:** Staves should be rotated every few months and kept away from rodents.
