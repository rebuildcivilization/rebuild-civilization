# Filleting Technique

## Objective
To remove the boneless meat from a fish in clean slabs, producing easy-to-eat portions free of bones, and maximizing meat yield.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- A sharp knife is essential; a dull blade tears flesh, wastes meat, and risks injury.
- A flat, stable cutting surface (flat rock, split log, or piece of board).
- Clean water for rinsing.
- A container for the fillets and another for scraps.

## Material
- Round fish (trout, bass, perch, catfish, carp, salmon, walleye, and similar round-bodied species).
- Fillets (target thickness: 5 mm (1/4 inch) for drying, 10–15 mm (1/2 inch) for smoking).
- Collar, belly flap, and skeleton (to be saved for broth or meat).

## System
- **Round Fish Filleting:** Involves cutting diagonally behind the pectoral fin down to the spine, rotating the blade toward the tail lying flat along the backbone, and using long smooth strokes to slice the fillet free while riding the blade on top of the rib bones.
- **Flat Fish Filleting:** Yields four fillets; requires cutting along the center line and slicing outward along the rib bones.

## Technology
- **Filleting Technique (Round Fish):**
    1. Position the fish on its side, head to the non-dominant side.
    2. First cut: Place the knife behind the pectoral fin and cut diagonally down to the spine, stopping at the bone.
    3. Turn the blade toward the tail, lying flat along the backbone.
    4. Slice from head to tail with long, smooth strokes, riding the blade on the rib bones.
    5. Rib separation options: Cut through the ribs (faster) or cut around the ribs (to separate meat from bone, sacrificing a thin strip of belly meat).
    6. Flip and repeat for the other side to yield two fillets and the skeleton.
- **Bone Removal:**
    - **Rib Bones:** Slide the knife under the rib bones at a slight angle and cut along the ribs to lift them away as a group.
    - **Pin Bones:** Can be removed using pliers/tweezers, or by making two angled cuts along the pin bone line to remove the narrow strip.
- **Skinning:** Involves making a small cut at the tail end to create a skin tab, holding it firmly, and gliding the knife blade between the flesh and skin to separate the fillet in one piece.

## Tools
- A sharp knife (non-negotiable).
- Optional tools for pin bones: pliers, tweezers, or two flat sticks used as pincers.

## Process
**Standard Round Fish Filletting Steps:**
1. **Position the fish:** Lay the fish on its side, head to the non-dominant side, belly facing you.
2. **First cut:** Place the blade behind the pectoral fin and cut diagonally downward toward the head until the spine is reached (stop at the bone).
3. **Turn the blade:** Rotate the blade toward the tail lying flat along the backbone.
4. **Slice toward the tail:** Use long, smooth strokes to slice along the spine, riding the blade on the rib bones.
5. **Pass over the rib cage:** Decide whether to cut through the ribs or cut around them to separate meat from bone.
6. **Complete the cut:** Continue slicing to separate the fillet completely. Turn the fish and repeat Steps 2–6.
7. **Removing Rib Bones:** Lay skin-side down, feel for bones in the belly section, and cut along the ribs to lift them.
8. **Removing Pin Bones:** Run a fingertip along the fillet; use tools to grip and pull, or make two angled cuts to remove the pin bone strip.
9. **Skinning:** Make a cut at the tail end to create a skin tab, grip it, and glide the blade between flesh and skin.

**Flat Fish Filleting Steps:**
1. **Position the fish:** Lay the fish dark-side up.
2. **Center cut:** Cut along the center line from head to tail to find the spine.
3. **Peel outward:** Angle the blade toward the outer edge from the center cut and slice outward along the rib bones to peel the fillet.
4. **Repeat:** Return to the center line and repeat on the other side for the two bottom fillets.
5. **Handle Gently:** Flat fish fillets are thin and delicate.

**Preparing Fillets for Preservation:**
- Cut fillets into uniform strips: 5 mm (1/4 inch) thick for drying or 10–15 mm (1/2 inch) for smoking.
- Cut with the grain for drying strips to ensure they hold together.
- Remove skin for drying (to trap moisture); leave skin on for smoking (to hold the piece together).
- Rinse fillets in clean water and pat dry before placing on racks.

**When to Fillet vs. Cook Whole:**
| Situation | Best Approach |
| :--- | :--- | 
| Immediate eating, small fish | Cook whole (gutted) — less waste |
| Drying or smoking | Fillet — uniform thin slices dry evenly |
| Feeding children or elderly | Fillet — no bone hazard |
| Large fish (over 1 kg / 2 lbs) | Fillet — easier to portion and cook evenly |
| Very small fish (under 15 cm / 6 inches) | Cook whole — not enough meat to fillet efficiently |
| Making fish broth | Cook whole, then pick meat off bones |

## Notes
- **Key Takeaways:** A sharp knife is essential, and sharpening should occur before every session. Wasted meat occurs when cutting too far from the spine.
- **Yield Expectation:** Realistic yield expectations are 30–35% for small fish, 35–45% for medium fish, and 40–50% for large fish.
- **Maximizing Yield Tips:**
    - Keep the blade flat against the backbone when cutting along the spine.
    - Do not discard the belly meat (belly flap) as it is fatty and calorie-dense.
    - The collar (triangular piece behind the head) and the rack (skeleton) still contain significant usable meat and broth material.
- **Improvised Techniques:** If a blade is inadequate, use a thinner blade (e.g., broken glass or sharpened shell) for rib work, or score and peel instead of a continuous cut.
- **Skin Tip:** Leave the skin on for grilling, smoking, and pan-frying as it helps the fillet stay together during cooking.
