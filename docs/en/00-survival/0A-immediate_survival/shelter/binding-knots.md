# Binding Knots

## Objective
To cover the three binding knots—the square knot, clove hitch, and constrictor knot—and teach the user how to choose the correct binding knot based on the required application.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
Mastery of the three binding knots is required to effectively apply them.

## Material
*   Rope
*   Bandages
*   Firewood or sticks
*   Poles or cylindrical objects
*   Sacks or bags
*   Soft materials (for constrictor knot application, e.g., green wood, rubber tubing, flesh)

## System
The system revolves around friction, compression, and locking mechanisms used to hold objects together.

## Technology
The binding knots function by wrapping around an object or bundle and holding it tight through friction and compression. Binding knots differ from bends (which join two ropes) or hitches (which attach a rope to a fixed object) because they work by squeezing.

## Tools
*   Hands (for tying and testing)
*   A weighing mechanism (for testing the clove hitch)

## Process
### Decision Flowchart
1. Are you binding a flat bundle or bandage? Use the **square knot**.
2. Are you binding around a pole or cylinder? Go to step 3.
3. Do you need to remove the binding later? Use the **clove hitch** (backed up with a half hitch).
4. Is this a permanent or semi-permanent binding? Use the **constrictor knot**.
5. Are you starting or finishing a lashing? Use the **clove hitch**.
6. Are you binding something under active tension while tying (wound dressing, compressed bundle)? Use the **surgeon’s knot** (square knot variant).

### Practice Exercise
Practice cycling through three tasks until completion in under 90 seconds:
1. Bundle three sticks with a square knot. Test: the bundle should not loosen when shaken vigorously.
2. Tie a clove hitch to a vertical pole, then add a half-hitch backup. Test: hang a 2 kg weight from the standing part for 60 seconds — no slip.
3. Tie a constrictor knot around a stick or dowel. Test: try to slide the knot along the stick. It should not move.

### Key Takeaways
*   The square knot is for flat bundles.
*   The clove hitch is for poles.
*   The constrictor knot is for permanent grip.
*   The square knot must always follow the sequence: right over left, then left over right.
*   Always back up a clove hitch with a half hitch for critical applications.
*   The constrictor knot is permanent on soft materials; use the slipped version for temporary bindings.

## Comparison Table
| Feature | Square Knot | Clove Hitch | Constrictor | 
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | 
| Tying speed | Fast (3 sec) | Fast (3 sec) | Moderate (5-8 sec) | 
| Grip strength | Moderate | Moderate | Very high | 
| Flat profile | Excellent | Good | Good | 
| Works on cylinders | Poor (slips) | Excellent | Excellent | 
| Easy to untie | Yes (usually) | Yes | Difficult to impossible | 
| Slips under shifting load | Yes (capsizes) | Yes (without backup) | No | 
| Best surface type | Flat bundles, fabric | Round poles, posts | Any shape | 
| Use as permanent binding | No | No | Yes | 

## Notes
*   **Square Knot Verification:** The finished knot should have two interlocked loops lying flat, like two links of a chain. A spiral indicates a granny knot.
*   **Square Knot Variations:** The Surgeon’s knot adds an extra turn in the first half-knot for higher grip strength under tension.
*   **Clove Hitch Countermeasure:** In wet conditions, add an extra wrap (making it a round turn and two half hitches) for security.
*   **Constrictor Knot Warning:** On soft materials, the constrictor can become permanent. Use the slipped constrictor for temporary bindings to ensure a quick-release option.
*   **Wet Conditions:** Wet rope makes the square knot tighter initially, but it may loosen as the rope dries.
