# Rope Strength Testing | Survival Wiki

## Objective
To test handmade rope, hang it from an overhead anchor, add weight incrementally — rocks of roughly 2–5 kg each work well — and record the total load at which the rope breaks; that figure is the breaking strength.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Handmade rope to test.
*   A sturdy overhead anchor (tree branch, beam).
*   A container to fill with weight (bag, bucket, or net of rocks).
*   Understanding of load terminology.

## Material
*   Handmade rope (made from various natural fibers).
*   Rocks (roughly 2–5 kg each) for deadweight testing.
*   Plant fibers (inner bark, stinging nettle, cattail leaf, dried grass, yucca leaf).
*   Rawhide strip.
*   Sinew.

## System
The system revolves around break and load testing to determine if handmade cordage can hold the required weight before it is used for shelter lines, snares, or hauling.

## Technology
*   **Breaking Strength:** The maximum load the rope can hold before it snaps.
*   **Working Load:** The maximum load that should be applied in use (typically 1/5 to 1/4 of breaking strength for general use, one-tenth for life safety).
*   **Shock Load:** A sudden, dynamic force (falling object, jerked line), which can be 2x to 10x the static weight.
*   **Safety Factor:** The ratio between breaking strength and working load (5:1 is standard; 10:1 for life safety).
*   **Creep:** Gradual stretching under sustained load that weakens fibers over time.

## Tools
*   A container for weight.
*   A test log (scratching surface like bark or charcoal on a rock).
*   Measuring tools (implied for calculating loads and testing increments).

## Process
### Deadweight Method (The Simplest and Most Reliable Field Test)
1.  **Building the Test Rig:** Find or create a solid overhead anchor point at least 1.5 meters (5 feet) above the ground (e.g., a living tree branch at least 10 cm in diameter).
2.  **Attachment 1:** Tie one end of the test rope to the anchor using a trusted knot (bowline or timber hitch).
3.  **Attachment 2:** Tie the other end to the weight container using the same type of knot planned for real application.
4.  **Weight Increments:** Prepare weight increments, using rocks of roughly 2 to 5 kg, or a known-weight container of water (1 liter = 1 kg) for precision.
5.  **Running the Test:** Stand clear of the drop zone. Add weight in increments of roughly 10% of the expected working load (e.g., 1 kg steps if the working load is 10 kg).
6.  **Observation:** After each increment, wait 30 seconds and watch for: audible creaking or popping, visible stretching or thinning, twist unwinding at splices, or the rope rotating/untwisting.
7.  **Failure:** Continue adding weight until the rope breaks. Record the total weight at failure (breaking strength).
8.  **Calculation:** Divide the breaking strength by the safety factor to get the working load.

### Lever Method (When Overhead Anchor is Unavailable)
1.  **Anchor:** Anchor one end of the test rope to a solid, immovable object at ground level.
2.  **Pole Attachment:** Tie the other end to a sturdy pole (1.5 to 2 meters long) at the 15 cm mark from one end.
3.  **Fulcrum:** Plant the short end of the pole against a fulcrum (rock, log) right next to the anchor.
4.  **Test:** Push down on the long end of the pole. A 2-meter pole with the rope attached 15 cm from the fulcrum yields roughly a 13:1 mechanical advantage.
5.  **Increase Force:** Increase the push force gradually until the rope breaks or the target test load is reached.

### Non-Destructive Testing
*   **The Hand Pull Test:** Grip the rope at two points 30 cm apart and pull as hard as possible. A full-effort hand pull should be sustained without audible fiber breakage or visible deformation.
*   **The Twist Inspection:** Run the rope through the fingers slowly to check for inconsistencies (lumps, thin spots, loose/tight twists). Grip on either side of each splice point and pull firmly to check for slippage.
*   **The Abrasion Check:** Rub a section of rope vigorously back and forth over a rough surface for 10 to 15 strokes. Check if outer fibers fuzz up dramatically or if the rope loses visible diameter.

### Degradation and Re-Testing
*   **Degradation Factors:** UV exposure (loses roughly 10% strength per week), moisture cycling (loosens the twist structure), abrasion (worn outer fibers), and biological attack (mold, mildew, insects).
*   **Re-test Schedule:** For rope in continuous outdoor service, destructively test a sample section every 5 to 7 days. If the breaking strength falls below the required safety margin, replace the rope.
*   **Storage:** For rope stored dry and out of sunlight, re-test monthly. Properly stored cordage can retain 80% or more of its strength for several months.

## Notes
*   The safety factor is non-negotiable.
*   Never load handmade rope to more than 20% of its tested breaking strength for sustained use.
*   Shock loads multiply static weight dramatically (e.g., a 5 kg object falling 1 meter generates 50+ kg of force).
*   Wet plant-fiber rope loses 10 to 30% of its strength.
*   Rope left in direct sun loses roughly 10% of its strength per week.
*   The safety factor for shelter lines is 5:1, and for hauling/lifting objects is 5:1 (or 10:1 for climbing aids).
*   A test log is invaluable for determining local fiber strength and rope longevity.
