# Debris Shelters

## Objective
Debris shelters aim to create a heat-efficient shelter by trapping dead air in a thick shell around a small interior space, thereby blocking wind and shedding rain when layered properly.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
Before building, assess what is available within 50 meters of the site. The type and quantity of available materials determine which debris shelter design will work best.

## Material
### What You Need
| Material | Purpose | Minimum Quantity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Ridgepole | Spine of the shelter | 1 sturdy branch, 3 m long |
| Rib sticks | Frame the sides | 30-50 branches, arm-length |
| Lattice material | Holds debris on frame | Handfuls of thin flexible sticks or brush |
| Debris (leaves, needles, grass) | Insulation and waterproofing | Enough to fill 15-20 large garbage bags |
| Ground debris | Floor insulation | Enough to create a 15 cm thick bed |

### Material Quality
- **Best** — Dry, dead leaves (oak and beech hold up especially well), dry pine needles, dry grass, dry ferns (trap air effectively and resist compaction).
- **Good** — Mixed forest litter, bark strips, small dry twigs mixed with leaves.
- **Acceptable** — Green leaves and ferns (heavier, less insulating, but better than nothing); moss (holds moisture but insulates when compressed).
- **Poor** — Wet leaves (pack flat, no insulation), rotten wood (crumbles and holds water), bare soil.

> **Warning:** Avoid using poison ivy, poison oak, or stinging nettle as building material, as handling them causes severe skin reactions.

## System
### The Principle
A debris shelter works by creating a thick shell of dead plant material around a small air space. The debris traps dead air (which insulates), sheds rain (when thick enough and properly layered), and blocks wind. The trapped air is the insulator, not the material itself. This principle mirrors a bird’s nest, a bear’s den, or a pile of blankets.

## Technology
The core technology relies on trapping still air for insulation and utilizing layered, thick plant material for waterproofing and wind blockage.

## Tools
- **Required:** None, as the core construction uses no tools or cordage.
- **Implied:** A measuring tool to determine material quantity (to ensure enough debris is used).

## Process
### Debris Hut (Primary Design)
1. **Select and test the ridgepole:** Find a straight, sturdy branch approximately 3 meters long (about 1.5 times your height) with at least 8 cm in diameter at the thick end. Test it by propping one end on a rock or stump at waist height and pressing down hard in the middle. It must flex less than 10-15 cm or crack.
2. **Set the ridgepole:** Prop the thick end on a solid support (stump, forked tree, large rock, or a tripod of three sticks lashed together). The supported end should be about 90-100 cm off the ground. Test stability by pushing the ridgepole side to side and secure it if it rolls off.
3. **Add rib sticks:** Lean sticks along both sides of the ridgepole at 45-60 degree angles. Use sticks roughly arm-length and finger- to wrist-thickness, placing them 5-10 cm apart. The interior width should be between 60-70 cm at the shoulders.
4. **Create the lattice layer:** Lay thinner sticks, brush, and small branches horizontally across the ribs. Weave flexible branches through the ribs where possible to prevent debris from falling through.
5. **Pile on debris:** Start from the ground and work upward like shingling a roof. Each layer must overlap the layer below so rain runs downward and off. Target thickness: **30 cm minimum in mild weather, 60 cm in cold weather.** Pack debris loosely; fluffy debris is better than compacted debris.
6. **Insulate the floor:** Fill the interior floor with at least 15 cm of dry debris, preferably 30 cm. This is critical, as the ground draws heat from the body.
7. **Build a door plug:** Options include a backpack stuffed into the opening, a bundle of debris wrapped in clothing, a woven mat of branches and leaves, or loose debris pulled in behind the body.
8. **Enter and test:** Crawl in feet first, pulling debris in to fill gaps. The interior should feel snug.

### A-Frame Debris Shelter
- The ridgepole is supported at both ends (between two trees, two stumps, or two forked sticks) at 60-90 cm height.
- Ribs lean against both sides along the full length.
- Entry is from one end.
- The A-frame uses roughly 40% more material than a debris hut for the same space due to the wider cross-section and two open ends to block.

### Round Debris Hut (Wickiup)
- Used for sitting up inside (injuries, fire tending, multi-person shelter).
- Drive 8-12 flexible poles into the ground in a circle about 2 meters in diameter.
- Bend the tops inward and lash or weave them together at the apex.
- Weave horizontal branches through the poles as lattice.
- Pile debris thickly over the entire structure.
- Leave a small entrance and a smoke hole at the top if using a small interior fire.

### Maintenance
- **Daily:** Repack any debris that has fallen off or been blown away.
- **After rain:** Check the interior for leaks and add debris where water penetrated. Let the interior air out if damp.
- **Every 2-3 days:** Refresh ground insulation by removing and fluffing the floor debris, or adding a fresh layer on top.
- **Weekly:** Inspect the ridgepole and ribs for signs of cracking, bending, or rot, and replace any weak ones.

## Notes
- The biggest mistake is underestimating the required debris volume; double the estimate if in doubt.
- Ground insulation (15-30 cm floor debris) is as critical as roof insulation because the ground steals more heat than the air.
- The most heat-efficient design is the debris hut because its small interior maximizes the effect of trapped body heat.
- Structural failure points include ridgepole collapse (due to thin/rotten poles), wind stripping debris (due to insufficient lattice), and rain penetration (due to thin layering).
- Building just big enough for the body is essential; every extra cubic centimeter of interior air requires body heat to warm it.
