# Lean-To Build

## Objective
The primary objective of the lean-to is to construct the fastest shelter possible, roughly taking 1-2 hours, and to leverage its compatibility with fire to provide radiant heat.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   **Fire:** Essential for the lean-to to provide its greatest strength (radiant heat from the front).
*   **Time:** Time is short, as a basic lean-to takes 1-2 hours, compared to a well-built debris hut which takes 2-3 hours.
*   **Weather/Conditions:** Suitable for warm weather where overnight temperatures stay above 10 degrees C, providing adequate rain and wind protection.
*   **Visibility:** The open front allows occupants to watch surroundings, tend a fire, and enter or exit quickly.

## Material
| Component | Specification | Quantity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Ridgepole | Straight, sturdy, 3-4 m long, 8-10 cm diameter | 1 |
| Support method | Two trees 3-4 m apart, OR two forked sticks 1-1.5 m tall, OR cordage | Varies |
| Rafters | Straight branches, 2-3 m long, 3-5 cm diameter | 10-15 |
| Lattice/cross-pieces | Thin flexible sticks and brush | Handfuls |
| Covering | Leafy branches, bark slabs, debris, or tarp | Large quantity |
| Ground insulation | Dry leaves, pine needles, grass, boughs | 15 cm thick bed |

## System
The lean-to functions as a one-sided shelter. Its single sloping roof blocks wind from behind, and when paired with a fire, it transforms into a cold-weather system.

## Technology
The technology relies on natural materials for construction and insulation.

## Tools
- Knife (to notch rafters)
- Rope, cordage, or vine (for lashing)

## Process

### Step 1 — Establish the Ridgepole
*   **Action:** The ridgepole is the horizontal beam forming the top edge. It must be strong, straight, and well-supported.
*   **Option A (Between two trees):** Find two trees 3-4 meters apart. Lash the ridgepole to both trees at 1-1.5 meters height using rope, cordage, vine, or strips of bark, using a clove hitch or two half hitches.
*   **Option B (Forked support sticks):** Cut or find two forked sticks (Y-shaped). Drive them into the ground 3-4 meters apart, deep enough to stand firm (at least 20-30 cm into soil). Rest the ridgepole in the forks and lash it to the forks for extra stability.
*   **Height:** Set the ridgepole at 1-1.2 meters for an ideal fire-facing shelter to ensure radiant heat reaches the body.

### Step 2 — Lean the Rafters
*   **Action:** Lay long, straight branches against the ridgepole from the windward side, leaning them at 45-60 degree angles. The butts rest on the ground, and the tops rest against or over the ridgepole.
*   **Key Details:** Aim for 50-55 degrees as a starting point. Rafters should be spaced 10-15 cm apart. Ensure rafters are secured by notching them or wedging small sticks between them to prevent sliding.

### Step 3 — Add the Lattice
*   **Action:** Weave thinner sticks and brush horizontally through the rafters, perpendicular to them. This stabilizes the rafters and creates a framework for covering material.
*   **Spacing:** Space lattice pieces 15-20 cm apart. Flexible green branches or vines are best, weaving over and under alternating rafters.

### Step 4 — Apply Covering
*   **Action:** Work from the bottom up, overlapping each layer like shingles on a roof to ensure water runs off.
*   **Leafy Branches (Boughs):** Evergreen boughs (spruce, pine, fir, cedar) are ideal. Lay them with stem ends up and leafy tips pointing downward.
*   **Bark Slabs:** Large pieces of bark (from birch, cedar, poplar, or elm) make excellent shingles. Overlap edges by 5-10 cm.
*   **Debris Pile:** If needed, pile loose debris (leaves, pine needles, grass) on the lattice. Requires a thick layer—at least 20-30 cm.
*   **Tarp or Plastic:** The most effective covering if available.

### Step 5 — Insulate the Floor
*   **Action:** Create a bed of dry material at least 15 cm thick to prevent heat loss through conduction.
*   **Order of Preference:**
    1. Pine or spruce boughs (stem ends toward the wall, tips toward you).
    2. Dry leaves (compress significantly under body weight).
    3. Dry grass or hay.
    4. Bark slabs (laid flat as a bottom layer).

### Step 6 — Close the Ends
*   **Action:** Close at least one end (preferably the windward end) with piled debris, woven branches, a propped-up slab of bark, or a pack/gear pile. Closing both ends yields a warmer shelter but limits access.

### Fire Integration
*   **Fire Placement:** Position the fire 1-1.5 meters from the open face of the lean-to.
*   **Reflector Wall:** Build a reflector wall on the far side of the fire, 60-90 cm high and as long as the lean-to. Stack green logs or flat rocks between stakes.
*   **Reflector Wall Construction:** Drive two pairs of stakes into the ground. Stack logs between the stakes, one on top of another. Green logs are preferred over porous stones to avoid moisture trapping.
*   **The Long Fire:** For full-body warmth, lay two logs parallel, 15-20 cm apart, running the length of the lean-to, and build the fire between them.

## Notes
*   **Ridgepole Height:** Keep the ridgepole at 1-1.2 meters for fire shelters to ensure radiant heat reaches the entire body.
*   **Rafter Angle:** Aim for 50-55 degrees to balance rain shedding and interior space; angles closer to 60 degrees shed rain better but offer less floor space.
*   **Covering Technique:** Always shingle covering material from bottom to top; this is crucial for shedding rain.
*   **Reflector Warning:** Never use river rocks, slate, or any stone that has been in water as a fire reflector, as trapped moisture can cause them to violently shatter.
*   **Wind Tunnel Effect:** An open-ended lean-to acts as a wind tunnel; closing at least the windward end is necessary for proper wind protection.
*   **Mistake Summary:** Common mistakes include ridgepole too high, rafters too shallow, lack of a lattice layer, poor shingling technique, fire too close, missing ground insulation, and leaving ends open.
*   **Variation:** Variations include the Double lean-to (two facing each other with a shared fire) and the Lean-to with a back wall (turning it into a three-sided shelter) and the Steep lean-to (70+ degrees for heavy rain).
