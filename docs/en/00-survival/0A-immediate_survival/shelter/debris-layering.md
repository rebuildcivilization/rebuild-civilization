# Debris Layering | Survival Wiki

## Objective
Part of Emergency Shelter Proper debris layering transforms a stick framework into a weatherproof, insulated cocoon that traps body heat and sheds rain.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- Stick framework (a lattice of small sticks and branches woven through ribs).
- Debris (leaves, needles, grass, moss, bark strips).

## Material
- Structural Components:
    - Small sticks and branches (for the lattice).
    - Finger-thick sticks and small leafy branches (for the lattice).
- Insulation Materials (Ranked by insulation value):
    1. Dry dead leaves (oak, beech, maple):
    2. Pine/spruce needles:
    3. Dry grass and sedges:
    4. Fern fronds (dead):
    5. Moss (use only in dry conditions or as an inner layer).
    6. Bark strips (poor insulator but excellent water shedder; best used as an outer layer).
- Waterproofing Material:
    - Bark slabs (best if available).
    - Evergreen boughs.
    - Large leaves (if tropical, e.g., banana leaves, palm fronds).
    - Lightweight sticks (for the final “hairnet” layer).

## System
### The Three-Layer System
1. Layer 1: The Lattice (Structural)
    - Purpose: To catch falling debris and prevent loose debris from falling through gaps.
    - Application: Woven through the ribs using finger-thick sticks and small leafy branches, laid horizontally across the ribs (roughly 5-10 cm apart).
2. Layer 2: The Insulation (Bulk)
    - Purpose: To provide the thick layer of loose debris that traps air for insulation.
    - Thickness Requirements (Minimum):
        - Above 10°C (50°F): 15-20 cm (6-8 in)
        - 0-10°C (32-50°F): 30-40 cm (12-16 in)
        - -10 to 0°C (14-32°F): 45-60 cm (18-24 in)
        - Below -10°C (14°F): 60-90 cm (24-36 in)
    - Application Technique: Pack debris loosely first, then push it into gaps between ribs and lattice sticks. Build thickness gradually and evenly.
3. Layer 3: The Shingle Layer (Weatherproofing)
    - Purpose: To shed rain from the outside.
    - Application: Overlap layers like roof shingles, applying from bottom to top. Includes bark slabs, evergreen boughs, and a final thin layer of lightweight sticks to pin debris.

### Waterproofing Principles
- The Steep Angle Rule: Ribs angled at 45-60 degrees from horizontal shed water quickly. Flatter angles require more debris thickness.
- The Shingle Principle: Each layer must overlap the layer below it so rain runs down to the ground.
- The Drip Line: Debris walls must extend 15-20 cm beyond the sleeping area to prevent pooling.

### Ground Insulation: The Forgotten Layer
- Minimum Requirement: 15 cm (6 inches) of compressible debris under the body.
- Calculation: Body weight compresses the layer to about half its depth. 15 cm of pile results in roughly 8 cm of insulation under the body.

## Technology
- Debris Insulation:
    - Principle: Trapped air provides warmth; debris merely holds the air in place and prevents wind from moving it.
    - Water Conduction:
        - Water conducts heat 25 times faster than air.
- Layering Mechanism:
    - Debris creates tiny pockets of still air (insulation).
    - Layering ensures outer layers shed water, keeping inner layers dry.

## Tools
- Implied Tools:
    - Measuring tools (to ensure correct thickness).
    - Gathering implements (to collect debris).

## Process
### Debris Collection
- Time Budget: Budget at least 1-1.5 hours for debris collection.
- Strategy: Work in a radius pattern outward from the shelter to minimize walking distance.
- Tip: Look for natural debris piles (base of fallen trees, against rock walls, inside hollows).

### Shelter Construction Sequence
1. Establish the Lattice (Layer 1).
2. Apply the Bulk Insulation (Layer 2), packing loosely, and filling all gaps.
3. Apply the Shingle Layer (Layer 3) on top of the bulk insulation.
4. Insulate the Ground Layer (Minimum 15 cm).

### Testing and Quality Control
- The Daylight Test: Crawl inside during daylight to look up through the walls; see if sky patches appear (indicating leaks/heat loss).
- The Push Test: Gently push outward on walls to ensure debris is held in place; add pinning sticks if debris falls away easily.
- The Rain Simulation: Pour water on the outside from the top to check for drips through the interior.

### Maintenance and Repair
- After Rain: Check for compressed or washed-away sections; rebuild thin spots immediately.
- After Wind: Re-pin outer layers with fresh sticks; pile additional debris on the windward side.
- Regular Maintenance (Every 2-3 days): Fluff and replenish the ground layer, as body weight compresses it nightly.
- Long-Term Repair: Add mud daubing to lower walls for wind sealing; weave in additional lattice; layer bark shingles as found.

## Notes
- Debris does not keep you warm; trapped air does.
- Compressed debris has fewer air pockets, reducing insulating value.
- The best materials for insulation are dry dead leaves (oak, beech, maple).
- Wind is the enemy; the outer layer must always be pinned.
- For a standard one-person shelter (3m long, 60cm wide, 90cm tall), 30 cm thickness requires roughly 1.5-2 cubic meters of loose debris (15-20 large armloads).
- The process emphasizes the critical role of layering for waterproofing, which is distinct from the material itself.
