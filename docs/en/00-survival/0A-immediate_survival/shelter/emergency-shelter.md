# Emergency Shelter

## Objective
The primary objective of building an emergency shelter is to survive exposure, as exposure kills faster than dehydration. A basic shelter buys time to solve other survival problems.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   **Knots & Cordage:** Necessary for lashings and securing structures.
*   **Site Selection:** Requires assessing natural protection (hillside, overhang, fallen tree), checking drainage (avoiding low spots and dry riverbeds), facing the opening away from prevailing wind, and staying near resources (water, firewood) but outside the immediate shelter area.

## Material
*   **For Debris Hut:** Ridgepole (3 meters), rib sticks, smaller branches, leaves, pine needles, grass, ferns, moss, debris for the floor (at least 15 cm).
*   **For Lean-To:** Horizontal support (ridgepole or rope/cordage), long straight branches for rafters, leafy branches, bark slabs, or tarp.
*   **For Tarp Shelter:** Tarp, plastic sheet, emergency blanket, poncho, cordage or rope, stakes or heavy rocks.
*   **For Snow Shelter:** Deep snow (1 meter+).

## System
**Core Principle: Dead Air Space**
Every shelter works by trapping a layer of still air around the body. Moving air strips heat away (wind chill). The shelter's job is to block wind, shed rain, and keep a small pocket of air still so body heat can warm it.

*   **Smaller is warmer:** A shelter just big enough to lie in is far warmer than one one can stand in.
*   **Insulation goes UNDER you too:** The ground steals more heat than the air. A 15 cm thick bed of debris under you is critical.
*   **Waterproofing matters:** Wet insulation is useless; a dry person is colder than a wet person in a shelter.

## Technology
Exposure kills in as little as 3 hours in cold, wet, or windy conditions (hypothermia).

## Tools
*   **No Tools Required (Debris Hut):** Requires only time and debris.
*   **Tools Required (General):** Knots and cordage for securing structures.

## Process
### Method 1: Debris Hut (Best No-Tool Shelter)
1.  **Find a ridgepole:** A sturdy, straight branch or small tree trunk about 3 meters long.
2.  **Prop the ridgepole:** Prop one end on a stump or rock at waist height; the other end rests on the ground (head position).
3.  **Lean ribs:** Lean sticks along both sides of the ridgepole at about 45-degree angles.
4.  **Lay lattice:** Lay smaller sticks, branches, and brush horizontally across the ribs to hold debris.
5.  **Pile debris:** Pile debris on top, aiming for at least 30 cm thick, closer to 60 cm in cold weather.
6.  **Insulate floor:** Fill the inside floor with a thick layer of leaves or pine needles, at least 15 cm deep.
7.  **Block entrance:** Block the entrance with a backpack, stuffed garbage bag, or debris to pull in.
8.  **Entry:** Crawl in feet first, pulling debris in.

### Method 2: Lean-To
1.  **Find support:** Lash a ridgepole between two trees (1-1.5 meters off the ground) or prop a long branch.
2.  **Lean rafters:** Lean long branches against the ridgepole at 45-60 degree angles on the windward side.
3.  **Weave covering:** Weave smaller branches, brush, or leafy boughs horizontally through the rafters, ensuring layers overlap to shed rain.
4.  **Add debris:** Apply a debris layer on top for insulation and waterproofing.
5.  **Fire placement:** If a fire is present, build it about 1 meter from the open side; use stacked logs behind the fire as a reflector wall.

### Method 3: A-Frame Tarp Shelter
1.  **String ridgeline:** String cordage between two trees at waist height and pull taut.
2.  **Drape tarp:** Drape the tarp over the ridgeline so equal amounts hang on each side, forming an A-shape.
3.  **Secure edges:** Stake or weight the edges down with rocks, logs, or stakes, ensuring the tarp is taut.
4.  **Angle:** Angle the A-frame so one end faces into the wind (close this end with debris or fold the tarp).
5.  **Insulate:** Insulate the ground underneath with debris, pine boughs, or a sleeping pad.

### Method 4: Snow Shelter Basics
1.  **Sinter snow:** Pile a large mound (2 meters high, 3 meters across) and let it sit for 1-2 hours to sinter.
2.  **Dig tunnel:** Dig an entrance tunnel from the downwind side, angling slightly upward.
3.  **Hollow interior:** Hollow out the interior, leaving walls at least 30 cm thick, and smooth the ceiling into a dome shape for meltwater runoff.
4.  **Ventilation:** Poke a ventilation hole in the ceiling to allow for airflow.
5.  **Sleeping platform:** Build a raised sleeping platform of packed snow to benefit from warmer air near the ceiling.

## Notes
*   **Rule of 3:** Shelter before food, as exposure kills in approximately 3 hours.
*   **Debris Hut Tip:** The interior should be just barely big enough to lie in; never big enough to stand in.
*   **Core Principle Reinforcement:** Always prioritize ground insulation (15 cm minimum) as the ground is a bigger threat than the air.
*   **Common Mistakes:**
    *   Building too large (requires too much body heat).
    *   Skipping ground insulation.
    *   Building in a low spot (risk of flooding and cold air pooling).
    *   Not enough debris (walls are too thin).
    *   Opening facing into the wind (must face opening 90 degrees from prevailing wind).
    *   Using a smooth, round ridgepole (should use a slightly rough or irregular pole).
*   **Snow Shelter Warning:** Never heat a snow shelter with an open flame, as it can melt the structure and produce carbon monoxide.
*   **Long-Term Next Steps:** Consider permanent shelters (wattle and daub, log cabins) and combining shelter with fire making for dramatic survival improvements.
