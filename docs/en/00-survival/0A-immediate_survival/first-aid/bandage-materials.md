# Bandage Materials | Survival Wiki

## Objective
Part of First Aid. Improvised bandages from cloth, bark, and plant fibers keep wounds clean and protected when manufactured medical supplies no longer exist.

A good improvised bandage does three things: absorbs wound drainage, holds dressings in place, and stays clean enough not to introduce new bacteria.

## Dependencies and prerequisites

*   Knowledge of materials that work versus those that cause infection.
*   Sterilization methods (boiling or dry heat).
*   Warm water.
*   Soap or wood ash (if available) for washing.

> **Warning:** Never use fabric that has been in contact with chemicals, motor oil, or solvents, as these cause chemical burns in open wounds and cannot be fully washed out.

## Material

### Cloth Strips
*   Cotton bedsheets
*   Linen cloth
*   Cotton t-shirts
*   Cotton pillowcases
*   Wool fabric (for outer wrapping only)
*   Synthetic polyester (for securing, not wound contact)

### Bark Wraps
*   Birch bark (inner/white side)
*   Elm bark (inner bark)
*   Willow bark
*   Pine bark (inner bark)

### Plant Fiber Bandages
*   Sphagnum moss
*   Cattail fluff
*   Plantain leaves (Plantago major)
*   Mullein leaves

## System

The system is Improvised Supplies Making medical gear, focusing on using available natural resources to replace manufactured medical supplies.

## Technology

The technology involves selecting materials based on absorbency, durability, and chemical properties, followed by specific application techniques to manage wound drainage and secure dressings.

## Tools

*   Knife (for cutting bark/cloth).
*   Pot (for boiling water).
*   Tongs or a stick (for removing boiling strips).
*   Cordage or cloth strips (for securing).
*   Thorn or sharpened sliver of wood (for pinning).

## Process

### Sourcing and Preparation
1.  **Cloth Strips:** Tear fabric along the grain for even strips.
    *   Narrow strips (2-3 cm): Finger wounds, toe wraps, securing small dressings.
    *   Medium strips (5-8 cm): Arm and leg wounds, most general bandaging.
    *   Wide strips (10-15 cm): Torso wraps, large wound coverage, slings.
    *   Long strips (1-2 meters): Spiral wrapping for limbs, figure-eight wraps for joints.
2.  **Bark Wraps:** Score a rectangle through the outer bark to the cambium layer, peel carefully, and scrape off the rough outer bark, leaving only the smooth inner layer for wound contact.
3.  **Sterilization (Preferred Boiling Method):** Bring water to a rolling boil, submerge fabric strips completely for a minimum of 10 minutes, remove with clean tongs or a stick, hang to dry in sunlight (UV), and store in a clean, dry container.
4.  **Sterilization (Dry Heat Method):** Wrap strips in clean cloth or aluminum foil, heat near a fire or makeshift oven at roughly 160-170°C for 30 minutes, then allow to cool.
5.  **Bark Preparation:** Soak strips in warm water for 15-30 minutes to make them pliable.
6.  **Plant Fiber Preparation:** Sphagnum moss is packed loosely against the wound; Cattail fluff is placed under a thin cloth layer; Plantain leaves are layered 3-4 thick and secured.

### Bandaging Techniques
*   **Spiral Wrap (Limbs):** Start below the wound and wrap upward in overlapping spirals, with each turn covering half the previous one. Secure the end by tucking it under the last wrap or tying a flat knot.
*   **Figure-Eight (Joints):** Wrap alternately above and below the joint in a crossing pattern to allow some joint movement while keeping the dressing secure (essential for ankles, knees, elbows, and wrists).
*   **Pressure Bandage (Bleeding Wounds):** Place the dressing pad directly over the wound, wrap firmly (tighter than normal but not cutting off circulation), and check below every 15-30 minutes for color, warmth, and sensation.
*   **Securing Without Tape:** Methods include tucking the end under the last wrap, tying a flat (reef) knot (never a granny knot), splitting the end lengthwise and tying the two tails around the limb, or pinning with a thorn or sharpened sliver of wood pushed through the layers.

### Storage and Reuse
1.  Remove soiled bandages carefully.
2.  Rinse immediately in cold water to prevent blood from setting.
3.  Wash with hot water and wood ash soap if available.
4.  Boil for 10 minutes to sterilize.
5.  Dry completely in sunlight before re-rolling and storing.

## Key Takeaways
*   Cotton and linen cloth torn into strips and boiled for 10 minutes are the most reliable improvised bandages.
*   Birch and elm inner bark make effective semi-rigid outer wraps with mild antiseptic properties.
*   Sphagnum moss is the best natural wound dressing, offering 20x absorbency of cotton with natural antibacterial action.
*   Always sterilize bandage materials before wound contact; a dirty bandage is worse than no bandage.
*   Store rolled bandages in clean, sealed containers and reuse them after boiling when supplies are limited.


## Notes

- The core principle of improvisation relies on selecting materials based on their ability to absorb fluid, resist lint, and offer antiseptic qualities.
- The difference between good and bad materials is determined by the risk of infection introduced by the material itself (e.g., contact with chemicals).
- For bark, the inner cambium layer is preferred for wound contact due to flexibility and antimicrobial properties.
