# Heat Stroke: Rapid Cooling Protocol | Survival Wiki

## Objective
The objective of the protocol is to rapidly cool the core temperature of an affected person below 39°C (102°F) within roughly 30 minutes to prevent permanent brain damage or death.

## Dependencies and prerequisites

*   **Condition:** The individual must have been working in heat and exhibit signs of the cooling system failure.
*   **Recognition:** The ability to recognize the specific symptoms of heat stroke, defined by the "hot, dry, confused" rule.

## Material
*   Available water (must be used for cooling, not drinking).
*   Shade (tree, building, or improvised cover like a tarp).
*   Clothing to be stripped.
*   Cold water or ice (for cold water immersion or cold packs).
*   Cloth (for wrapping ice).

## System
*   **Failing System:** The body’s core temperature regulation and cooling system.
*   **Core Failure Indicator:** The cessation of sweating, resulting in hot, dry skin and confusion.

## Technology
*   **Evaporation:** The primary mechanism for cooling through aggressive fanning.
*   **Conduction/Convection:** The mechanism utilized by moving the person to shade and cold water immersion.

## Tools
*   **Shade Provider:** To move the person out of direct sun.
*   **Water Source:** To drench the body.
*   **Fanning Implement:** Anything available (shirt, bark, mat, cardboard, large leaf).
*   **Cold Agents:** Cold water immersion or cold packs applied to pulse points.
*   **Recovery Position:** To place confused or unconscious persons to prevent aspiration.

## Process

### Rapid Recognition
Heat stroke is recognized by the "hot, dry, confused" rule:
*   **Hot:** Skin feels abnormally hot.
*   **Dry:** No sweating despite extreme heat.
*   **Confused:** Inability to answer simple questions or may be combative.

### The Cooling Protocol (Target: Below 39°C / 102°F within 30 minutes)

1.  **Step 1: Move to Shade (30 seconds):** Immediately move the person out of direct sun.
2.  **Step 2: Remove Clothing (1 minute):** Strip off as much clothing as possible to allow heat to escape.
3.  **Step 3: Wet the Entire Body (2 minutes):** Pour water over the entire body. Focus on the head, neck, armpits, and groin where major vessels run near the surface.
4.  **Step 4: Fan Aggressively (continuous):** Use available implements to promote evaporation.
5.  **Step 5: Ice Point Cooling (if available):**
    *   **Cold Water Immersion:** Submerge the person up to the neck in cold water. Keep the head above water.
    *   **Cold Packs at Pulse Points:** Apply cold wet cloths or ice wrapped in cloth to the neck, armpits, groin, and forehead.

### Fluid Replacement
*   **If Conscious and Alert:** Give cool (not ice-cold) water in small amounts (half a cup every 5-10 minutes), adding a pinch of salt per cup.
*   **If Confused or Unconscious:** Do not give oral fluids due to aspiration risk; focus solely on external cooling and place the person in the recovery position.

### Monitoring Progress
Check every 5 minutes to assess:
*   Skin temperature (chest and abdomen).
*   Mental state (checking for improved answers).
*   Breathing (should slow and deepen).
*   Pulse (should slow from rapid, bounding rate).

### When to Stop Active Cooling
Stop aggressive cooling when:
*   Skin feels warm but no longer burning hot.
*   Shivering begins (indicating the body’s thermostat is restarting).
*   Mental clarity significantly improves.

### After the Crisis
*   **Duration:** Keep the person in shade for at least 24 hours due to remaining thermoregulation damage.
*   **Hydration:** Continue small fluid replacement using oral rehydration solution.
*   **Watch for Delayed Complications (12-48 hours):** Dark or reduced urine (kidney damage), ongoing confusion (brain swelling), muscle cramps or dark brown urine (rhabdomyolysis), and bleeding from gums or nose.

## Heat Exhaustion vs. Heat Stroke

| Feature | Heat Exhaustion | Heat Stroke |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Sweating** | Heavy, profuse | Stops — skin is dry and hot |
| **Skin** | Cool, clammy, pale | Hot, red, dry (or occasionally damp) |
| **Mental state** | Alert but fatigued, maybe dizzy | Confused, combative, delirious, or unconscious |
| **Temperature** | Below 40°C (104°F) | Above 40°C (104°F) |
| **Headache** | Mild to moderate | Severe, throbbing |
| **Pulse** | Rapid but weak | Rapid and strong (bounding) |
| **Response** | Rest, shade, fluids | **Immediate aggressive cooling** |

## Prevention
*   **Hydrate before thirst:** Drink regularly throughout the day to prevent early dehydration.
*   **Work in shifts:** Implement a 50-minutes-on, 10-minutes-off shade break pattern for heavy labor.
*   **Wet clothing:** Use damp shirts for continuous evaporative cooling.
*   **Cover the head:** Use a wide-brimmed hat or wet cloth on the head to prevent solar heating of the brain.
*   **Schedule work:** Perform heavy work during early morning and late evening, avoiding the peak heat between 11:00 and 15:00.
*   **Buddy System:** Assign heat buddies to check on each other every 30 minutes.

## Key Takeaways
*   Heat stroke is defined by hot, dry skin plus confusion, marking the failure of the cooling system.
*   The critical window is about 30 minutes to cool the core below 39°C (102°F).
*   The essential protocol is: shade, strip, drench, fan aggressively, and apply cold agents.
*   Cooling takes priority over fluids for the confused or unconscious victim.
*   The person remains vulnerable for at least 3 days after the crisis.

Notes
- Heat stroke is the opposite end of the temperature emergency spectrum from hypothermia and is just as lethal.
- Direct ice contact on the skin is contraindicated because it causes blood vessels to constrict, paradoxically trapping heat in the core.
- Cold water immersion should use water around 15-20°C (59-68°F) rather than ice water.
- Aftercare requires close monitoring for delayed complications such as kidney damage, brain swelling, and rhabdomyolysis.
