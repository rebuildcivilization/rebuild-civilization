# Wound Closure Guide

## Objective
To close a wound to bring the edges together, accelerate healing, reduce scarring, and create a barrier against bacteria. The ultimate objective is to ensure the injury becomes survivable, preventing an open wound from becoming a fatal one due to infection.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   **Wound Quality:** The wound must be clean and sharp-edged for the best results.
*   **Time Frame:** Closure is best applied if the wound is less than 6-8 hours old (the “golden period”).
*   **Irrigation:** The wound must be irrigated thoroughly with at least 500 ml of clean water under pressure before closure.
*   **Contamination Check:** There must be no signs of contamination (dirt, rust, organic debris) and no visible signs of infection (redness, warmth, swelling, pus, foul smell).
*   **Tension Assessment:** Wound edges must be assessed; high tension may require leaving the wound open or using partial closures.

## Material
*   Adhesive Strips (Butterfly Closures)
*   Improvised materials for improvised strips (cut thin strips of adhesive tape, duct tape, or electrical tape).
*   Wound Closure Tape / Steri-Strips (purpose-built adhesive strips).
*   Tissue Glue (Superglue/cyanoacrylate).
*   Sutures (Needle and thread).
*   Medical Staples.
*   Clean water (at least 500 ml for irrigation).
*   Moist dressing (for delayed primary closure).
*   Honey or antiseptic (for application after suture removal).

## System
*   **Framework:** First Aid and Wound Management.
*   **Core Principle:** The critical decision is whether to Close or Leave Open, based on the wound's age and contamination status.
*   **Alternative Healing:** Secondary intention (healing from the bottom up) is the preferred method when leaving a wound open.

## Technology
*   **Closure Mechanics:** Bringing wound edges together to facilitate healing and create a bacterial barrier.
*   **Irrigation Efficacy:** Mechanical flushing with pressurized water is superior to antiseptics alone in removing bacteria.
*   **Delayed Primary Closure:** A technique where the wound is cleaned, packed with moist dressing, and closed only after 3-5 days, avoiding the risk of trapping infection.

## Tools
*   Clean, sharp blade (for debridement).
*   Syringe or bottle (for aggressive irrigation).
*   Fingers (for assessing tension and holding edges).
*   Stapler (for medical staples).
*   Needle and thread (for suturing).
*   Clean, sharp blade (for debridement).

## Process
### 1. Critical Decision
Assess the wound age and contamination level to decide between closing or leaving open.

*   **Close the wound if:** Less than 6-8 hours old, clean sharp laceration, thorough irrigation, no contamination, edges can be brought together without excessive tension.
*   **Leave the wound OPEN if:** Older than 8 hours, caused by a bite, visible contamination, signs of infection (redness, warmth, swelling, pus, foul smell), or low confidence in closure.

### 2. Preparation for Closure (Universal Steps)
1.  **Irrigate aggressively:** Flush the wound with at least 500 ml of clean water under pressure.
2.  **Debride if necessary:** Remove dead tissue (grey, white, or black tissue that does not bleed when cut) and foreign material using a clean, sharp blade.
3.  **Control bleeding:** Apply pressure until bleeding is minimal. Small oozing is acceptable.
4.  **Examine depth:** If tendon, bone, or joint capsule is visible, specialized repair is needed; close only the skin layer if necessary.
5.  **Assess tension:** Gently push edges together. Proceed only if edges come together easily; otherwise, leave open or use partial closures.

### 3. Closure Methods
*   **Adhesive Strips (Butterfly Closures):** Best for shallow, clean cuts with straight edges. Technique involves drying the skin, placing strips across the center, alternating sides, spacing strips 3-5 mm apart, and applying longitudinal strips to anchor them.
*   **Wound Closure Tape / Steri-Strips:** Purpose-built adhesive strips using the same butterfly closure technique.
*   **Tissue Glue:** Used for shallow wounds. Technique involves holding edges together, applying a thin line of glue along the surface (not inside the wound), holding for 60 seconds, and applying 2-3 layers.
*   **Suturing (Stitching):** The strongest and most reliable method, requiring materials and skill. See [Suture Technique] for detail.
*   **Staples:** Fast and effective for scalp lacerations and long straight wounds. Position the staple across the wound and squeeze the stapler, spacing staples 5-8 mm apart.

### 4. Closure by Body Region
| Region            | Recommended Method                               | Notes                                                                                                                    |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Scalp             | Staples or sutures                               | Scalp bleeds profusely; hair can be used to tie wound edges together (hair apposition technique).
| Face              | Fine sutures or adhesive strips                   | Face wounds heal well due to excellent blood supply.
| Arms and legs     | Sutures or adhesive strips                       | Use sutures for anything over joint surfaces.
| Hands and fingers | Sutures                                          | Close skin only; avoid suturing tendons.
| Torso             | Sutures for deep lacerations, strips for shallow   | Low-tension area.
| Feet             | Sutures                                          | High-stress area; wound must hold against weight-bearing.

### 5. Delayed Primary Closure
For wounds that are too old or contaminated but not severely infected:
1.  Clean and pack the wound with moist dressing.
2.  Change the dressing daily, irrigating each time.
3.  Close at 3-5 days if no signs of infection (no pus, expanding redness, fever) are present.

### 6. Aftercare
1.  Keep the closure dry for the first 24-48 hours.
2.  Inspect daily for signs of infection (increasing pain, redness spreading beyond 1 cm, warmth, swelling, discharge).
3.  **If infection develops:** Remove some or all closures immediately to allow drainage, irrigate, pack the wound open, and treat as an infected wound.
4.  **Suture/Strips Removal Timing:**
    *   Face: 5 days
    *   Scalp: 7-10 days
    *   Arms / Trunk: 7-10 days
    *   Legs: 10-14 days
    *   Over Joints: 14 days
    *   Feet / High-tension areas: 14 days
5.  **Post-Removal Treatment:** Apply honey or antiseptic to the healing wound after suture removal to prevent infection at the suture holes.

## Notes
*   **Golden Rule:** "a clean wound left open heals better than a dirty wound closed shut."
*   **Key Takeaway:** Never close a wound that is contaminated, older than 8 hours, or showing signs of infection.
*   **Infection Sign:** Red streaks extending from the wound indicate lymphangitis (spreading infection).
*   **Tension Risk:** Failure to assess tension leads to tissue death (grey/black edges).
