# Wound Treatment Plants

## Objective
To provide critical, effective field wound care using readily available plants without access to a pharmacy, antiseptic, bandages, or a doctor.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   No pharmacy or commercial antiseptic.
*   No bandages or doctor.
*   Basic field supplies (e.g., rocks, hard objects, clean water).

## Material
*   **Yarrow** (*Achillea millefolium*):
    *   Leaves and flowers (fresh or dried).
*   **Plantain** (*Plantago major* / *P. lanceolata*):
    *   Leaves (fresh or dried).

## System
The system relies on real biochemistry and practical tools to treat wounds by addressing the three needs: stopping bleeding, preventing infection, and promoting healing. The effectiveness is validated by centuries of battlefield and folk medicine.

## Technology
*   **Yarrow's Action:** Achilleine accelerates blood clotting; tannins constrict tissue and seal wounds; camphor and eucalyptol act as antimicrobial volatile oils; flavonoids support tissue repair.
*   **Plantain's Action:** Aucubin accelerates wound healing; allantoin stimulates cell growth and tissue repair; mucilage provides a soothing coating; tannins help control minor bleeding.

## Tools
*   Fresh yarrow leaves and flowers.
*   Plantain leaves.
*   Warm water (for antiseptic wash and rehydration).
*   Oil (for making yarrow wound salve).
*   Beeswax (for making yarrow wound salve).
*   Containers for storage.

## Process
### Identification
**Yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*):
*   **Leaves:** Feathery and finely divided, alternate along the stem.
*   **Flowers:** Flat-topped clusters (corymbs) of tiny white or pale pink flowers.
*   **Height:** 30-90 cm (1-3 feet).
*   **Stem:** Single, mostly unbranched, slightly hairy.
*   **Smell:** Strong, aromatic, slightly bitter when leaves are crushed.
*   **Habitat:** Meadows, roadsides, fields, disturbed ground.
*   **Season:** Flowers June-September; leaves available spring through autumn.
*   **Confusion Risks:** Young leaves can resemble wild carrot or poison hemlock. Key difference: yarrow leaves are feathery/finely divided with a strong aromatic scent. Poison hemlock has a musty smell and hollow stems. Wild carrot has a hairy solid stem and a single tiny purple flower in the center of each white flower cluster.

**Plantain (*Plantago major* and *P. lanceolata*):
*   **Broadleaf Plantain (*P. major*):**
    *   **Leaves:** Wide, oval, spoon-shaped.
    *   **Veins:** 3-5 prominent parallel veins.
    *   **Growth:** Flat basal rosette.
    *   **Flower:** Tall spike with tiny greenish flowers.
    *   **Habitat:** Lawns, paths, compacted soil.
*   **Ribwort Plantain (*P. lanceolata*):
    *   **Leaves:** Long, narrow, lance-shaped.
    *   **Veins:** 3-5 prominent parallel veins.
    *   **Growth:** More upright rosette.
    *   **Flower:** Short dark head on tall stalk.
    *   **Habitat:** Meadows, roadsides, fields.
*   **Definitive Test:** Snap a leaf stem slowly and look for stringy fibers that pull apart like threads.
*   **Confusion Risks:** Virtually none. Lily-of-the-valley has somewhat similar leaves but grows in woodland shade, has a different vein pattern, and is strongly toxic.

### Active Compounds
**Yarrow Active Compounds:**
*   Achilleine: Promotes blood clotting by accelerating platelet aggregation.
*   Azulene and chamazulene: Anti-inflammatory compounds.
*   Tannins: Astringent compounds that constrict tissue and seal wounds.
*   Camphor and eucalyptol: Antimicrobial volatile oils.
*   Flavonoids: Support tissue repair and reduce swelling.

**Plantain Active Compounds:**
*   Aucubin: Antimicrobial compound that accelerates wound healing.
*   Allantoin: Stimulates cell growth and tissue repair.
*   Mucilage: Soothing coating that protects damaged tissue.
*   Tannins: Astringent, help control minor bleeding.

### Wound Treatment Protocols
**Yarrow Treatment Protocol:**
1.  **Control Bleeding:** Chew or crush fresh leaves/flowers to release compounds. Apply pressure for at least 5 minutes on a bleeding cut. For deep wounds, pack yarrow into the cavity.
2.  **Bandage:** Wrap with clean cloth, large flexible leaves (burdock leaves recommended), or strips of inner bark.
3.  **Change Poultice:** Replace every 4-6 hours initially, then every 8-12 hours as healing progresses. Inspect for signs of infection (redness, heat, swelling, red streaks, pus, foul smell).
4.  **Internal Support:** Steep 1-2 teaspoons of dried yarrow flowers/leaves in hot water for 10-15 minutes and drink 2-3 cups daily for anti-inflammatory support.
*   **Warning:** For deep puncture wounds, flush thoroughly with clean water first, then apply yarrow to the surface only; never pack deep into a puncture wound.

**Plantain Treatment Protocol:**
1.  **Insect Stings/Bites:** Chew a leaf for 10-15 seconds to create a wet poultice; apply directly to draw out venom and irritants. Replace every 30-60 minutes until swelling subsides.
2.  **Minor Cuts/Scrapes:** Chew/crush several leaves; apply as a poultice over the cleaned wound. Bandage and change every 4-6 hours.
3.  **Splinters/Embedded Debris:** Apply a crushed leaf poultice over the object and bandage. Leave overnight; the splinter may be easier to remove the next morning.
4.  **Blisters:** Apply a fresh plantain leaf directly over the blister as a protective and healing layer.

**Combining Yarrow and Plantain:
*   Actively bleeding wound: Yarrow first (clotting), then plantain poultice on top (healing).
*   Infected wound: Yarrow wash (antimicrobial flush), then alternating poultices.
*   Insect sting with scratch: Plantain first (draws venom), yarrow if inflammation persists.
*   Deep cut, no longer bleeding: Clean with yarrow wash, cover with plantain poultice.
*   Minor burns: Plantain poultice only (soothing); yarrow can irritate burns.

**Yarrow Antiseptic Wash Protocol:**
1.  Boil yarrow leaves and flowers in water for 5 minutes to create a decoction.
2.  Let the decoction cool until warm (not hot).
3.  Flush the wound with the warm yarrow wash to kill surface bacteria.
4.  Follow with a fresh poultice.

### Preparation
**Drying for Storage:**
1.  Harvest yarrow flowers and upper leaves when in full bloom (June-August); harvest plantain leaves anytime during the growing season.
2.  Bundle stems loosely and hang upside down in a dry, airy, shaded location to prevent degradation of active compounds.
3.  Once completely dry (stems snap cleanly, 1-2 weeks), strip leaves and flowers from stems. Store in sealed containers away from light and moisture.
4.  Dried material retains potency for 6-12 months. To use dried material as a poultice, soak in warm water to rehydrate before application.

**Making Yarrow Wound Salve:**
1.  Fill a jar halfway with dried yarrow flowers and leaves.
2.  Cover completely with oil (rendered animal fat, olive oil, or clean oil).
3.  Seal and leave in a warm spot for 3-4 weeks, shaking daily.
4.  Strain through cloth, squeezing out all the oil.
5.  Gently heat the infused oil. Add grated beeswax at roughly 1 part wax to 4 parts oil, stirring until melted and combined.
6.  Pour into small containers and let cool.

### When Plants Aren’t Enough
Medical intervention is required if:
*   Red streaks extend from the wound toward the heart (lymphangitis).
*   The wound develops thick green/yellow pus with a foul smell.
*   Fever exceeds 39°C (102°F) and does not respond to willow bark or yarrow tea.
*   The wound is deep enough to see bone, tendon, or fat.
*   Bleeding cannot be controlled with direct pressure and yarrow.
*   The wound was caused by an animal bite (high infection risk).

## Key Takeaways
*   **Yarrow stops bleeding** through achilleine.
*   **Plantain draws out irritants** and promotes healing.
*   **Combined Use:** Yarrow for bleeding/antiseptic action, plantain for healing/protection.
*   **Yarrow Antiseptic Wash:** Boiled/cooled water is the field cleaning solution.
*   **Storage:** Dry and store plants in summer for 6-12 months of potency.
*   **Reality Check:** These are real medicine, but limits exist for deep punctures, spreading infections, and major trauma.
*   **Identification is Key:** The feathery leaves and stringy fibers distinguish plantain.

## Notes
- The source text explicitly mentions that yarrow's active compounds (achilleine, tannins, camphor, eucalyptol, flavonoids) are the same ones used in pharmaceutical wound care, highlighting the practical biochemical basis of the treatment.
- The distinction between the leaves of *P. major* (wide, oval, spoon-shaped, basal rosette) and *P. lanceolata* (long, narrow, lance-shaped, more upright rosette) is detailed, alongside the unique physical test of stringy fibers in plantain leaves.
- A specific warning is given for yarrow poultices on deep puncture wounds: they carry a risk of trapping bacteria, necessitating a flush with clean water first.
