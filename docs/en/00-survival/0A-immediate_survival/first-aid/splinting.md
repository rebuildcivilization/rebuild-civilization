# Splinting | Survival Wiki

## Objective
Part of First Aid. A splint's single job is to hold broken bones still so they can heal in the right position. Doing it well turns a crippling injury into a full recovery; doing it poorly causes more damage than the break itself.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
1. **Assessment:** Identify the fracture location and check circulation below the injury (pulse, sensation, movement in fingers/toes). Refer to Bone and Joint for assessment techniques.
2. **Materials:** Gather two rigid supports, padding (cloth, moss, clothing), and ties (cloth strips, cordage, belts, tape).
3. **Communication:** Explain the process to the patient so they understand and cooperate.

## Material
| Material | Best For | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Straight sticks/branches | Arm and leg fractures | Most universally available; choose 3-5 cm diameter |
| Bark slabs | Forearm, lower leg | Moldable when fresh, stiffens as it dries |
| Boards/planks | Any long bone | Scavenged from buildings, furniture, pallets |
| Rolled newspaper/magazine | Forearm, wrist, ankle | Surprisingly rigid when rolled tightly |
| Cardboard | Arm, wrist, ankle | Fold into multiple layers for rigidity |
| SAM splint (if scavenged) | Any bone | Moldable aluminum with foam padding — the gold standard |
| Trekking poles/umbrella | Leg fractures | Already the right shape and length |
| Pillow or blanket | Ankle, foot, wrist | Wrap around the injury and secure — a “soft splint” |
| The body itself | Fingers, legs | Buddy-tape a broken finger to its neighbor; bind a broken leg to the uninjured leg |

## System
**The Cardinal Rule: Joint Above, Joint Below**
Every splint must immobilize the joint above the fracture AND the joint below the fracture. This is the single most important principle because joints allow movement, and immobilizing only the bone leaves rotation and bending forces to reach the fracture site.

## Technology
The core technology of splinting is immobilization to achieve three goals:
1. **Prevent further damage:** Eliminate motion to prevent sharp bone ends from cutting into muscle, blood vessels, and nerves.
2. **Reduce pain:** Immobilization drops pain levels by preventing bone ends from grinding against each other and damaged soft tissue.
3. **Allow healing:** Requires broken ends to remain in stable contact for weeks to facilitate callus formation.

## Tools
- Two rigid supports
- Padding (cloth, moss, clothing)
- Ties (cloth strips, cordage, belts, tape)

## Process
### Preparation
1. Assess the injury.
2. Gather materials.
3. Cut or tear ties to length before starting (need 4-6 ties).
4. Explain the procedure to the patient.

### Application
**Step 1:** Place padding along the entire length of each splint where it contacts skin.
**Step 2:** Position the splints on opposite sides of the limb (one on top, one underneath, or one on each side). Do NOT place a splint directly over the fracture site—position them alongside it.
**Step 3:** While an assistant gently supports the limb (preventing movement), slide the splints into position.
**Step 4:** Secure with ties. Start with a tie ABOVE the fracture, then one BELOW the fracture, then work outward toward the joints.
**Step 5:** Tie firmly enough that the splint does not shift, but not so tight that it compresses blood vessels. A finger should be able to slide between the tie and the skin.
**Step 6:** Recheck circulation after splinting. If circulation is worse, loosen the ties immediately.
**Step 7:** Elevate the splinted limb above heart level if possible to reduce swelling.

### Specific Splinting Techniques by Location
**Forearm (Radius/Ulna)**
- Two splints: one on the palm side, one on the back of the forearm.
- Extend from mid-upper-arm (above elbow) to beyond the fingertips.
- Pad the palm with a rolled cloth for the patient to grip.
- Secure the elbow at roughly 90 degrees.
- Support with a sling after splinting.

**Upper Arm (Humerus)**
- Place a padded splint along the outer arm from shoulder to elbow.
- Bend the elbow to 90 degrees and place the forearm in a sling.
- Bind the upper arm to the torso with a broad wrap (swath) — the chest wall acts as the second splint.

**Lower Leg (Tibia/Fibula)**
- Two splints: one on the inner side, one on the outer side of the leg.
- Extend from mid-thigh (above knee) to beyond the foot.
- Pad heavily at the ankle bones.
- Secure the ankle at 90 degrees (neutral position) to prevent foot drop during healing.

**Ankle/Foot**
- A pillow splint works well: wrap a pillow or folded blanket around the foot and ankle, secure with ties.
- Alternatively, use a figure-eight wrap with padded board splints on each side.
- Maintain 90-degree ankle position.

**Fingers**
- Buddy-tape the broken finger to an adjacent uninjured finger using cloth strips or tape.
- Place a small pad between the fingers to prevent skin maceration.
- For severely displaced fractures, use a small stick or tongue depressor as a dorsal splint, padded and taped in place.

**Collarbone (Clavicle)**
- Cannot be splinted directly—use a sling to support the arm.
- Add a swath (broad wrap around the arm and torso) to prevent the arm from swinging outward. 

## Notes
- **Cardinal Rule Reinforcement:** The absolute necessity is immobilizing the joint above AND the joint below the fracture.
- **Warning:** NEVER tie directly over the fracture site, as this causes extreme pain and can displace bone fragments.
- **Key Takeaways:**
    - Every splint must immobilize the joint above AND the joint below the fracture.
    - Padding between the splint and skin prevents pressure sores and nerve damage.
    - Check circulation (pulse, sensation, movement) after every splint application and every 30 minutes for the first 2 hours.
    - The splint must be long enough to span from the joint above to the joint below.
- **When to Resplint:** Remove and reapply a splint only when circulation is compromised despite loosening ties, the splint breaks/shifts, the wound needs treatment, or swelling significantly decreases.
- **Common Splinting Mistakes:**
    - **Splint too short:** Fails to immobilize joints above and below.
    - **No padding:** Leads to pressure sores and nerve compression.
    - **Tied too tight:** Cuts off circulation.
    - **Tied over fracture site:** Causes extreme pain and bone displacement.
    - **Not rechecking circulation:** Leads to ties becoming too tight.
    - **Removing the splint to ‘check’ the fracture:** Displaces healing bone.
    - **Splinting in an unnatural position:** Results in joint contracture and poor healing alignment.
