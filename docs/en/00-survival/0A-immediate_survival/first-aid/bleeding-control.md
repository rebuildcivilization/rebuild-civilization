# Bleeding Control

## Objective
To stop blood loss effectively and quickly.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Clean cloth or material (shirt, bandana, towel, gauze).
*   Knowledge of the three-step protocol.
*   Availability of materials for pressure bandages or wound packing.
*   For internal bleeding: keeping the person lying flat and elevating legs (if no spinal injury suspected).

## Material
*   **Blood Types:
    *   Arterial bleeding:
        *   Appearance: Bright red blood that spurts rhythmically with each heartbeat.
        *   Danger: Most dangerous; unchecked arterial bleed can be fatal in minutes.
    *   Venous bleeding:
        *   Appearance: Dark red blood that flows steadily without pulsing.
        *   Severity: Serious but slower than arterial; most can be controlled with direct pressure.
    *   Capillary bleeding:
        *   Appearance: Slow oozing from scrapes and shallow cuts.
        *   Danger: Rarely dangerous on its own; usually stops within minutes.
*   **Blood Volume:
    *   An average adult has roughly 5 liters (10.5 pints) of blood.
    *   Blood loss progression:
        *   Up to 750 ml (~15%): Minimal symptoms; slight increase in heart rate.
        *   750-1,500 ml (15-30%):
            *   Symptoms: Anxiety, faster heart rate, pale skin, delayed capillary refill.
        *   1,500-2,000 ml (30-40%):
            *   Symptoms: Confusion, rapid weak pulse, cold clammy skin, very low blood pressure.
        *   Over 2,000 ml (>40%):
            *   Symptoms: Unconsciousness, organ failure, death without intervention.

## System
*   **Hemostasis:** The process of stopping blood flow.
*   **Shock Management:** The system response to significant blood loss (inadequate blood flow to vital organs).
    *   Signs of Shock: Rapid weak pulse, pale cold skin, confusion, and sometimes without visible blood loss.
    *   Key System Requirement: Keeping the patient warm, as hypothermia worsens bleeding because cold blood clots poorly.

## Technology
*   **Direct Pressure:** Stops most bleeding, including many arterial bleeds.
*   **Pressure Bandage:** Converts direct pressure to free hands, maintaining constant compression to achieve hemostasis for hours.
*   **Elevation:** Reduces blood pressure at the wound site by working with gravity.
*   **Wound Packing:** Used for deep wounds where surface pressure fails or when compressing through tissue is necessary.
*   **Pressure Points:** Used to compress major arteries upstream of the injury to buy time.
*   **Hemostatic Agents:** Commercial products used to concentrate clotting factors and speed hemostasis.

## Tools
*   Clean cloth (shirt, bandana, towel, gauze).
*   Pressure bandage materials (long strip of cloth, elastic bandage, belt, or flexible material).
*   Hemostatic Agents (QuikClot / Celox / HemCon, Yarrow).

## Process
**The Three-Step Protocol:**

**Step 1: Direct Pressure**
1.  Grab the cleanest cloth available. If nothing is available, use the bare hand.
2.  Press firmly directly on the wound, using the palm flat, centered on the wound, applying body weight.
3.  Hold for a minimum of 10 minutes without lifting to check.
4.  If the cloth soaks through, add more material on top and increase pressure. Do not remove the first cloth.

*Critical Rule:* Do NOT keep lifting the dressing to check if bleeding has stopped; this resets the clotting clock.

**Step 2: Pressure Bandage**
1.  Keep the original dressing in place. Add more clean cloth padding on top if needed.
2.  Wrap firmly with a long strip of cloth, elastic bandage, belt, or flexible material, overlapping each pass by half.
3.  Check circulation below the bandage; loosen if fingers or toes turn blue, pale, cold, or numb.

**Step 3: Elevation**
*   If the wound is on a limb, elevate it above the level of the heart (e.g., prop the arm on clothing or a pack; support the leg on a log or gear stack).

**When Pressure Fails: Wound Packing**
1.  Use clean cloth strips (roughly 2-3 inches wide).
2.  Push the fabric into the wound cavity tightly, working from the bottom up.
3.  Continue packing until the cavity is filled and the fabric is level with or slightly above the skin surface.
4.  Apply direct pressure on top of the packed wound with the palm.
5.  Hold for 10 minutes, then secure with a pressure bandage.

**Pressure Points (To Buy Time):**
*   Upper arm (Brachial): Press fingers into the inner arm, between bicep and tricep, against the humerus.
*   Groin/upper thigh (Femoral): Press hard with the heel of the hand into the crease where the leg meets the pelvis.
*   Neck (Carotid): Press into the side of the neck, in front of the large muscle; NEVER compress both sides.
*   Wrist (Radial/Ulnar): Press against the inner wrist bones.
*   Behind the knee (Popliteal): Press deep behind the knee joint.

**Internal Bleeding Recognition:**
*   Signs include:
    *   Rigid or distended abdomen after blunt trauma.
    *   Deep bruising over the torso.
    *   Coughing or vomiting blood.
    *   Blood in urine or stool.
    *   Shock symptoms (rapid weak pulse, pale cold skin, confusion) without visible blood loss.
    *   Pain and swelling at fracture sites (a broken femur can lose 1-2 liters internally).

## Notes
*   The most common fatal mistake is lifting the dressing to check the bleeding.
*   For deep wounds, wound packing is essential to compress the vessel directly inside the wound.
*   Hypothermia is a key factor, as keeping the patient warm prevents blood from clotting poorly.
*   Knowing the three bleeding types (Arterial, Venous, Capillary) dictates the initial response.
