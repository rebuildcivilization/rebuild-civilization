# Bow Drill Fire Starting

## Objective
To reliably start a fire using the bow drill method, which is the most reliable friction fire method because it multiplies effort.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   **Materials:** Dead, dry softwood for both fireboard and spindle (willow, cottonwood, and cedar are top choices). A cleanly cut 1/8-circle notch in the fireboard. A suitable bow. A handhold (socket).
*   **Technique:** Proper application of downward pressure and correct positioning to ensure the spindle stays vertical.

## Material
| Component | Recommended Wood Type | Quality Requirements | Dimensions/Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Fireboard (Hearth Board) | Willow, Cottonwood, Cedar, Basswood, Poplar | Must be dead, dry softwood; not green, rotten, or resinous. | Roughly 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) long, 2-3 inches (5-7 cm) wide, 3/4 inch (2 cm) thick. |
| Spindle (Drill) | Same species as fireboard or equally soft. | Straight, dry stick. | About 8-10 inches (20-25 cm) long, 3/4 inch (2 cm) in diameter. Must be rounded on one end and carved to a blunt point. |
| Bow | Any curved or slightly flexible branch. | Rigidity is helpful; slight natural curve is ideal. | 18-24 inches (45-60 cm) long, about thumb thickness. |
| Handhold (Socket) | Hard piece of wood, stone with a depression, bone, shell, or hardwood knot. | Must be hard enough to apply downward pressure. | Sits on top of the spindle. |
| Tinder Bundle | Cedar bark fibers, dried grass, cattail fluff, birch bark shavings. | Must be fine, dry, and suitable for catching an ember. |

## System
The bow drill functions by converting rotational energy into heat through mechanical advantage, multiplying the operator's effort.

## Technology
*   **Friction:** Wood rubbing against wood generates friction heat.
*   **Mechanical Advantage:** The bow maintains constant rotation while the operator applies steady downward pressure, allowing the system to generate heat.
*   **Physics:** Friction heat generated by wood rubbing against wood reaches ignition temperature (roughly 400-450°F or 200-230°C), forming a glowing coal.

## Tools
*   Knife/Carving tool (to prepare the notch and spindle).
*   Fire (to test materials).
*   Tinder bundle (to transfer the ember).

## Process
### Step 1: Prepare the Fireboard
1. Carve a small starter depression about 3/4 inch (2 cm) from the edge of the board.
2. Burn in the hole first: place the spindle in the depression and bow back and forth until a dark, round socket about the diameter of the spindle is created.
3. Cut the notch: Carve a V-shaped or pie-slice notch from the edge of the board into the center of the burned socket. The notch should be roughly 1/8 of the circle (a 45-degree wedge).

### Step 2: Set Up Your Position
1. Place a thin piece of bark, dry leaf, or flat chip of wood under the notch to catch the ember.
2. Kneel with the right foot pinning the fireboard to the ground (left foot if left-handed).
3. Rest the left wrist or forearm against the left shin to lock the hand in place and keep the spindle vertical.
4. Hold the handhold in the left hand, pressing down on top of the spindle.

### Step 3: Bow Technique
1. Wrap the string once around the spindle, ensuring the spindle is on the outside of the string (between the string and the operator).
2. Grip the bow at the far end with the right hand.
3. Begin with slow, full-length strokes to warm the set and seat the spindle.
4. Gradually increase speed while maintaining strong downward pressure. The spindle must stay vertical (lock the wrist).

### Step 4: Creating the Ember
1. When thick smoke pours from the notch and continues when paused, an ember is present.
2. Give 10-15 more strong strokes, then carefully lift the spindle away.
3. The notch should contain a small pile of dark brown or black dust. If it smokes on its own, an ember exists.

### Step 5: Transfer to Tinder Bundle
1. Carefully tip the ember onto the tinder bundle (a bird’s-nest-shaped ball of fine, dry material).
2. Fold the bundle loosely around the ember and blow steadily, increasing intensity until the bundle bursts into flame.

## Troubleshooting
| Problem | Likely Cause | Fix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| No smoke at all | Wood too hard, wet, or resinous | Switch species; test with thumbnail |
| Light smoke but no ember | Notch too small or wrong angle | Widen to 1/8 of circle; cut cleanly |
| Dust is light brown | Not enough pressure | Push harder; reduce speed slightly |
| String slips on spindle | Cord too smooth or loose | Roughen cord; add half-wrap |
| Spindle pops out | Too much lateral force | Lock wrist against shin; stay vertical |
| Ember dies in tinder | Tinder too coarse or damp | Use finer, drier material; blow steadily |

## Extending Your Set’s Life
*   A good fireboard can produce 8-12 embers before the notches wear out.
*   Rotate to fresh spots along the board.
*   Keep the kit dry—store it inside shelter or wrap it in bark.

## Key Takeaways
*   Use **dead, dry softwood** for both fireboard and spindle; same species is ideal.
*   The notch is the most critical element: 1/8 circle (45-degree wedge), cut cleanly to the center of the burned socket.
*   **Pressure matters more than speed**—lock your wrist against your shin and push down hard.
*   An ember will smoke on its own when you stop drilling; don’t rush the transfer.
*   Protect your working set from moisture—a proven kit is a survival asset.

## Notes
*   The bow drill multiplies effort by maintaining constant rotation while the operator applies steady downward pressure.
*   The physics dictate that friction heat (400-450°F / 200-230°C) is required to generate an ember.
*   Common mistakes include letting the spindle wobble (failure to lock the wrist) and using too much speed without sufficient downward pressure.
