# Bow Drill, Hand Drill, Fire Plough

## Objective
To create fire by friction, which involves rubbing two pieces of wood together until fine dust accumulates in a notch and reaches roughly 250–315°C, coalescing into a glowing ember.
The ignition temperature of wood is not a fixed value and depends on the conditions, the material and the process used.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Correct materials.
*   Proper technique.
*   Practice.
*   Dry wood selection (80% of success).

## Material
**Wood Selection:**
*   Must be dry (snap test: bend the wood; if it snaps cleanly, it is dry enough).
*   Must be soft to medium hardness (think balsa to cedar range).
*   Must be non-resinous (Pine, spruce, conifers contain resin that gums up the friction interface).
*   Must be straight-grained.
*   Spindle and fireboard must be the same type of wood or very similar hardness.
*   Cordage must be strong enough not to break and rough enough to grip the spindle without slipping (Paracord, rawhide strip, yucca fiber rope, or twisted bark cordage).

**Best Wood Species (by region):
*   North America:
    *   Excellent: Cottonwood, willow, basswood, cedar
    *   Good: Aspen, poplar, box elder
    *   Avoid: Pine, oak, maple, hickory
*   Europe:
    *   Excellent: Willow, lime/linden, clematis, ivy
    *   Good: Elder, poplar, hazel
    *   Avoid: Beech, oak, ash, pine
*   Tropical:
    *   Excellent: Hibiscus, sotol, fig, frangipani
    *   Good: Bamboo (dry, dead), balsa
    *   Avoid: Teak, mahogany, ironwood
*   Arid/Desert:
    *   Excellent: Sotol, yucca, cottonwood
    *   Good: Willow (near water), sage
    *   Avoid: Mesquite, acacia, creosote

## System
**Technology: Friction Fire**
Friction fire is the oldest ignition technology, relying on the physics where two pieces of wood rub together, generating heat through friction. The softer wood produces fine dust particles that collect in a notch. When the dust reaches roughly 250–315 degrees C (the ignition point of wood), it coalesces into a glowing ember.
The ignition temperature of wood is not a fixed value and depends on the conditions, the material and the process used.

**Variables Determining Success:**
1.  **Speed:** Faster friction generates heat faster.
2.  **Pressure:** More downward force increases friction.
3.  **Duration:** Sustained effort prevents the hot zone from cooling between strokes.

**Failure Mode:** Heat is generated but dissipates faster than it accumulates due to wrong wood, wrong speed, wrong pressure, or taking a break at the wrong moment.

## Tools
**Method 1: Bow Drill Components**
*   **Spindle:** 30-45 cm long, 2 cm diameter, straight and round. Carved bottom end to a blunt point; top end carved to a narrower point for the bearing block.
*   **Fireboard:** 30 cm long, at least 5 cm wide, 1.5-2 cm thick, flat on both sides. Same wood type as the spindle.
*   **Bow:** A sturdy, slightly curved stick (60-75 cm long).
*   **Bearing block:** Palm-sized piece of hardwood, stone, bone, or shell with a smooth dimple. Must be HARDER and SMOOTHER than the spindle top.
*   **Cordage:** Must be strong and rough enough to grip the spindle.

**Method 2: Hand Drill Components**
*   **Spindle:** 45-75 cm long, 1-1.5 cm diameter, must be very straight.
*   **Fireboard:** Same as bow drill (flat, soft, dry, with prepared dimple and notch).

**Method 3: Fire Plough Components**
*   **Plough stick:** A hardwood stick 30-45 cm long with a blunt, slightly rounded tip (must be harder than the base).
*   **Base board:** A flat piece of soft, dry wood at least 30 cm long, with a shallow groove 15-20 cm long down the center.

## Process
**General Friction Process:** Rub two pieces of wood until dust reaches 250-315°C and coalesces into a glowing ember. Transfer the ember to a tinder bundle and blow steadily until flames erupt.

Friction fire is a group of techniques that generate heat through
the relative movement of two materials.

The most important variables include:

- Material selection and moisture content.
- Contact geometry and surface condition.
- Applied pressure.
- Movement speed and consistency.
- Heat retention around the contact area.
- The ability to transfer a developing ember into suitable tinder.

Different techniques distribute effort and pressure differently.
A method that works well for one combination of materials may fail
when the same technique is applied to unsuitable materials.

**Practice Schedule:**
*   **Week 1:** Gather materials; practice carving spindles, preparing fireboards, and cutting notches; make 3-4 complete bow drill sets.
*   **Week 2:** Practice the bow drill; focus on form (locked wrist, full strokes, steady rhythm). Aim to get smoke reliably.
*   **Week 3:** Get the first ember; practice the transfer to tinder bundle and blowing it to flame until the sequence is fluid.
*   **Week 4:** Try the hand drill, then the fire plough; compare success rates and identify the best method for local wood.
*   **Ongoing:** Make fire by friction at least once per month, using different wood species, and practice in different weather conditions.

## Notes
*   Wood selection is the most critical factor (80% of the battle).
*   The bow drill is the most reliable method and should be mastered first.
*   The V-notch geometry is critical: it must be a pie-slice shape, approximately 1/8th of the circle (45 degrees), cut to full depth.
*   Body mechanics are paramount: lock the wrist to the shin and maintain steady pressure.
*   The hand drill will blister palms; this is normal and can become infected if hands are already injured.
*   Troubleshooting highlights: String slip requires roughening the spindle; smoke from the top requires lubrication of the bearing dimple; dark dust but no ember requires a properly cut notch.
*   The fire plough is the least reliable but requires the fewest preparation skills.
