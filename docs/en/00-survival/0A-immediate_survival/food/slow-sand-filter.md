# Slow Sand Filter

## Objective
To provide the most reliable low-technology water treatment available by removing over 99% of bacterial pathogens without the use of chemicals or electricity.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- Patience is required for maturation: A new filter takes 2–4 weeks to mature.
- The filter must never be allowed to dry out, as the biofilm dies within hours and requires weeks to re-establish.
- During the maturation period, all drinking water must be boiled.

## Material
- A watertight container (e.g., concrete box, stone-mortared basin, large barrel, or lined pit).
- Fine sand (0.15-0.35 mm), washed.
- Coarse gravel (5-20 mm).
- A drain pipe or outlet tube positioned at the bottom.
- A means to control flow rate (valve, adjustable siphon, or calibrated outlet).

## System
The slow sand filter functions as a living ecosystem disguised as a box of sand.

## Technology
**Core Mechanism:** Water percolates downward through a deep bed of fine sand at a very slow rate (0.1-0.3 meters per hour).

**Four Simultaneous Processes:**
1. **Physical straining:** Traps particles larger than pore spaces (~50 micrometers).
2. **Sedimentation:** Deposits smaller particles on grain surfaces as water velocity drops.
3. **Adsorption:** Binds dissolved organics to biological coatings on sand grains.
4. **Biological predation:** Occurs in the schmutzdecke (biological layer) that colonizes the upper sand layer and consumes waterborne pathogens, organic matter, and nutrients.

**Critical Component:** The schmutzdecke—a biological community of bacteria, protozoa, and other microorganisms that colonizes the upper sand layer.

## Tools
- Calibration method: A container of known volume to measure flow rate.
- Testing Sand Grain Size: A jar of still water from 5 cm above the surface to observe particle settling time.

## Process
### Construction: Step by Step
1. **Build the Container:** Ensure it is watertight. Options include a barrel method, concrete block box, ferrocement tank, or stone masonry. Must have a drain outlet at the very bottom.
2. **Install the Underdrain:**
    - Layer 1: Place a perforated drain pipe at the bottom, sloped slightly toward the outlet.
    - Layer 2: Cover with 5-10 cm of coarse gravel (15-20 mm).
    - Layer 3: Add 5-10 cm of fine gravel (3-6 mm) as a transition layer.
3. **Add the Sand Bed:** Fill with pre-washed fine sand to a depth of 80-100 cm, adding in 10-15 cm lifts, flooding each lift before adding the next.
4. **Establish Supernatant Depth:** Maintain water level above the sand at 30-60 cm to provide hydraulic head, prevent surface disturbance, and act as a reserve.
5. **Set the Flow Rate:** Calculate flow rate using the formula: Flow rate (m/hr) = Volume collected (liters) / (Filter area in m2 x Time in hours x 1000). Target flow rate is 0.1-0.2 m/hr.
6. **Maturation Period:** Allow 2-4 weeks for the biological layer to develop. Monitor for flow rate decrease, improved output clarity, a visible slimy layer, and the disappearance of raw water smell.

### Operating Your Filter
**Daily Operation:**
- Maintain continuous flow, ideally 24 hours a day.
- Keep water level above the sand surface.
- Add source water gently through the diffuser, avoiding disturbance.

**Monitoring Performance:
| Indicator | Normal | Problem | 
|---|---|---|
| Flow rate | 0.1-0.3 m/hr | Below 0.05 (needs cleaning)
| Output clarity | Clear, no visible particles | Cloudy (sand channeling or bed disturbance)
| Output taste | Clean, no earthy or swampy taste | Off-taste (anaerobic conditions developing)
| Sand surface | Thin greenish-brown layer | Thick black layer (overloaded, clean soon)
| Output smell | None | Sulfur/rotten egg (anaerobic, serious problem)

**When Things Go Wrong:**
- **Anaerobic conditions (sulfur smell, black sand):** Filter is oxygen-depleted due to high organic loading or thick biofilm. Fix by scraping the top 2-3 cm. Pre-treatment (settling basin) may be needed for high organic load.
- **Channeling (fast flow, cloudy output):** Water bypassed filtration, usually after the filter dries out. Fix by draining, re-leveling, compacting, and refilling.
- **Freezing:** Unprotected filters will crack. Insulate with straw bales, earth mounding, or build inside a shelter.

## Output Quality
A properly matured and maintained filter typically achieves the following removal rates:

| Contaminant | Removal Rate |
|---|---|
| Turbidity | 95-99% |
| E. coli and coliform bacteria | 95-99% |
| Giardia cysts | 99%+ |
| Cryptosporidium oocysts | 99%+ |
| Viruses | 90-99% (variable) |
| Organic matter | 50-80% |
| Iron and manganese | 30-70% |

## Key Takeaways
- The biological layer (schmutzdecke) is the critical element, performing the actual pathogen removal.
- Patience is essential; the filter requires 2-4 weeks to mature.
- Proper flow rate and sand grain size are non-negotiable for biological treatment.
- A dry filter is a dead filter that needs weeks to recover.

Notes
- The technology is proven historically, demonstrated by London's experience with cholera in 1854.
- The biological action alone is sufficient for pathogen removal, requiring no chemicals or electricity.
- The biological layer generates a small amount of heat that aids in freezing protection, but insufficient in hard freezes.
- For detailed procedures, reference [Filter Maintenance] and [Biofilm Layer].

## How It Works
A slow sand filter is a living ecosystem where water percolates slowly through fine sand, resulting in physical straining, sedimentation, adsorption, and biological predation by the schmutzdecke.

## Historical Proof
- The first large-scale slow sand filters were installed in London in 1829.
- Neighborhoods served by sand-filtered water experienced dramatically lower death rates during the 1854 cholera outbreak.
