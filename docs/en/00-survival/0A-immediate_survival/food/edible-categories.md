# Edible Categories | Survival Wiki

## Objective
> Wild edible plants fall into distinct categories based on which part you eat and how you process it. Understanding these categories helps you systematically assess any environment for food potential.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Knowledge of the six foraging categories.
*   Ability to assess an environment for food potential.

## Material
**The Six Foraging Categories:**
1. Leafy Greens
2. Roots and Tubers
3. Nuts and Seeds
4. Fruits and Berries
5. Bark and Cambium
6. Flowers and Pollen

**Nutritional Strategy:**
*   Leafy greens provide vitamins and minerals but almost no calories.
*   Roots and tubers provide starch and energy.
*   Nuts and seeds deliver fats and protein.
*   Fruits offer quick sugar and vitamins.

**Minimum Daily Calorie Target:** An active adult in a survival situation needs 1,500-2,500 calories per day.

**Seasonal Strategy Focus:**
*   Spring: Greens, shoots, roots (highest vitamin content).
*   Summer: Berries, fruits, greens, pollen (peak variety).
*   Autumn: Nuts, seeds, roots, late fruits (critical harvest season).
*   Winter: Stored nuts/seeds, cambium, pine needles, roots (lowest availability).

## System
The system categorizes wild edible plants based on two criteria: the part of the plant consumed and the processing method applied.

**Category Details:**

**1. Leafy Greens**
*   **What they are:** Young leaves, shoots, and tender stems eaten raw or cooked.
*   **Nutritional role:** Vitamins (A, C, K), minerals (iron, calcium), fiber. Very low in calories.
*   **Where to find them:** Disturbed ground (roadsides, clearings, old fields), forest edges, stream banks.

**2. Roots and Tubers**
*   **What they are:** Underground storage organs — taproots, rhizomes, tubers, corms, and bulbs.
*   **Nutritional role:** Starch (calories), some protein, minerals. Primary calorie source.
*   **Where to find them:** Marshes and wetlands (cattail), fields and roadsides (burdock, wild carrot), forest floors (Jerusalem artichoke).

**3. Nuts and Seeds**
*   **What they are:** Hard-shelled or husked reproductive structures from trees and plants.
*   **Nutritional role:** Fats, protein, calories. The most calorie-dense wild foods.
*   **Where to find them:** Deciduous forests (oaks, walnuts, hickory), pine forests (pine nuts), grasslands (grass seeds).

**4. Fruits and Berries**
*   **What they are:** Fleshy reproductive structures containing seeds, designed by the plant to be eaten.
*   **Nutritional role:** Sugars (quick energy), vitamins (especially C), water content. Low in calories and protein.
*   **Where to find them:** Forest edges, hedgerows, clearings, stream banks. (Fruits need sunlight).

**5. Bark and Cambium**
*   **What they are:** The inner bark (cambium layer) — the soft, moist tissue between the hard outer bark and the wood of a tree.
*   **Nutritional role:** Starch, fiber, some sugars. Emergency calorie source.
*   **Best species:** Pine, birch, willow, elm, maple, poplar.

**6. Flowers and Pollen**
*   **What they are:** Reproductive structures of flowering plants, including petals, whole flower heads, and pollen.
*   **Nutritional role:** Minor calories, some vitamins, useful primarily as flavor and variety. Cattail pollen is high in protein.

## Tools
*   Knife or sharp stone (for scraping bark).
*   Processing techniques (boiling, roasting, threshing, winnowing, grinding).

## Process
**Foraging Process:**
1.  **Assessment:** Systematically assess the environment for food potential using the six categories.
2.  **Identification:** Identify species within the categories based on habitat and characteristic features.
3.  **Harvesting:** Apply specific harvest rules (e.g., taking only one-third of a patch for greens; collecting fallen nuts).
4.  **Processing:** Apply necessary processing techniques based on the category (e.g., cooking nettles, peeling cattail shoots, crushing acorns).
5.  **Nutritional Balancing:** Combine categories to build a balanced diet, ensuring calories are met while vitamin gaps are filled.

## Notes
*   **The "weed" rule:** If a plant aggressively colonizes disturbed ground, it is disproportionately likely to be edible because evolution favors fast-growing, nutrient-dense species in these niches (Dandelion, plantain, lamb’s quarters, chickweed).
*   **The carrot family warning:** Wild carrot (Queen Anne’s lace), wild parsnip, and relatives look identical to poison hemlock and water hemlock. Positive distinction (hairy solid stem for carrot; smooth purple-blotched hollow stem for hemlock) is mandatory.
*   **Elderberry Rule:** Elderberries must be cooked because raw elderberries contain cyanogenic glycosides that cause severe nausea and vomiting.
*   **Bark Sustainability:** Stripping bark from a living tree in a complete ring (girdling) kills it; always take bark from one side only, limiting the area to less than one-quarter of the circumference.
*   **Critical Timing:** Autumn nut harvest is the most critical survival task, as stored food determines winter survival.
