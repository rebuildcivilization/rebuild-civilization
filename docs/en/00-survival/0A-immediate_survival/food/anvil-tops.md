# Anvil Tops | Survival Wiki

## Objective
To reveal storm severity, direction, and intensity at a glance by observing the flat, spreading top of a thunderstorm.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- Observation of thunderstorms.
- Understanding of atmospheric layers (troposphere, stratosphere, tropopause).
- Awareness of upper-level winds and the jet stream.

## Material
- **Anvil Tops:** The flat, spreading top of a thunderstorm, made entirely of ice crystals carried outward by upper-level winds.
- **Overshooting Tops:** A dome or bubble of cloud protruding above the flat anvil, indicating exceptionally strong updrafts.
- **Back-Sheared Anvils:** Anvil portions extending upwind, indicating extreme updraft strength.
- **Flanking Line:** A line of towering cumulus stepping down in height on the southwest side of severe thunderstorms, indicating active air ingestion.

## System
- **Updraft Mechanism:** Warm, moist air is pushed upward at speeds exceeding 100 mph, cooling and condensing to form the towering cumulonimbus.
- **Ceiling Mechanism:** The tropopause acts as a ceiling where the updraft slams into the boundary between the troposphere and stratosphere, halting vertical ascent.
- **Wind Field:** Upper-level winds (jet stream) dictate the horizontal spread of the anvil.

## Technology
- **Visual Interpretation:** Using the anvil shape and related features to diagnose storm characteristics.
- **Intensity Indicator:** Anvil brightness (brilliant white for thick/dense ice) and lightning rate (more than one flash per second for extremely powerful storms).
- **Directional Indicator:** Anvil direction reveals storm movement based on upper-level flow.

## Process
### How Anvils Form
1. **Updraft:** Warm, moist air is pushed upward at speeds exceeding 100 mph.
2. **Condensation:** The column cools and condenses, forming the cumulonimbus.
3. **Ceiling Encounter:** The updraft hits the tropopause, acting as a ceiling.
4. **Spread:** The cloud top spreads horizontally, forming the flat anvil shape, made of ice crystals carried by upper-level winds.

### Reading the Anvil
- **Direction:** Observe the downwind spread to determine storm movement.
- **Size:** Determine storm power; massive anvils mean a powerful storm.
- **Brightness:** A brilliant white anvil indicates a thick anvil with dense ice (strong storm).

### Severe Weather Signals
- **Overshooting Tops:** Identify a rounded dome above the anvil to confirm exceptional updraft strength, signaling the potential for large hail, damaging winds, or tornadoes. Action required: Seek solid shelter immediately.
- **Back-Sheared Anvils:** Identify an anvil portion extending upwind to confirm extreme updraft strength, indicating a very high probability of severe weather.

### Distance Estimation
- **Full Anvil + Dark Tower:** Storm is 20-50 miles away (30-90 minutes).
- **Significant Anvil + Visible Dark Base:** Storm is 10-20 miles away (15-45 minutes).
- **Overhead Anvil + Visible Horizon Base:** Storm is 5-10 miles away (10-20 minutes).
- **Thunder Heard:** Storm is within 10 miles.

### Multi-Day Planning
- **Stable Pattern:** Anvils pointing the same direction indicate a stable upper-level wind pattern, suggesting continued movement.
- **Shifting Pattern:** Changing anvil direction suggests an incoming weather regime within 1-3 days.
- **Increasing Size:** Successive days with larger anvils mean increasing atmospheric instability and a peak risk of severe weather on the day of the largest anvil.

### Night Observation
- **Inference:** Continuous lightning illuminating a large dome shape above the horizon indicates a cumulonimbus with an anvil. A huge illuminated area signals a severe storm. The rate of lightning flashes indicates intensity (more than one flash per second = extremely powerful storm).

## Notes
- The most dangerous storm type, a supercell thunderstorm, is visually confirmed when a back-sheared anvil is combined with an overshooting top.
- The anvil can extend 50-100 miles downwind from the storm tower.
- Anvil rollover occurs when the updraft pulls the anvil ice crystals back downward, signifying a powerful, well-organized storm.
- Storms without flanking lines are typically weakening.
- The core information extracted from the source text is highly focused on the visual cues associated with convective storm structure and kinematics.
