# Salt Sources and Food Preservation

## Objective
The primary objective is to identify salt sources and methods for long-term food preservation, recognizing that without salt, long-term food preservation is severely limited. Salt is identified as the single most important mineral for post-collapse survival, essential for food preservation, and necessary for maintaining nerve function, muscle contraction, and fluid balance in the body.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   **Salt:** Essential for dietary needs (3-5 grams of sodium per day) and preservation (roughly 1 pound of salt per 5 pounds of meat for dry curing, plus salt for brining vegetables and tanning hides).
*   **Reliable Sources:** Finding reliable sources is a top priority for long-term survival.

## Material
*   Seawater (contains 3.5% salt by weight).
*   Rock Salt and Mineral Deposits.
*   Plant Ash (Coltsfoot, Glasswort/samphire, Saltwort, Hickory bark, Dried kelp/seaweed).
*   Meat and vegetables (for curing and brining).
*   Hides (for tanning).
*   Containers (for collection and storage).

## System
*   **Sodium Chloride System:** The primary salt form, which satisfies dietary sodium requirements and is the base for most preservation.
*   **Potassium Chloride System:** Produced by plant ash; works for preservation (draws moisture via osmosis) but does not satisfy dietary sodium requirements.

## Technology
*   **Solar Evaporation:** A method relying on direct sunlight and wind exposure for salt concentration.
*   **Boiling Method:** A faster but fuel-intensive method for concentrating seawater.
*   **Leaching/Refinement:** A chemical process used to extract dissolved salts from plant ash.

## Tools
*   Wide, shallow containers (baking trays, shallow pits lined with clay or plastic, or flat rocks).
*   Large pots (for boiling).
*   Hammer stone (for crushing rock salt).
*   Cloth (for filtering brine).
*   Hot fire/Heat source (for boiling and burning plant material).
*   Driest containers (sealed plastic, glazed ceramic, or metal with a tight lid) for storage.

## Process
### Source 1: Seawater Evaporation
*   **Solar Evaporation Steps:**
    1. Collect seawater in clean containers (avoiding harbor water).
    2. Pour into wide, shallow containers.
    3. Place in direct sunlight with maximum wind exposure; keep water depth under 2 inches (5 cm).
    4. Stir daily to prevent surface crust formation.
    5. Add more seawater to the same pan to build salt concentration.
    6. Stop adding water when a thick slurry remains and let it dry completely.
    7. Scrape up crystallized salt and crush chunks.
*   **Boiling Method Steps:**
    1. Bring seawater to a rolling boil.
    2. Keep boiling until volume reduces by 90%.
    3. Reduce heat to a bare simmer once crystals start forming.
    4. Spread on a flat surface to dry completely.

### Source 2: Rock Salt and Mineral Deposits
*   **Identifying Deposits:**
    *   Salt licks: Where animals congregate at mineral outcrops.
    *   Crystalline crusts: White or gray on rock faces, cave walls, or dry lake beds.
    *   Salt springs: Indicate underground deposits.
    *   Dry lake beds (playas): Taste test carefully; some contain sodium carbonate instead of sodium chloride.
*   **Processing Rock Salt Steps:**
    1. Crush the deposit into small pieces using a hammer stone.
    2. Dissolve in water using the minimum amount needed.
    3. Let sediment settle for 30 minutes, then carefully pour off the clear brine through cloth.
    4. Boil or evaporate the filtered brine to re-crystallize clean salt.

### Source 3: Plant Ash (Potash Salt)
*   **Ash Salt Extraction Process:**
    1. Dry the plant material completely (sun-dry for 2-3 days or dry near a fire).
    2. Burn to ash in a hot fire (aim for white or gray ash).
    3. Leach the ash: place ash in a container with a small hole, pour clean water slowly through, and collect the brown lye water.
    4. Evaporate the lye water: boil until crystals form.
    5. Refine (optional): dissolve crude salt, filter, and re-evaporate to improve purity.

### Source 4: Scavenging
*   Salt is heavy and low-value but more valuable than almost anything else carried.
*   Prioritize salt during scavenging runs (grocery stores, road maintenance depots, farm/feed stores, restaurant supply, water softener salt).

### Estimating Salt Needs
| Use | Amount Per Person Per Month | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Dietary intake | 3-5 oz (90-150 g) | Higher in hot weather or hard labor |
| Meat curing | 3-5 lbs (1.4-2.3 kg) | Based on 1 lb salt per 5 lbs meat |
| Vegetable fermentation | 0.5-1 lb (0.2-0.5 kg) | 2-3% salt by vegetable weight |
| Hide tanning | 2-5 lbs per hide | Varies by animal size |
| Cheese making | 0.5-1 lb per batch | Brining and surface salting |
| **Total per person** | **6-12 lbs (2.7-5.4 kg)** | **Conservative estimate** |

### Storage
Salt does not spoil. Store in the driest container available (sealed plastic, glazed ceramic, or metal with a tight lid). If salt clumps when wet, break it up and dry it out. Keep salt elevated off dirt floors and away from direct rain.

## Notes
*   **Impurities:** Crude sea salt contains magnesium chloride and calcium sulfate, which taste bitter. For preservation, this is acceptable.
*   **Potash vs. Sodium Salt:** Plant ash yields potassium salts, which are better for preservation (osmosis) but must be supplemented with sodium chloride (from mineral or seawater) to meet dietary needs, as potassium does not satisfy sodium requirements.
*   **Yield Comparison:** Solar evaporation yields about 4.5 oz of salt per gallon; boiling is faster but fuel-intensive (roughly 1 pound of dry hardwood per gallon boiled).
*   **Key Takeaway:** Salt never expires; scavenging priority is high.
