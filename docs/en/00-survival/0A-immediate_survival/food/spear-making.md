# Spear Making

## Objective
To make a hunting spear.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- A straight live hardwood shaft (ash or hickory preferred).
- Suitable stone tip for cutting/piercing.
- Binding materials (sinew, rawhide, or plant cordage).
- Adhesive/Waterproofing agent (pine pitch mixed with crushed charcoal).

## Material
### Wood Species
**Hardwoods (Preferred):**
- Ash (strong, straight-grained, excellent shock resistance; best all-around).
- Hickory (extremely tough, superb for thrusting spears).
- Oak (heavy and strong, better for thrusting than throwing).
- Maple (good balance of weight and strength).
**Softwoods (Acceptable):**
- Yew (tough for a softwood).
- Pine (widely available; straight saplings for quick spears).
- Spruce (lightweight, reasonably straight; good for temporary spears).
**Woods to Avoid:**
- Willow (bends too easily).
- Elm (splits unpredictably).
- Poplar (too soft).
- Birch (decent but rots quickly when wet).
- Dead, dry wood from the ground (brittle and will shatter).

### Stone Types
Flint, chert, obsidian, jasper, quartzite (Any fine-grained stone that fractures with sharp edges).

### Binding Materials
- Animal sinew (Superior, makes the strongest natural binding).
- Rawhide strips.
- Plant cordage (from nettle, dogbane, or inner bark fibers).

## System
The system involves transforming a crude pole into a lethal tool through the combination of a chosen shaft and a fire-hardened, stone-tipped point. The spear is considered humanity’s oldest hunting weapon, requiring the least skill to build and the least practice to use effectively, making it versatile for hunting and defense.

## Technology
### Fire-Hardening
Raw wood consists roughly of 60% cellulose, 25% lignin, and 15% water plus other compounds. Gentle heating between 150–250°C (300–480°F) drives out water and begins cross-linking the cellulose polymers. The result is a surface 2–3 times harder than the original wood.

### Stone Tip Function
Transforms the spear from a poking tool into a cutting weapon capable of causing fatal hemorrhaging.

## Tools
- Knife or sharp stone tool (for cutting/shaping the shaft).
- Sharp stone edge (for re-sharpening).
- Coals (for fire-hardening).
- Measure tools (to ensure correct shaft length and diameter).

## Process
### Harvesting and Initial Preparation
1. Select a live sapling or straight branch at least 15 cm longer than the target length.
2. Cut cleanly at the base with a knife, hatchet, or sharp stone tool (must not snap or twist).
3. Remove all side branches flush with the shaft.
4. Strip the bark if desired (provides grip but risks moisture/rot).
5. Straighten by heating gently over coals and bending, holding in position until cool.

### Shaft Selection
- **Ideal Properties:**
    - Straightness: Naturally straight growth, minimal curve.
    - Diameter: 2.5–4 cm (1–1.5 inches) for throwing; 3–5 cm for thrusting.
    - Length: 1.5–2.5 m (5–8 feet) depending on use.
    - Weight: Dense enough to carry momentum.
    - Flexibility: Slight flex without breaking.

### Fire-Hardening the Tip
1. **Carve the tip first:** Make the point 10–15 cm long for thrusting or 7–10 cm for throwing.
2. **Build a bed of coals:** Achieve steady, moderate heat without active flames.
3. **Rotate the tip slowly:** Move the tip 10–15 cm above the coals constantly (like a rotisserie).
4. **Observe:** The wood should turn a deep brown or light tan, never black.
5. **Test:** Press a fingernail into the surface to check resistance.
6. **Scrape:** Scrape off any char to reveal the hardened wood beneath.

### Hafting the Stone Point
**Split-shaft method (Strongest):**
1. Split the shaft end 8–12 cm deep.
2. Insert the point base, centered and aligned.
3. Bind tightly with wet sinew or rawhide strips.
4. Apply pine pitch or tree resin over the binding as adhesive/waterproofing.
5. Let dry completely (sinew shrinks, tightening the bond).

**Socket method (Simpler):**
1. Hollow out the shaft end 5–7 cm deep by burning and scraping.
2. Shape the point base to fit the socket snugly.
3. Glue with pine pitch (heat pine resin with crushed charcoal at a 3:1 ratio).
4. Bind over the joint for insurance.

### Spear Types by Function
| Type | Length | Shaft Diameter | Point Detail | Use Case | Key Feature |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Thrusting Spear | 1.8–2.5 m (6–8 ft) | 3–5 cm | Longer, narrower point | Ambush, blinds, defense | Two-handed grip, thrust with full body weight |
| Throwing Spear (Javelin) | 1.5–2 m (5–6.5 ft) | 2–2.5 cm | Lighter shaft; Balance 40–45% from tip | Throwing with effective range of 10–20 m | Lighter shaft, balance point critical |
| Fishing Spear (Gig) | 2–3 m (7–10 ft) | N/A | Split tip into 2–4 prongs; sharp and fire-hardened; backward-facing barbs | Reach into water | Multi-prong structure |

### Maintenance and Field Repairs
- Re-sharpen by scraping the tip against a rough stone in one direction.
- Re-harden the tip over coals after resharpening (takes only a few minutes).
- Check bindings daily if using a stone tip; retighten or re-wrap at the first sign of looseness.
- Keep the shaft oiled with rendered animal fat to prevent drying and cracking.
- Store vertically or hanging horizontally (avoid leaning to prevent warping).

## Notes
- The key takeaway is that the shaft quality (straightness and diameter) matters more than the tip quality.
- Fire-hardening must be slow and controlled; the surface must turn deep brown, never black.
- Sinew is explicitly stated as superior for binding materials.
- For throwing spears, carrying multiples is advised because they are likely to miss.
