# Gill Net Making

## Objective

A gill net is a wall of mesh stretched across the water that catches fish by their gill covers as they try to swim through — the most productive passive fishing method you can build.

## Dependencies and prerequisites

Before starting to weave, two tools must be built: the Shuttle and the Mesh Gauge.

## Material

*   **Net Components:** Mesh, line.
*   **Tool Materials:** Hardwood, bone, or stiff plastic (for the shuttle). Reeds, cork, bark, sealed plastic bottles, tin cans, and carved dry wood blocks (for floats).

## System

**How Gill Nets Work**
A fish swims into the mesh head-first. The mesh opening is sized so the fish’s head passes through but its body cannot. When the fish tries to back out, its gill covers (opercula) flare open and catch in the mesh like barbs, trapping the fish.

**Mesh Size Criticality**
If the mesh is too large, fish swim straight through. If the mesh is too small, fish bounce off the mesh wall without entering.

**Rigging System**
A gill net must hang vertically in the water, with floats on top and weights on the bottom.

## Technology

**Mesh Measurement**
The mesh is measured as the stretched distance between two opposite knots.

**Knot Foundation**
The Sheet Bend Knot is the foundation of every gill net; if tied incorrectly, the mesh unravels.

**Buoyancy and Weighting**
Floats are attached every 50-80 cm (20-30 inches). Weights are attached every 50-80 cm, and the net must sink vertically but not drag the floats under.

## Tools

**The Shuttle**
*   **Function:** A flat, elongated tool that holds the working line, acting like a large, flat needle.
*   **Construction Steps:**
    1.  Find a flat piece of hardwood, bone, or stiff plastic, about 20 cm (8 inches) long, 2.5 cm (1 inch) wide, and 5-8 mm (1/4 inch) thick.
    2.  Carve a forked notch at each end (about 1 cm deep, slightly narrower than the body).
    3.  Load the shuttle by winding line in a figure-eight between the two notches. A loaded shuttle holds 15-25 meters of line depending on thickness.
    4.  When tying, pull line from the shuttle tip for smooth feeding and consistent tension.

**The Mesh Gauge**
*   **Function:** Sets every mesh opening to the same size.
*   **Construction Steps:**
    1.  Cut a flat piece of smooth wood as wide as half the desired mesh size (e.g., 2.5 cm wide for 5 cm mesh).
    2.  Sand or scrape the gauge smooth to prevent rough surfaces from snagging the line.

## Process

**Setting Up**
1.  Stretch a strong top line (headline) between two fixed points (trees, stakes, or rocks). The headline should be 5-10 meters (16-33 feet) long for a useful net.
2.  Tie the end of the shuttle line to the left end of the headline with a clove hitch.

**Casting On (First Row)**
1.  Hold the gauge flat against the headline.
2.  Bring the shuttle line down and around the bottom of the gauge, then back up and tie a sheet bend (netting knot) around the headline, positioned one gauge-width from the starting point.
3.  Slide the gauge out to make the first loop (one mesh opening).
4.  Repeat this process across the entire headline.

**Weaving Subsequent Rows**
1.  When the first row ends, turn around and work back the other direction.
2.  Hold the gauge against the bottom of the first row of loops.
3.  Pass the shuttle through the first loop from the previous row (front to back).
4.  Bring the line down around the gauge and back up through the loop, tying a sheet bend knot at the bottom of the loop.
5.  Continue across the row, connecting each new knot to one loop from the row above, creating a diamond mesh pattern.
6.  Continue adding rows until the desired depth is reached (typically 1-1.5 meters for river nets, up to 2-3 meters for lake or coastal use).

**Sheet Bend Knot Detail**
1.  Hold the loop from the previous row between thumb and forefinger.
2.  Pass the shuttle line up through the loop from behind.
3.  Wrap the shuttle line around the back of both sides of the loop (consistently left to right or right to left).
4.  Tuck the shuttle line under itself, under the strand that came up through the loop.
5.  Pull tight with steady, firm pressure to ensure the knot sits snugly at the bottom of the loop.

**Rigging: Floats and Weights**
1.  **Float Line (Top):** Thread a strong line through the top edge of the finished net or use the headline.
2.  **Lead Line (Bottom):** Thread a strong line through the bottom edge.
3.  Attach floats every 50-80 cm (20-30 inches).
4.  Attach weights every 50-80 cm. Test the net to ensure it hangs vertically with floats on the surface and weights near the bottom.

**Deployment**
1.  **Location Selection:** Best spots are across a stream narrows (perpendicular to current), along a lake shore (set at an angle), across a river channel between pools, or at the mouth of a tributary.
2.  **Anchoring:** Anchor one end to the shore (stake, tree, rock). Wade or paddle out, paying the net off keeping it straight.
3.  Anchor the far end. In a stream, both ends should be secured against the current.
4.  **Monitoring:** Check the net every 3-6 hours. In warm water (above 20C / 68F), check every 2-3 hours.
5.  **Harvesting:** Remove fish by working the mesh gently over the gill covers (the reverse of entry). Do not yank or cut the mesh.

**Maintenance and Repair**
*   **Drying:** Dry the net completely after every use; natural fiber nets rot within 3-5 days if stored wet. Spread over bushes in sunlight.
*   **Treatment:** Soak in bark tea (oak, willow, pine) for 4-8 hours to preserve fibers and reduce visibility. Re-treat every 2-3 weeks.
*   **Repair:** Patch holes immediately by re-tying new mesh into the gap using the same sheet bend knots, carrying the shuttle loaded with matching line.
*   **Inspection:** Re-tie any knots that feel loose due to UV light or abrasion.

**Production Timeline (for one person)**
| Task | Time |
| :--- | :--- | 
| Make cordage (50-80 meters) | 6-10 hours |
| Build shuttle and gauge | 30 minutes |
| Weave net (5m x 1.5m, 5cm mesh) | 8-12 hours |
| Rig floats and weights | 1-2 hours |
| **Total** | **16-25 hours** |

## Notes

*   **Key Takeaways:**
    *   A gill net is the highest-yield passive fishing tool.
    *   Building a shuttle and gauge first makes net-weaving dramatically faster and more consistent.
    *   The sheet bend knot is essential and must be tied correctly every time.
    *   Mesh size must be tailored to the target species.
    *   Religiously drying, treating, and repairing the net is crucial because it is a valuable asset.
