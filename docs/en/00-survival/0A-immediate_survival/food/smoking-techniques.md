# Smoking Techniques

## Objective
Smoking does more than add flavor; the chemical compounds in wood smoke actively kill bacteria and create a protective barrier on food surfaces, extending shelf life from days to months.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- Wood for smoke generation (Hardwood recommended).
- Salt for curing (multiplies shelf life).
- Equipment for methods (Simple rack over fire for hot smoking; dedicated smokehouse for cold smoking).

## Material
Meat (steaks or strips, fish fillets, butterfly whole fish).

## System
Smoking acts as a multi-layered defense, combining reduced moisture, antimicrobial surface chemistry, and a physical barrier (the “pellicle”).

## Technology
### Chemical Compounds in Smoke
Wood smoke contains over 200 chemical compounds. Key compounds for preservation include:
- **Phenols:** Antimicrobial compounds that penetrate the food surface and inhibit bacterial growth; the primary preservative agents.
- **Formaldehyde:** A powerful antimicrobial produced during incomplete combustion, cross-linking proteins on the food surface to create a tough outer layer.
- **Organic acids (acetic acid, formic acid):** Lower the surface pH of the food, making it less hospitable to bacteria.
- **Carbonyls:** Contribute to the characteristic brown color and provide additional antimicrobial action.

### Smoking Methods Comparison
| Factor | Hot Smoking | Cold Smoking |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Temperature at food | 55-80 C (130-175 F) | 15-30 C (60-85 F) |
| Duration | 4-8 hours | 12-48 hours (or longer) |
| Result | Cooked and smoked | Raw but preserved |
| Shelf life (no salt cure) | 1-2 weeks | 2-4 months |
| Shelf life (with salt cure) | 2-4 weeks | 4-12 months |
| Equipment needed | Simple rack over fire | Smokehouse with offset firebox |
| Difficulty | Easy | Moderate |

### Wood Selection Guide
| Wood | Smoke Character | Best For |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Oak | Medium, versatile | All meats, fish |
| Hickory | Strong, bold | Red meat, game |
| Apple / Cherry / Pear | Mild, sweet | Fish, poultry, light meats |
| Maple | Mild, slightly sweet | Fish, vegetables |
| Beech | Light, clean | Fish, delicate meats |
| Alder | Light, slightly sweet | Fish (traditional for salmon) |

> **Toxic Woods -- Never Use:** pine, spruce, fir, cedar, cypress, eucalyptus, or any conifer (produce toxic creosote and turpentine compounds). Also avoid yew, oleander, rhododendron, and any unidentified wood.

## Tools
- Hardwood chunks or chips for the coal bed.
- Green (fresh, recently cut) hardwood for smoking.
- Salt.
- Rack (metal grill, wire mesh, or green stick lattice).
- Pit for pit smoking.
- Tipi frame for tipi smoking.

## Process
### Hot Smoking: Step by Step
**Preparation**
1. Slice or portion the food (Meat: 1-2 cm thick steaks or strips; Fish: butterfly whole or cut into fillets). Leave skin on fish.
2. Salt cure if possible: Rub food with salt (3-5% of weight) for 1-2 hours to draw out moisture and firm the flesh. Rinse off excess salt.
3. Air-dry briefly (30-60 minutes) until the surface is slightly tacky (forming the pellicle).

**The Smoking Process**
1. **Build a hardwood coal bed:** Burn hardwood down to a thick bed of glowing coals (roughly 30-45 cm across).
2. **Add smoking wood:** Place chunks or chips of green hardwood on the coals. Dry wood catches fire and produces heat with minimal smoke.
3. **Place food on the rack:** Position the rack 45-70 cm above the coal bed, ensuring the temperature is hot enough that the hand cannot hold it for more than 2-3 seconds.
4. **Maintain smoke for 4-8 hours:** Add fresh green wood every 30-60 minutes. The food should be surrounded by a steady, visible haze of smoke.
5. **Monitor temperature:** If flames appear, reduce airflow by partially covering the fire with a flat stone or green branches. If the smoke dies down, blow gently on the coals or add smaller pieces of dry wood.
6. **Test for doneness:** Food should be firm to the touch, dark brown to mahogany colored on the surface, cooked through (interior fully cooked), and meat should flake or pull apart rather than feeling rubbery.

### Smoking Without a Dedicated Smokehouse (Improvised Methods)
**Pit Smoking**
1. Dig a pit 60 cm deep, 60 cm across.
2. Build a fire in the pit and burn it to coals.
3. Add green hardwood chunks to the coals.
4. Place a grill of green sticks across the pit opening.
5. Lay food on the grill.
6. Cover loosely with large leaves, bark slabs, or a damp cloth to trap smoke while allowing some airflow (do not seal completely).

**Barrel or Drum Method**
1. Cut ventilation holes near the bottom (4-6 holes, each 3-5 cm across).
2. Build a small fire inside on the bottom.
3. Place a rack (metal grill, wire mesh, or green stick lattice) halfway up.
4. Food goes on the rack.
5. Cover the top loosely.

**Tipi Smoker**
1. Build a tipi frame from 6-8 poles (200-250 cm tall), lashed at the top.
2. Cover the lower half with hides, bark, or thick leaf layers, leaving the top open.
3. Build a small fire inside at the center.
4. Hang food from cross-sticks near the top where smoke concentrates.
5. The open top creates a natural draft.

### Combining Smoking with Other Preservation
- **Salt + Smoke:** The most effective field combination. Salt draws moisture, and smoke adds antimicrobial compounds and a protective surface layer.
- **Smoke + Full Drying:** After hot smoking, continue drying the food until it passes the moisture testing snap test.
- **Smoke + Fat Sealing:** After smoking, coat or submerge the food in rendered animal fat to exclude air and further prevent oxidation and bacterial access.

## Notes
- Smoking is fundamentally a chemical process, not just a flavoring technique.
- The pellicle formation (tacky surface layer) by air-drying salted food is crucial for effective smoke adherence.
- Hardwood selection is mandatory, with conifers being explicitly toxic.
- Hot smoking is the practical survival choice, while cold smoking requires a dedicated setup.
- The shelf life potential is maximized when combining smoking with salt curing and subsequent drying or fat sealing.
