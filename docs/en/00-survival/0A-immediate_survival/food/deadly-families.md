# Deadly Families: Hemlock, Nightshade, and Mushrooms

## Objective
To teach readers how to recognize the three plant groups responsible for the vast majority of fatal wild-food poisonings and how to master them to eliminate roughly 80% of lethal foraging mistakes.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- Expert-level identification skills for mushrooms and plants.
- Ability to perform field sketching.
- Knowledge of the Universal Edibility Test protocol.

## Material
### Group 1: Hemlock and the Carrot Family (Apiaceae)
*   **Family Signature:** Umbrella-shaped flower cluster called an umbel.
*   **Key Species:
    *   **Poison Hemlock (*Conium maculatum*):** Grows on roadsides, ditches, and disturbed ground, reaching 6-10 feet tall.
    *   **Water Hemlock (*Cicuta* species):** Grows in wet areas (stream banks, marshes, wet meadows).
    *   **Giant Hogweed (*Heracleum mantegazzianum*):** Known for phototoxic sap.

### Group 2: Nightshade Family (Solanaceae)
*   **Key Species:
    *   **Deadly Nightshade (*Atropa belladonna*):
        *   Identification:
            *   Bushy plant, 2-5 feet tall.
            *   Bell-shaped flowers, dull purple with greenish tinge.
            *   Berries are shiny, black, cherry-sized, in clusters.
            *   Leaves are large, oval, pointed, arranged alternately.
            *   Entire plant has a faint, unpleasant odor.
        *   Toxin:
            *   Tropane alkaloids (atropine and scopolamine).
        *   Symptoms:
            *   Dilated pupils, blurred vision.
            *   Rapid heartbeat.
            *   Dry mouth and skin.
            *   Flushed, hot skin.
            *   Hallucinations, agitation, delirium.
            *   Seizures and coma in severe cases.
        *   Lethal Dose:
            *   10-20 berries for an adult, 3-5 for a child.
    *   **Jimsonweed / Thorn Apple (*Datura stramonium*):
        *   Identification:
            *   Grows 2-5 feet tall, bushy.
            *   Large white or purple trumpet-shaped flowers (3-4 inches long).
            *   Spiny, golf-ball-sized seed pods (distinctive and diagnostic).
            *   Leaves are coarsely toothed, large, and foul-smelling when crushed.
        *   Toxin:
            *   Same tropane alkaloids as deadly nightshade; concentration varies wildly.

### Group 3: Toxic Mushrooms
*   **The Amatoxin Group (Destroys the liver):**
    *   **Death cap (*Amanita phalloides*):** Responsible for over 90% of fatal poisonings worldwide.
    *   **Destroying angel (*Amanita virosa*, *A. bisporigera*, *A. ocreata*):** All-white, elegant, and deadly.
    *   **Autumn skullcap (*Galerina marginata*):** Small brown mushroom growing on decaying wood.

### Mushroom Differentiation
*   **Mimics Safe Species:
    *   **Death cap vs. Paddy straw mushroom/common field mushroom:** Death cap has a sack-like volva at the base; dig up the entire mushroom.
    *   **Destroying angel vs. Button mushroom/horse mushroom:** Destroying angel has a volva; button mushrooms do not.
    *   **False morel vs. True morel:** True morels are completely hollow; false morels have cottony or chambered interiors.
    *   **Jack o’lantern vs. Chanterelle:** Jack o’lanterns have true blade-like gills; chanterelles have blunt ridges.

## System
*   **Classification:** Based on plant groups (Carrot family, Nightshade family, Fungi/Mushrooms).
*   **Risk Assessment:** Categorization of danger levels (EXTREME, HIGH, Moderate, Low).

## Technology
*   **Identification Tools:** Visual inspection for key features (stem surface, color, smell, root structure, flower shape, gill structure).
*   **Toxin Analysis:** Understanding the mechanism of action (e.g., piperidine alkaloids causing ascending paralysis, tropane alkaloids blocking acetylcholine receptors).

## Tools
*   **Field Sketching:** A tool for recording essential details of dangerous species (overall shape, leaf arrangement, stem features, flower/fruit structure, smell, sap color, location).
*   **Universal Edibility Test:** A protocol to follow when classification is uncertain.

## Process
### Cross-Family Identification Protocol
1.  **Carrot family?** Look for umbrella flower clusters, compound leaves, hollow stems. Check for purple stem blotches, musty smell, smooth stems — any of these means AVOID.
2.  **Nightshade family?** Look for star or bell-shaped flowers, berries in clusters, alternate leaves. If the berries are black or the plant smells foul, AVOID.
3.  **Mushroom?** If it has gills, a ring on the stem, or a cup at the base, AVOID unless expert skills are present.
4.  **Cannot classify?** Follow the Universal Edibility Test protocol.

### Field Sketching for Group Safety Process
1.  Draw the overall shape and approximate size.
2.  Detail the leaf arrangement and shape.
3.  Note stem features (color, texture, hollow vs. solid).
4.  Draw the flower or fruit structure.
5.  Record the smell and any sap color.
6.  Mark the location where found.
7.  Post these sketches where the group can study them.

## Notes
- The amatoxin mushroom poisoning has a deceptive “recovery” phase; feeling better after initial symptoms does not mean the danger has passed.
- The most critical identification feature for deadly Amanitas is the volva (cup at the base), which is usually buried underground and must be dug up entirely.
- For Nightshade family berries, the rule is strict: black berries are almost always dangerous, and red berries are usually dangerous. Only fully ripe fruits of positively identified species are safe.
- In general, when in doubt about edibility, the recommended action is to go hungry because starvation takes weeks to kill, whereas these toxins can kill in hours to days.
