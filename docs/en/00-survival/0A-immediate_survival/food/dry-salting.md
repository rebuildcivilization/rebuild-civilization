# Dry Salting

## Objective
Dry salting is the most straightforward preservation method that works at scale. The objective is to use salt to preserve meat or fish for months to years.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Patience and correct ratios.
*   Temperatures below 50°F (10°C) during curing.
*   A cool, dry, ventilated space for after curing.
*   Cold, clean water for desalting before eating.

## Material
Meat or fish.

## System
**The Principle (Osmosis):** When salt contacts wet flesh, osmosis occurs. Water molecules migrate from inside the meat cells (low salt concentration) to the surface (high salt concentration). Simultaneously, salt diffuses inward, causing the meat to lose moisture (typically 20-35% of its original weight) while the internal salt concentration rises to levels where bacteria, mold, and enzymes cannot function. This creates a self-reinforcing, dense, antibacterial outer layer (pellicle) that acts as armor against contamination.

## Technology
Dry Salting: A preservation technique requiring no fire, no special equipment, and no skill beyond patience and correct ratios.

## Tools
*   Knife or skewer (for the Bone Smell Test).
*   Flat board, plate, or flat stone (for weight).
*   Container (for layering salt).
*   Measurement tools (implied for ratios).

## Process
### Preparation
1. **Trim aggressively:** Remove all visible fat, sinew, and connective tissue from the surfaces. Interior marbling is acceptable.
2. **Bleed the meat thoroughly:** Press and squeeze to expel residual blood.
3. **Score thick cuts:** For cuts over 2 inches (5 cm) thick, cut deep slashes every 1-2 inches, down to but not through the bone to increase salt contact area and penetration speed.
4. **Weigh the meat:** Calculate salt amount based on the ratio table.

### Salting
5. **Rub salt into every surface:** Push salt into cuts, crevices, joints, and scored slashes. The meat should be white with a salt coating.
6. **Pack the bone ends:** Pack salt generously around and into the bone, as the marrow cavity is a prime spoilage point.
7. **Layer in a container:** Spread a 1/2-inch (1 cm) bed of salt on the bottom, lay the first piece of meat flesh-side down, cover with a 1/4-inch (6 mm) layer of salt, repeat, and finish with a thick salt cap (at least 1 inch / 2.5 cm) on top.

### Curing
8. **Weight it down:** Place a flat board, plate, or flat stone on top and load with 15-20 pounds (7-9 kg) of weight to accelerate moisture expulsion.
9. **Tilt for drainage:** Position the container at a slight angle and tip daily to pour off accumulated brine; punch a small hole at the low point.
10. **Re-salt on day 3-4:** Remove the weight, add fresh salt to any exposed surfaces, repack, and re-weight.
11. **Check progress:** Press the thickest part of the meat. Properly cured meat feels uniformly firm throughout.

### Desalting Before Eating
1. Slice the cured meat into serving portions.
2. Soak in a large volume of cold, clean water for 12-24 hours.
3. Change the water every 4-6 hours.
4. Taste a small piece to ensure it is pleasantly savory, not aggressively salty.

### After Curing
1. **Brush or rinse off excess surface salt** under clean water and pat dry.
2. **Hang in a cool, dry, ventilated space** (ideally 35-50°F / 2-10°C) with good airflow.
3. **Do not wrap tightly:** Air circulation prevents surface mold. White mold is harmless; green or black mold indicates too much moisture, requiring improved ventilation.
4. **For maximum shelf life:** Follow dry salting with cold smoking.

### Adding Flavor
Flavor additives can be mixed into the cure:
*   **Sugar** (brown or white): 1 part sugar to 4 parts salt (reduces harshness, encourages lactobacillus).
*   **Black pepper:** Coarsely ground, mixed into the salt rub (mild antimicrobial).
*   **Dried herbs** (thyme, rosemary, sage, bay leaf): Crush and mix into salt (aromatic, mildly antimicrobial).
*   **Juniper berries:** Crushed, 1 tablespoon per pound of salt.
*   **Garlic:** Minced or powdered, 1 teaspoon per pound of salt.

### The Bone Smell Test
For large bone-in cuts, after the calculated cure time:
1. Insert a thin, clean knife or skewer deep alongside the bone.
2. Pull it out and immediately smell the tip.
3. **Clean, salty, meaty smell** = properly cured.
4. **Sour, sulfurous, or putrid smell** = internal spoilage; discard the piece.

## Salt Ratios
| Application | Salt Ratio | Practical Measure | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Light cure (eat within 1-2 weeks) | 3-5% of meat weight | 1 tablespoon per pound | Subtle flavor, short shelf life |
| Standard cure (1-3 months) | 8-10% of meat weight | 2-3 tablespoons per pound | Good balance of flavor and preservation |
| Heavy cure (3-12+ months) | 15-20% of meat weight | 1 lb salt per 5 lbs meat | Very salty — requires desalting before eating |
| Maximum preservation (indefinite) | 25-33% of meat weight | 1 lb salt per 3-4 lbs meat | Used historically for naval stores and army rations |

## Cure Times
| Cut | Typical Thickness | Cure Time | Check Point |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Jerky strips | 1/4 inch (6 mm) | 12-24 hours | Firm, leathery texture |
| Fish fillets | 1/2-1 inch (1-2.5 cm) | 24-48 hours | Flesh translucent and firm |
| Pork chops / steaks | 1-1.5 inches (2.5-4 cm) | 3-5 days | No soft center when pressed |
| Pork belly (bacon) | 1.5-2 inches (4-5 cm) | 7-10 days | Firm throughout, darker color |
| Small whole fish | Varies | 3-5 days | Eyes sunken, body rigid |
| Large whole fish | Varies | 5-7 days | Stiff as a board |
| Bone-in ham | 6-8 inches (15-20 cm) | 2 days per pound | No give at bone joint |
| Whole shoulder/leg | 8-12 inches (20-30 cm) | 2.5-3 days per pound | Smell test at bone |

**Rule of Thumb:** 2 days of cure time per pound of meat for thick cuts. A 10-pound bone-in ham needs approximately 20 days.

## Key Takeaways
*   Use a minimum of 1 pound of salt per 5 pounds of meat for long-term preservation (more is safer than less).
*   Trim all surface fat and score thick cuts deeply before salting to ensure penetration.
*   Cure for 2 days per pound of meat on thick cuts, at temperatures below 50°F (10°C).
*   Drain liquid daily and re-salt on day 3-4 as the original coating dissolves.
*   Always smell-test at the bone on large cuts before trusting the cure; internal spoilage is invisible from the outside.

## Notes
- The danger zone for dry salting is temperatures above 50°F (10°C), where bacteria multiply faster than salt diffuses through thick cuts.
- Over-salted food tastes bad but is safe; under-salted food can harbor *Clostridium botulinum*.
- The process relies on salt penetrating to the center of the meat before bacteria reach it.
- The combination of dry salting and cold smoking produces meat that can last 6-18 months without refrigeration.
- For maximum preservation, the process should be followed by cold smoking.
