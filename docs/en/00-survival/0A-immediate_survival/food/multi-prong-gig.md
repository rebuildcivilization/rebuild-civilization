# Multi-Prong Gig

## Objective
To create the most effective hand-spearing tool from a single piece of wood, designed for downward thrusts into shallow water.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   **Shaft Selection:** Choose a hardwood sapling 2-3 meters long with a diameter of 4-5 cm at the working end.
*   **Shaft Quality:** The last 25-30 cm of the shaft must be free of knots, side branches, or grain irregularities.
*   **Preparation:** The shaft must be stripped of bark, and partially dried (24-48 hours) for more predictable splitting.
*   **Binding Material:** Wet rawhide is ideal (shrinks 10-15% as it dries). Alternatives include wet sinew, soaked bark strips (elm or basswood), or twisted plant fiber cordage.

## Material
*   Hardwood sapling (2-3 meters long).
*   Wet rawhide (or alternatives like wet sinew, soaked bark strips, or twisted plant fiber cordage) for binding.
*   Pine resin (for weekly re-application to bindings).

## System
The gig is a multi-pronged spearing tool designed for downward thrusts into shallow water, intended to stay in the user's hands.

## Technology
*   **Prong Design:** Split prongs with carved barbs.
*   **Prong Geometry:** Barbs are positioned 3-5 cm from the tip, angled at 25-35 degrees from the prong axis, and should be no more than one-third of the prong’s diameter into the prong.
*   **Split Control:** The ability to steer a crooked split by gently bending the thicker side away from the split.
*   **Barb Geometry:** Each prong receives one or two barbs on opposite sides (Two-Barb Prongs create a double catch).

## Tools
*   Knife tip or sharp flake (for scoring the end grain).
*   Baton (a short heavy stick) (for starting the first split).
*   Thin stick or bone sliver (to insert a wedge).
*   Carved wooden plug (3-4 cm long, tapered at both ends) (to insert for the center prong spread).
*   Rough stone (for re-sharpening prong tips).
*   Hot coals (for fire-hardening barbs).

## Process
### Shaft Preparation
1. Choose the hardwood sapling.
2. Strip bark from the entire shaft.
3. Shave down any bumps.
4. Partially dry the shaft (24-48 hours if possible).

### Splitting the Prongs
*   **Three-Prong Pattern (Most Reliable):**
    1. Score the end grain to mark split locations.
    2. Start the first split (20-25 cm deep).
    3. Insert a wedge near the base.
    4. Start the second split 60 degrees from the first (same depth).
    5. Insert the spreader (carved wooden plug) into the center.
*   **Four-Prong Pattern:** Two perpendicular splits, each 20-25 cm deep. Insert a cross-shaped spreader (two notched sticks interlocked).
*   **Five-Prong Pattern (Requires thicker shafts $\ge$ 5 cm):** Use a star pattern (two splits plus a third at 36 degrees from one of them). The center prong stays as-is.
*   **Split Control:** If a split runs crooked, bend the thicker side gently away from the split to steer it back.

### Binding Below the Split
1. Position the wrap 2-3 cm below where the split ends.
2. Wrap tightly in a single layer, overlapping each turn by half the width of the wrapping material.
3. Cover at least 4-5 cm of shaft.
4. Finish by tucking the end under the last two wraps and pulling tight.
5. Allow rawhide/sinew to dry completely before use.
6. **Test the binding:** Grip below the wrap and push each prong outward; add more wraps if movement occurs.

### Carving the Barbs
1. Mark the barb location 3-5 cm from the prong tip.
2. Cut a stop notch into the prong at the barb’s base position.
3. Carve toward the stop notch, angling the blade down into the prong.
4. Remove a thin wedge of wood.
5. Clean the barb (the remaining raised lip is the barb); thin it slightly if too thick.
6. Fire-harden by holding near hot coals for 1-2 minutes, rotating.
*   **Two-Barb Prongs:** Carve a second barb on the opposite side, staggered 1-2 cm above or below the first.

### Shaping and Finishing
1. Taper each prong to a sharp point over the last 5-8 cm.
2. Fire-harden all tips (light brown, not charred).
3. Smooth the inner faces of each prong to reduce drag.
4. Check alignment: All prong tips should be roughly in the same plane and spread evenly.

### Using the Gig
*   **Grip:** Dominant hand at the balance point (usually one-third from the butt end); guide hand forward.
*   **Stance:** Lean forward over the water, gig tip 15-30 cm above the surface, angled roughly 45 degrees.
*   **Strike:** A fast downward thrust, not a throw. Push down harder upon contact to seat the barbs.
*   **Recovery:** Pin the fish against the bottom, slide the guide hand down the shaft, and lift with the fish trapped between prongs and substrate.
*   **Prong Breakage Rule:** Never use the gig to pry rocks or lever against hard surfaces; push the gig deeper to free it.

### Maintenance
*   Rinse after every use.
*   Re-sharpen prong tips by scraping with a rough stone.
*   Re-carve any cracked barbs immediately.
*   Re-apply pine resin to bindings weekly if used daily.
*   Replace the spreader if prongs begin to close together.

## Notes
*   The most reliable configuration is the Three-Prong Pattern for general fishing.
*   The effectiveness of the gig relies on the barbs seating when the gig is struck downward and the recovery technique used.
*   Barbs must be fire-hardened to maximize holding power.
*   The primary failure point without proper binding is the split continuing down the shaft.
*   The spread width corresponds to prong count: 3 prongs (10-12 cm), 4 prongs (12-15 cm), 5 prongs (15-18 cm).
