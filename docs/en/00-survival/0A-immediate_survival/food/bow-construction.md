# Bow Construction

## Objective
To build a functional bow that stores energy efficiently (doesn’t take excessive force to draw), releases energy quickly (limbs snap back fast), and survives thousands of cycles without breaking. A functional bow extends the effective hunting range from 10 meters to 30+ meters and allows the user to take game silently.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Understanding of stave selection, tillering, and string making.
*   Harvested and dried stave (see Stave Selection for details).
*   Basic skill in shaping wood and string preparation.

## Material
### Stave Materials
*   Ash or Elm (recommended for beginners, described as the most forgiving beginner woods).
*   Other potential woods include Yew, Osage orange, Hickory, Maple, and Black locust.

### String Materials
*   Sinew (animal tendon): Excellent strength, low stretch, high durability. Best natural option, requiring twisting or braiding from leg tendons.
*   Plant fiber (dogbane, nettle, milkweed): Good strength, low stretch, moderate durability. Must be processed into fine cordage.
*   Linen (flax fiber): Excellent strength, very low stretch, high durability. Superb if available.
*   Rawhide: Good strength, moderate stretch, high durability. Requires cutting thin strips and twisting tightly.
*   Inner bark (basswood, elm): Fair strength, moderate stretch, low durability. Emergency use only.

## System
A bow functions as a spring. Energy is stored in the bent limbs when the string is drawn, and this energy transfers to the arrow upon release. The system must be designed for efficient energy storage and rapid release to ensure durability.

## Technology
### Critical Rules of Construction
1.  **Back Integrity:** The back of the bow (facing the target) MUST follow a single continuous growth ring. This rule prevents most bow failures by eliminating stress risers.
2.  **Tillering Goal:** To remove wood from stiff spots only, ensuring both limbs bend in a smooth, even curve through their entire length, with no hinges or flat spots.
3.  **Draw Limits:** Never draw past the target draw length; over-pulling weakens the wood.
4.  **String Length:** The finished bow string should be 10–15 cm shorter than the bow’s unstrung tip-to-tip length to create proper brace height.

## Tools
*   Hatchet, knife, or sharp stone edge (for removing excess wood/shavings).
*   Tillering stick (roughly 70-80 cm long, 5 cm wide, 2.5 cm thick, with a notch for the handle).
*   Scraper (sharp stone edge works well).
*   Wax (beeswax or rendered fat) for string treatment.
*   Charcoal or pencil for marking stiff spots.

## Process
### Rough Shaping the Stave
1.  **Identify the Back:** Ensure the back follows a single continuous growth ring; leave it alone or carefully chase a single ring with a knife.
2.  **Handle Marking:** Find the center and mark the handle section (10-12 cm centered on the midpoint). This section must stay thick and rigid.
3.  **Tip Marking:** Mark the limb tips, leaving 3-4 cm at each end for string nocks.
4.  **Taper Width:** Taper the width from the handle to the tips:
    *   Handle: 4-5 cm wide
    *   Mid-limb: 3.5-4 cm wide
    *   Near Tips: 2-2.5 cm wide
5.  **Initial Thickness:** Start the belly roughly 2.5-3 cm thick at the handle, tapering to 1.5-2 cm at the tips. Leave extra wood and tillering should refine this.

### Tillering: The Art of the Bow
1.  **Build the Tillering Stick:** Cut the board, cut a notch for the handle, cut notches every 5 cm down one side to hold the string at increasing draw lengths, and mount it at eye level.
2.  **String the Bow Loosely:** Use a temporary tillering string of strong cordage, ensuring about 15 cm of brace height (handle to string).
3.  **Test and Mark:** Place the bow on the stick and pull to the first notch. Mark any stiff spots with charcoal or pencil.
4.  **Remove Wood:** Remove wood from stiff spots only using a scraper, knife, or rasp, taking off thin shavings (think sanding, not chopping).
5.  **Re-check:** Pull to the same notch. If the stiff spot has started bending, move to the next stiff area. If not, remove a bit more.
6.  **Gradual Increase:** Move to the next notch and repeat the process, re-examining the bend profile at each draw length.
7.  **Full Draw:** Continue until the full draw is reached (typically 70-72 cm for an adult male).

### Reading the Bend
*   **Near handle (inner limb):** Most bend. Often too stiff; remove belly wood here first.
*   **Mid-limb:** Moderate bend. Should flow smoothly from inner to outer.
*   **Outer limb / tips:** Least bend. If bending too much, the limb is too thin; leave tips slightly stiff.

### String Making
1.  **Prepare Fibers:** Split sinew bundles thin and dry, or rete the plant fibers and separate them.
2.  **Reverse-twist (Ply):** Reverse-twist two bundles together into a 2-ply cord. The finished string should be about 4-5 mm diameter.
3.  **Length Adjustment:** Make the string 10-15 cm shorter than the bow’s length (tip to tip, unstrung) to create proper brace height.
4.  **Form Loops:** Form loops at each end by splicing the cord or tying secure non-slip loops (timber hitch for temporary use).
5.  **Waxing:** Wax the string with beeswax or rendered fat to reduce moisture absorption and friction wear.

### Cutting the Nocks
1.  **Location:** Cut nocks on the sides of the limb tips, not the belly or back.
2.  **Angle:** Angle the nocks slightly toward the belly (about 30 degrees from perpendicular).
3.  **Depth:** 3-5 mm, just enough to hold the string securely.
4.  **Refinement:** Smooth the edges to prevent cutting the string. Reinforce with a wrap of sinew or cord if the wood is soft.

### Finishing and Protection
*   **Smoothing:** Sand or scrape the belly smooth to eliminate splinters and indicate grain violations.
*   **Sealing:** Apply oil or rendered fat to the entire bow to seal the wood against moisture changes that cause warping.
*   **Handle Protection:** Wrap the handle with leather, cord, or bark for a comfortable, non-slip grip.
*   **Storage:** Build a bow sock from leather or heavy cloth to protect the bow during transport and storage.

### Testing Your Bow
1.  **Initial Check:** Start with short draws even after tillering to confirm nothing creaks or pops.
2.  **Initial Test:** Shoot blunt arrows into a dirt bank at close range first.
3.  **String Follow Check:** After unstringing, check if the bow stays bent toward the belly. Normal follow is 2-3 cm; excessive follow (5+ cm) indicates too far drawing or wood fatigue.
4.  **Inspection:** Inspect for cracks after every session for the first week, running fingers along the back to feel for lifted grain or splinters.

## Notes
*   The single most critical rule to prevent failure is never cutting through the growth rings on the back (the side facing the target).
*   Patience during tillering is non-negotiable; the rule is: remove wood from stiff spots only, and never add wood back.
*   The lower limb is usually slightly stiffer than the upper limb, an intentional feature due to the bow hand position.
