# Spear Construction

## Objective
To build a fishing spear.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
*   Availability of suitable raw materials (wood, bone, antler, shell, stone).
*   Access to fire and hot coals for fire-hardening the wood tips.
*   Water for maintenance (rinsing).

## Material
*   **Shaft Wood:** Straight, green wood.
*   **Point Materials (Optional):** Bone, antler tine, flaked stone (obsidian or flint), large mussel or oyster shells, metal scrap.
*   **Binding Material:** Wet cordage, sinew, rawhide strips, plant cordage.
*   **Sealing Material:** Pine resin mixed with charcoal powder.

## System
The system involves selecting a shaft, carving a working end, hardening the tip, and designing the working end to suit the target species and water conditions.

## Technology
*   **Fire-Hardening:** Rotating the working tip above coals for 3–5 minutes until the surface darkens to light brown (never black).
*   **Shaft Selection:** Choosing wood properties (straightness, strength, flexibility) based on intended use.
*   **Design Types:** Single-point, Multi-prong (Split-Shaft), Lashed-Point.

## Tools
*   Knife or sharp stone flake (for carving the working end).
*   Cordage (wet for binding).
*   Rough stone (for re-sharpening points).

## Process
### Shaft Selection
*   **Ideal Dimensions:** Length of 2–2.5 meters (6–8 feet) for wading; 3–4 meters (10–13 feet) for bank spearing.
*   **Ideal Diameter:** 2.5–3.5 cm (1–1.5 inches) at the grip, tapering toward the tip.
*   **Selection Criteria:** Wood must be straight, strong, and long enough to reach fish from a standing position.
*   **Green Wood Preference:** Cut green wood rather than dead wood, as green wood flexes instead of snapping.

### Single-Point Spear Construction
1.  **Select a straight shaft** 2-2.5 meters long.
2.  **Carve the tip** using a knife or sharp stone flake, whittling the last 15-20 cm into a gradual taper ending in a sharp point.
3.  **Harden the tip:** Hold 10–15 cm above hot coals, rotate slowly for 3–5 minutes until the surface darkens slightly and becomes glossy.
4.  **Add a barb (optional):** Cut a notch 3–4 cm from the tip, angled backward (roughly 30 degrees), about 5 mm deep, to prevent fish from sliding off.

### Multi-Prong Spear (Split-Shaft Design)
1.  **Start with a shaft** at least 3 cm in diameter at the working end.
2.  **Split the tip** into prongs:
    *   **Two-prong fork:** One split down the center, 15–20 cm deep.
    *   **Three-prong trident:** Two perpendicular splits forming a cross pattern, then remove one quarter section.
    *   **Four-prong:** Two perpendicular splits, keeping all four sections.
3.  **Spread the prongs:** Wedge a small piece of wood or stone into the center of the split to force prongs apart.
4.  **Bind the split:** Wrap cordage in tight, overlapping turns for at least 3–4 cm below the split ends; wet the cordage before wrapping.
5.  **Shape each prong tip:** Carve to a point and fire-harden individually.
6.  **Add barbs:** Notch each prong 3–4 cm from its tip.

### Lashed-Point Spear Construction
1.  **Prepare the shaft:** Split the tip 5–8 cm or carve a notch.
2.  **Seat the point:** Insert the base of the point material into the split or notch.
3.  **Bind:** Wrap with wet sinew, rawhide strips, or strong plant cordage in a figure-eight pattern around the shaft and over the point base.
4.  **Seal:** Apply pine resin mixed with charcoal powder (about 3:1 ratio) over the binding and heat gently to melt and seal.

### Maintenance and Care
*   **After use:** Rinse in fresh water.
*   **Re-sharpen points:** Daily by scraping with a rough stone.
*   **Check bindings:** Before each session, as wet-dry cycles loosen cordage.
*   **Storage:** Store vertically or hanging; laying a spear on damp ground warps the shaft.
*   **Tip Replacement:** Replace fire-hardened tips when they chip or crack—re-carve and re-harden.

### Choosing Your Design
| Situation | Best Design | Rationale |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Large slow fish (carp, catfish) | Single point, lashed bone/stone | Penetration matters more than coverage |
| Small fast fish in shallows | Multi-prong (3–4 prongs) | Coverage compensates for speed |
| Night spearing with torch | Multi-prong | Fish hold still in light; wide prongs forgive aim errors |
| Deep water from bank | Long shaft, single heavy point | Need reach and momentum |
| Rocky streams | Multi-prong with barbs | Fish dart between rocks; barbs prevent escape |

### Wood Species Comparison
| Wood | Properties | Availability |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Ash | Straight grain, flexible, shock-resistant | Temperate forests |
| Hazel | Naturally straight shoots, lightweight | Hedgerows, woodland edges |
| Willow | Very straight, easy to work, but soft | Riverbanks (convenient) |
| Bamboo | Hollow, light, extremely strong | Subtropical/tropical |
| Pine (young) | Straight saplings, resinous (resists rot) | Conifer forests |

## Notes
*   **Fire Hardening Criticality:** Wood must be fire-hardened to prevent brittleness; charring wood black results in brittleness.
*   **Binding Technique:** Wet cordage shrinks as it dries, creating a tighter bind when wrapped.
*   **Durability:** Bone, antler, or stone points outlast wood points many times over when available.
*   **Maintenance Cycle:** Maintenance requires daily re-sharpening of points and checking bindings due to wet-dry cycles.
