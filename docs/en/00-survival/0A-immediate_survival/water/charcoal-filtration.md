# Charcoal Filtration

## Objective
To remove chemical contaminants, bad taste, and turbidity from water.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
Always follow charcoal filtration with boiling or another disinfection method, as charcoal does not kill bacteria or viruses.

## Material
- Container with a narrow opening (plastic bottle with bottom cut off, hollow log section, birch bark cone, clay pot with a hole in the bottom).
- Charcoal from a hardwood fire, crushed to pieces ranging from pea-sized to coarse powder.
- Fine sand (NOT beach sand with salt — use river sand or crushed rock fines).
- Coarse gravel or pebbles.
- Cloth, grass, or moss for plugging.

## System
**Charcoal Filtration:** A system utilizing the adsorptive properties of charcoal to remove chemical contaminants, improve taste, and reduce turbidity from water.

**Multi-Stage Filtering:** A three-stage approach mimicking modern municipal water treatment: sediment removal (pre-filter), carbon filtration, then disinfection (boil or UV-treat).

## Technology
**Adsorption and Pyrolysis:**
- **Pyrolysis:** Heating organic material in the absence of oxygen transforms it into a porous carbon structure riddled with microscopic tunnels and cavities, creating an enormous surface area.
- **Adsorption:** Contaminants in water stick to the charcoal surface through adsorption, bonding to the carbon rather than being absorbed into it.

## Tools
- Rocks for crushing charcoal.
- Measuring tools (implied for flow rate/layer thickness).
- Container for the filter.

## Process
### Building the Charcoal Filter
1. **Prepare the container:** Cut off the bottom of a bottle or ensure the bottom opening of a log is small enough to restrict flow. Poke a small drainage hole in the cap or stuff the neck loosely with clean grass or cloth.
2. **Crush the charcoal:** Grind hardwood charcoal chunks down to a mix of pea-sized pieces and coarser powder.
3. **Layer the filter (bottom to top):**
    - Cloth or grass plug at the neck.
    - Fine charcoal layer: 8–10 cm (3–4 inches) thick.
    - Fine sand layer: 5–8 cm (2–3 inches).
    - Coarse sand or fine gravel layer: 5–8 cm (2–3 inches).
    - Large gravel or pebbles on top: 5 cm (2 inches) to diffuse incoming water.
4. **Prime the filter:** Pour clean water through the entire filter twice. Discard the first runoff (black with charcoal dust). Continue priming until the output runs relatively clear.
5. **Begin filtering:** Pour water slowly into the top of the filter to give the charcoal maximum contact time.

### Optimizing Filter Performance
- **Contact Time Matters:** Longer contact time means more contaminant adsorption. Control flow rate by packing the charcoal layer tighter, using finer charcoal particles, making the layer thicker, or running a second charcoal filter in series.
- **Multi-Stage Filtering Sequence:**
    1. First pass: coarse pre-filter (gravel and sand only) to remove visible sediment.
    2. Second pass: charcoal filter for chemical contaminants and taste.
    3. Final step: boil or UV-treat the filtered water to kill pathogens.

### Maintenance and Replacement
- **Signs of Need for Replacement:**
    - Filtered water starts tasting or smelling off again.
    - Flow rate slows dramatically (pores clogged with sediment).
    - More than roughly 50–80 liters through a bottle-sized filter.
    - Charcoal has been in use for more than 1–2 weeks.
- **Replacement Procedure:**
    1. Disassemble the filter completely.
    2. Discard the old charcoal (cannot be effectively regenerated without a kiln).
    3. Rinse the sand and gravel layers vigorously with clean water.
    4. Rebuild with fresh charcoal.
    5. Prime the rebuilt filter before use.

### Scaling Up for a Group
- **Barrel-Scale Filter:** Use a 20–50 liter container (salvaged drum, large clay pot, wooden barrel).
- **Layer Thickness:** Increase all layer thicknesses proportionally; charcoal should be at least 20 cm (8 inches).
- **Flow Control:** Install a spigot or tap near the bottom for controlled flow.
- **Capacity:** A barrel filter can process 50–100 liters per day and serve 10–20 people.
- **Schedule:** Assign someone to monitor output quality and replace charcoal on a weekly schedule.

### Charcoal Quality Comparison
| Charcoal Source | Filtration Quality | Availability | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hardwood (oak, maple, hickory) | Excellent | Common in temperate forests | Dense wood makes dense, high-surface-area charcoal |
| Softwood (pine, spruce, fir) | Fair | Common in northern/mountain regions | Less porous, resin residue may affect taste |
| Bamboo | Excellent | Tropical and subtropical regions | Extremely high surface area when properly carbonized |
| Coconut shell | Excellent | Coastal tropical areas | One of the best natural carbon sources, very hard and porous |
| Activated charcoal | Superior | Requires extra processing | Chemically or steam-treated to massively increase pore structure |

### What Charcoal Removes vs. What It Misses
| Contaminant | Removed by Charcoal? | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sediment and turbidity | Yes | Physically trapped in pore structure |
| Organic chemicals | Yes | Pesticides, herbicides, many industrial chemicals |
| Chlorine and chloramines | Yes | Excellent removal through chemical reaction |
| Bad taste and odor | Yes | Primary reason charcoal improves water palatability |
| Dissolved metals (lead, mercury) | Partially | Activated charcoal better than regular; not reliable for all metals |
| Bacteria | No | Too small for most charcoal pore structures |
| Viruses | No | Far too small to be physically trapped |
| Parasites (giardia, crypto) | Partially | Cysts may be trapped by fine-pore activated charcoal, but do not rely on this |
| Dissolved salts | No | Charcoal cannot desalinate water |

## Notes
- **Critical Safety Warning:** Charcoal filtration provides a false sense of security; always disinfect after filtering because charcoal does not reliably kill pathogens.
- **Charcoal Quality:** Use only natural hardwood charcoal; avoid commercial briquettes due to binders and additives.
- **Flow Rate Guideline:** Aim for roughly one drop per second for a bottle-sized filter; a steady stream indicates the charcoal is packed too loosely and contact time is too short.
- **Surface Area:** A single gram of well-made charcoal can have a surface area exceeding 500 square meters.
- **Maintenance:** The primary signs of failure are the return of bad taste/smell or a dramatic slowdown in flow rate.
