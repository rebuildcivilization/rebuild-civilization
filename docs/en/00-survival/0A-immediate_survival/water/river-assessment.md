# River Assessment: Evaluating Flowing Water | Survival Wiki

## Objective
To teach users how to systematically evaluate any flowing water source before relying on it for drinking water, understanding that not all rivers are equal.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- The ability to use the eyes, nose, and systematic observation as tools in a post-collapse world without water testing labs.

## Material
Flowing water sources (rivers, lakes, springs) and the physical characteristics of the water, including flow speed, clarity, smell, and the visible ecosystem.

## System
The Five-Point River Assessment checklist, used to systematically evaluate a flowing water source.

## Technology
- Sensory evaluation (eyes, nose, touch).
- The Stonefly Test (using rock inspection as a bioindicator).

## Tools
- A checklist for the Five-Point Assessment.
- A rapid field assessment card.
- A measurement method (stick/leaf for flow speed).

## Process
### The Five-Point River Assessment
1. Walk Upstream:
    - Walk upstream for at least 500 meters (roughly a 5-10 minute walk) to look for contamination sources.
    - Red flags include dead animals, human settlements/camps, agricultural fields, roads/bridges, mining operations, or industrial buildings/dumps.
2. Assess Flow Speed and Volume:
    - Fast-flowing water indicates higher dissolved oxygen and continuous flushing (best choice).
    - Moderate flow is acceptable.
    - Slow flow indicates higher bacterial loads and potential stagnant backwaters.
    - Measurement method: Drop a stick/leaf to check speed (faster than a slow walk suggests good flow).
3. Check Water Clarity:
    - Hand test: If fingers are visible at wrist depth, clarity is good.
    - Clarity Levels:
        - Crystal clear (see bottom at 1m+): Low suspended sediment, still requires purification.
        - Slightly cloudy: Moderate sediment/organic particles, pre-filter recommended.
        - Opaque/murky: Heavy sediment, let settle 2-4 hours, pre-filter, then purify.
        - Colored (tea/brown): Tannins, usually not dangerous, filter through charcoal.
        - Colored (orange/red): Iron oxide or acid mine drainage; potentially dangerous, avoid.
        - Colored (green): Algal contamination, may contain cyanotoxins, avoid if scum is visible.
4. Smell the Water:
    - No smell: Normal.
    - Earthy/musty smell: Organic decomposition; filter through charcoal.
    - Rotten eggs (sulfur): Hydrogen sulfide from anaerobic decomposition; usable if aerated and purified, but find better sources.
    - Chemical or petroleum smell: Do NOT use (industrial/chemical contamination).
    - Sweet or fruity smell: Can indicate industrial solvents; Do NOT use.
    - Chlorine smell: May indicate pipe corrosion products, use with caution.
5. Observe the Ecosystem:
    - Positive signs (likely safe): Fish, aquatic insects (mayfly, caddisfly, stonefly larvae), frogs/tadpoles, freshwater mussels/snails, healthy green algae, water birds.
    - Warning signs (caution needed): No visible aquatic life, dead fish, thick algal mats, absence of insects, unusual persistent foam.

### Choosing Your Collection Point
- Best locations:
    - Where the river flows through a rocky section with rapids or small cascades.
    - Just downstream of a natural spring (look for a noticeably colder current).
    - Where the channel is narrow and deep (faster flow).
    - On the outside of a bend where the current is fastest and deepest.
- Worst locations:
    - Inside of bends (sediment deposits, slow water).
    - Directly downstream of any tributary joining the river (unknown contamination).
    - Near the shoreline, especially where animals access the water.
    - Below a bridge, road crossing, or settlement.
    - In wide, shallow sections where water warms and slows.

### Time-of-Day Considerations
- Early morning: Coolest water temperature, lowest bacterial activity (Best time).
- Midday: Temperature rising; potential for cyanobacteria activity in slow sections.
- After heavy rain: Turbidity spikes dramatically; wait 24-48 hours for stabilization.
- During drought: Flow drops, concentration of all contaminants increases.

### Quick Field Assessment Card
1. Walked upstream 500m? (If no, do it now.)
2. Any dead animals, settlements, or industrial sites upstream? (If yes, move on.)
3. Flow speed? (Faster is better; stagnant means treat as a pond.)
4. Can you see your hand at wrist depth? (If no, let settle before collecting.)
5. Smell? (If chemical or sulfur, find another source.)
6. Life in the water? (If no life at all, proceed with extreme caution.)
7. Rocks under the surface? (Turn one over; insects = good sign.)

**Key Takeaways**
- Always walk at least 500 meters upstream before collecting.
- Fast-flowing water over rocks is the best collection point.
- Heavy metals and industrial chemicals cannot be removed by field purification methods.
- The stonefly test is the best field bioindicator of water quality available without lab equipment.
- Assessment reduces risk but does not eliminate it; purification is always required.

Notes
- Boiling, filtering, and UV treatment remove biological pathogens but do NOT remove dissolved heavy metals (lead, mercury, arsenic, cadmium) or industrial chemicals.
- Heavy metals and industrial chemicals require finding a different source if mining or industrial contamination is observed upstream.
- The Stonefly Test is a key bioindicator, indicating high oxygen content and low pollution.
- Rotten egg smell (sulfur) is present in anaerobic decomposition and is usable only if aerated and purified.
