# Ceramic Filtration

## Objective
To physically remove bacteria and parasites from water by forcing water through microscopic pores left behind when combustible material burns away during firing.

## Dependencies and prerequisites
- Clay soil: Must be sticky when wet, hold its shape, and crack when dry. Sources include river banks, stream cuts, and areas where pottery clay has been historically dug.
- Combustible addite (burnout material): Fine sawdust, rice husks, ground dried leaves, coffee grounds, or finely crushed dried manure.
- A kiln or firing pit capable of reaching 850-1,000 degrees Celsius.
- A receiving vessel (clean bucket, pot, or container).
- Colloidal silver (optional but recommended for enhanced antibacterial effect).

## Material
- Clay soil
- Combustible addite (e.g., Sawdust, Rice husks, Coffee grounds, Dried grass/leaves, Dried manure).
- Colloidal silver (if available).

## System
- Ceramic pot filter: A pot shaped from the clay-burnout mixture, fired to create microscopic pores.
- Receiving vessel: Container placed below or around the ceramic pot to collect filtered water.
- Filtered water collection system: Allows clean water to seep through the walls and collect in the receiving vessel.

## Technology
Ceramic filters utilize the natural microporosity of fired clay. When clay is mixed with a combustible material and fired, the organic matter burns away, leaving behind a network of microscopic pores (typically 0.5 to 2 micrometers) that physically block contaminants.

## Tools
- Kiln or firing pit (for firing).
- Measuring tools (implied for ensuring uniform wall thickness).
- A pointed stick (to check wall thickness during shaping).
- Electric current source (to create colloidal silver solution).

## Process
### Step 1 — Prepare the Clay
Dig clay and remove rocks, roots, and debris. Soak it in water for 24 hours, then knead it thoroughly to achieve a uniform consistency, like thick bread dough. Test plasticity by rolling a pencil-thick coil; it should bend without cracking.

### Step 2 — Mix in the Burnout Material
Mix finely ground combustible material into the clay at a ratio of roughly **40-50% clay to 50-60% burnout material by volume**. Thorough mixing is critical to ensure consistent pore distribution.

### Step 3 — Shape the Filter Pot
Form the clay-burnout mixture into a pot shape (truncated cone or flowerpot). The dimensions are approximately:
- Diameter at the top: 25-30 cm (10-12 inches)
- Diameter at the bottom: 20-25 cm (8-10 inches)
- Height: 20-25 cm (8-10 inches)
- Wall thickness: 1.5-2 cm (roughly 3/4 inch), must be uniform.

### Step 4 — Dry Slowly
Allow the pot to dry in shade for 3-7 days, depending on humidity. Turn the pot daily. It is ready to fire when it feels bone-dry, is lighter in color, and sounds hollow when tapped.

### Step 5 — Fire the Pot
Fire the pot in a kiln or firing pit at **850-1,000 degrees Celsius** (1,560-1,830 degrees Fahrenheit). This temperature is critical:
- Below 800 C: Clay will dissolve in water.
- 850-1,000 C: Optimal range; clay vitrifies enough to hold water while burnout material combusts completely, leaving open pores.
- Above 1,100 C: Pores begin to close as clay over-vitrifies.

### Step 6 — Test the Filter
Fill the fired pot with water and observe flow:
- **Successful flow:** Water seeps through slowly, forming droplets, with a drip rate of 1-3 liters per hour.
- **Too large pores:** Water pours through rapidly (filters sediment but not bacteria).
- **Poor filtration:** No water comes through after an hour (pot was over-fired or burnout ratio was too low).

### Step 7 — Apply Colloidal Silver (If Available)
Brush or soak the inside and outside of the ceramic pot with a colloidal silver solution. Let it dry. Reapply every 2-3 months for sustained antibacterial effect.

### Step 8 — Assemble the Filter System
Place the ceramic pot inside or on top of a clean receiving vessel, ensuring the pot's open top faces up. Pour untreated water into the pot. Clean water seeps through the walls and collects in the receiving vessel below.

## Maintenance
- **Clean the inner surface weekly** by gently scrubbing with a soft brush or cloth under running water to remove biofilm and sediment.
- **Never use soap or detergent** as they will be adsorbed by the ceramic and leach into the filtered water.
- **Re-fire if flow drops dramatically** (though making a new pot is often easier).
- A well-maintained ceramic filter pot lasts **1-3 years** before needing replacement.

## Scaling for a Community
- One ceramic pot filter serves a household of 4-6 people (producing 10-20 liters per day).
- For larger groups, build multiple filter pots and distribute them.
- Train community members in the clay-burnout mixing ratio and firing temperature.
- Establish a pottery workshop for regular replacement filters.
- **Stacking:** Route water through a sand pre-filter first, then through the ceramic filter, to extend the ceramic pot’s lifespan.

## Key Takeaways
- Ceramic filters physically remove bacteria and parasites through microscopic pores in fired clay.
- The critical recipe is 40-50% clay to 50-60% fine burnout material by volume.
- Fire at 850-1,000 C—too low results in dissolution, too high results in pore sealing.
- Wall thickness must be uniform at 1.5-2 cm throughout the pot.
- Ceramic filters do NOT remove viruses or chemical contaminants; combine with boiling or UV treatment for complete safety.
- A single pot filter produces 1-3 liters per hour and serves 4-6 people.
- Clean weekly by scrubbing the inner surface; apply colloidal silver if available for enhanced antibacterial effect.

Notes
- Ceramic filters do not remove viruses or chemical contaminants, requiring combination with boiling or UV treatment for complete safety.
- The critical recipe ratio is 40-50% clay to 50-60% burnout material by volume.
- The required firing temperature range is 850-1,000 °C.
- The effective pore size for removing bacteria (0.2 to 5 micrometers) is compared against the particle sizes of contaminants (e.g., giardia 8-15 micrometers).
- Maintenance requires weekly scrubbing of the inner surface to prevent biofilm clogging.
- The lifespan of a well-maintained ceramic filter is estimated at 1-3 years.
