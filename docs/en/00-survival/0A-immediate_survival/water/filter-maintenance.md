# Filter Maintenance

## Objective
The objective of filter maintenance is to ensure the continuous safe production of water by preventing the failure of filters, which creates a false sense of security that leads directly to waterborne disease outbreaks.

## Dependencies and prerequisites

*   **Understanding Flow Rate:** The primary dependency is the ability to accurately measure flow rate, as cleaning decisions must be based on flow rate, not just appearance.
*   **Water Quality Awareness:** Understanding that different source water qualities dictate maintenance frequency.
*   **Redundancy:** Building redundancy by keeping at least one mature filter in operation while another is being cleaned or rebuilt.

## Material

*   **Sand:** Used in slow sand filters and rapid sand filters.
*   **Cloth:** Used in cloth strainers.
*   **Charcoal:** Used in improvised bottle filters and charcoal layers.
*   **Gravel:** Used in the underdrain system.
*   **Schmutzdecke:** The biological layer that develops on the filter surface and must be preserved.

## System

The system is a water filtration setup, with an emphasis on ensuring continuous operation through the use of multiple, redundant filters.

## Technology

*   **Biological Filtration:** The slow sand filter relies on the growth and survival of the *schmutzdecke* biofilm to trap sediment and biomass.
*   **Adsorption:** Charcoal filters utilize the adsorption capacity of charcoal to remove chemicals.
*   **Flow Rate Dynamics:** Maintenance triggers are based on flow rate, indicating when the biological layer or physical structure is failing.

## Tools

*   Flat shovel, board, or improvised scraper (for scraping).
*   Container for collecting removed sand.
*   Container for measuring flow rate (e.g., 10 liters).
*   Stick or paddle (for manual agitation during backwashing).

## Process

### Maintenance Schedules by Filter Type

| Filter Type | Primary Maintenance | Frequency | Secondary Maintenance | Frequency |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cloth strainer | Wash and dry cloth | After every use | Replace cloth | When torn or thinning |
| Improvised bottle filter | Replace charcoal | Every 3-5 days | Replace all media | Every 2-4 weeks |
| Rapid sand filter | Backwash | Every 1-3 days | Replace sand | Every 6-12 months |
| Slow sand filter | Scrape top layer | Every 1-6 months | Rebuild sand bed | Every 1-3 years |
| Charcoal filter | Replace charcoal | Every 1-2 weeks | Clean container | At each charcoal swap |
| Ceramic filter | Scrub exterior | Weekly | Replace element | When too thin to scrub |

### Slow Sand Filter Maintenance

**Recognizing When Cleaning Is Needed (Flow Rate Criteria)**

| Flow Rate | Status | Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0.1-0.3 m/hr | Normal operating range | No action |
| 0.05-0.1 m/hr | Declining but usable | Plan cleaning within 1-2 weeks |
| Below 0.05 m/hr | Critically slow | Clean now |
| Zero flow | Filter is blocked | Emergency cleaning required |

**The Scraping Procedure**

1.  **Lower the water level:** Drain or siphon supernatant water until it is just above the sand surface (no more than 2-3 cm above). The sand must remain saturated.
2.  **Scrape the top layer:** Carefully remove the top 1-2 cm of sand along with the *schmutzdecke* using a flat tool, working in sections.
3.  **Level the surface:** Smooth the sand surface with the flat edge of a board to eliminate preferential flow paths.
4.  **Refill slowly:** Gently refill the supernatant water above the sand, pouring against a baffle or over a plate.
5.  **Resume operation:** Allow the surface biological layer to re-establish for 1-7 days before resuming normal flow.

**Sand Replacement Requirements**

Each scraping removes 1-2 cm of sand. When the bed reaches minimum safe depth, replenishment is required.

| Original Bed Depth | Minimum Safe Depth | Number of Scrapings Before Rebuild |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 60 cm | 45 cm | 7-15 |
| 80 cm | 55 cm | 12-25 |
| 100 cm | 65 cm | 17-35 |
| 120 cm | 80 cm | 20-40 |

**Full Rebuild Procedure**

1.  Drain the filter completely.
2.  Remove all sand; inspect the gravel underdrain for clogging or displacement and clean or replace as needed.
3.  Wash all sand thoroughly or replace with fresh pre-washed sand.
4.  Rebuild following the original construction procedure (gravel, transition layer, sand in flooded lifts).
5.  Fill with water and allow a 2-4 week maturation period before using the output.

### Rapid Sand Filter Maintenance

**Backwashing Procedure**

1.  Stop the inflow and close the outlet.
2.  If applicable, open the upflow backwash inlet and force clean water through the bed, or manually agitate the top 20-30 cm of sand.
3.  Open a waste outlet and flush the dirty water out until it runs relatively clear (typically 3-5 minutes).
4.  Close the waste outlet, resume inflow, and allow the sand to resettle for 5-10 minutes before reopening the clean water outlet.
5.  Discard the first 10-20 liters of output after backwashing.

**Sand Replacement**

Rapid filter sand degrades; replace every 6-12 months, or when backwashing no longer restores adequate flow rates.

### Improvised Filter Maintenance

**Charcoal Replacement**

*   **Signs of Exhaustion:** Output water taste deteriorates (chemical or earthy flavor returns), output water color darkens or yellows, or flow rate remains high even when the charcoal looks clean (pores are sealed with adsorbed material).
*   **Replacement Procedure:** Remove old charcoal, crush new hardwood charcoal to pea-sized pieces (5-10 mm), rinse with clean water (3-4 rinses), pack into the filter, and flush 2-3 liters through.

**Sand Layer Maintenance (Improvised Filters)**

*   **Frequency:** Every 1-2 weeks.
*   **Procedure:** Remove sand, stir vigorously in water to release trapped sediment, pour off dirty water (repeat 3-5 times), repack sand, and flush with 1-2 liters.

### Troubleshooting Guide

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Fix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Flow drops to zero suddenly | Ice formation, or large debris blocking inlet/outlet | Check for blockages, thaw if frozen |
| Flow drops gradually | Normal *schmutzdecke* growth (slow filter) or sediment accumulation (all types) | Scrape (slow) or backwash (rapid) |
| Output suddenly cloudy | Sand bed cracked from drying, or channeling after disturbance | Drain, re-level sand, refill. May need full restart. |
| Sulfur/rotten egg smell | Anaerobic conditions in the filter bed | Scrape aggressively (top 3-5 cm). Improve aeration of incoming water. Reduce organic load with pre-settling. |
| Green water output | Algae growing inside the filter body | Cover the filter to block light. Clean container walls. |
| Taste like dirt or mud | Anaerobic breakdown products, or exhausted charcoal | Replace charcoal. Check for anaerobic conditions. |
| Sand appears in output | Gravel underdrain failed or displaced | Rebuild underdrain. Check for gravel bridging or sand migration. |
| Worms visible in output | Filter is hosting macro-invertebrates | Increase flow rate slightly. Ensure gravel layer is intact. |

## Record Keeping

Establish a system to track performance by recording:

*   Date of last cleaning.
*   Flow rate at each check (to establish the decline curve).
*   Sand depth (to estimate rebuild necessity).
*   Any unusual events (filter ran dry, flood water entered, output smelled off).

## Key Takeaways

*   Maintenance is crucial because a neglected filter is more dangerous than no filter due to the false confidence it generates.
*   For slow sand filters, maintenance must be data-driven: measure flow rate and scrape only when flow rate demands it.
*   The sand must remain wet during scraping to preserve the biofilm (*schmutzdecke*).
*   Removed sand must be saved, washed, and dried for later replenishment.
*   Always build redundancy to ensure continuous safe water production.
*   Proactive tracking of performance data is the difference between reactive repair and proactive maintenance.

## Notes

- The source explicitly states that the decision to clean a filter should *never* be based on a fixed schedule alone; flow rate measurement is the definitive trigger.
- The full rebuild process destroys all biological development, necessitating a long maturation period before water is safe to drink.
- The process of scraping requires careful handling to prevent the creation of channels and craters on the sand surface.
